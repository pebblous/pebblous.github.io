## LinkedIn (EN)

Five of the seven shareholders in the data supply company China stood up this month are telecom carriers and power grid operators.

The firm is National Data Group Co., Ltd., which opened on 20 October in Xiong'an New Area, Hebei Province. It is a central state-owned enterprise supervised by the State Council's asset commission, and it builds neither models nor chips. Its published remit states that it will ship high-quality datasets and large-scale corpora to the AI industry. Standing alongside the State Council and the Xiong'an administration on the shareholder list are China Telecom, China Unicom, China Mobile and two grid companies. The claim that training data is now being handled as infrastructure, on a par with electricity and connectivity, is usually an interpretation. At this company it is the share register.

The same remit carries one more line: industry data governance services. The company that supplies the data also sells the work of ordering and managing it. Supply and appraisal sit inside one legal entity.

A week earlier, on 13 October, Korea's Cabinet approved amendments requiring AI training data produced under government-funded projects to be routed into AI Hub. They take effect in April 2027. China built a company; Korea built an obligation. The vessels differ and the blank cell is the same. The duty to take quality-management measures falls on the side that receives and gathers the data, which leaves neither design with a verifier separate from the supply.

Korea has already paid once for what sits in that blank. Reopening all 691 datasets accumulated in AI Hub through 2022 and picking 30 for rework toward generative AI cost KRW 3 billion, about $2.1 million. Thirty is the number selected, not a failing grade on the rest.

We download a training dataset and rarely go on to check where the material came from.

If the counter handing it over looks credible, that is usually where the checking stops.

On 20 October, in Xiong'an New Area in Hebei Province, China opened a company that runs such a counter on the state's own behalf.

National Data Group Co., Ltd. A central state-owned enterprise supervised by the State Council's asset commission.

It builds no models and no chips. What it sells is data.

What kind of company it is needed no explaining. The shareholder list had already said it.

Five of the seven shareholders are telecom carriers and power grid operators.

Next to the firms that deliver electricity and the firms that deliver connectivity, a firm that delivers data has taken its place in the same row.

Read the published remit all the way down, though, and one more line is attached.

Industry data governance services.

The company supplying the data also sells the work of ordering and managing it. Supply and appraisal, sitting inside one legal entity.

A week before that, Korea reached for a different vessel.

On 13 October its Cabinet approved amendments requiring AI training data built through government-funded projects to be handed to AI Hub. They take effect in April 2027.

China built a company. Korea built an obligation.

The vessels differ, and the cell they left blank is the same one. The duty to take quality-management measures rests with the side that receives and gathers the data.

Which is why one question kept coming back while the piece was being written.

"If the data the state gathered turns out to be defective, who do you ask, and what do you ask them for?"

Neither country's documents write down an answer.

Korea has already paid once for what sits in that blank.

Reopening all 691 datasets accumulated in AI Hub through 2022 and selecting 30 for rework toward generative AI cost KRW 3 billion, around $2.1 million. Thirty is the number chosen, not a verdict that the rest are unusable.

Accumulating and selecting are different processes. They run on different budgets, different procedures and a different set of reviewers.
