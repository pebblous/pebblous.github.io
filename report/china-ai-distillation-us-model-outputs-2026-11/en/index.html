Executive Summary

Chinese researchers and companies ask a leading US model for answers and feed those answers to a smaller domestic model as training material. That work has left the experiment stage and become a production process. Reuters reported it exclusively on 17 November, after reviewing more than 300 Chinese academic papers, patents, corporate disclosures, recruitment notices and government documents. Software automates the collection of the answers, and there are staff assigned to nothing else.

The documents Reuters counted were never hidden, though. They were published as papers, filed as patents, posted as job ads. And the paper the report held up as its leading example happens to be the one that criticizes distillation at the greatest length, and the one that built a scorecard for whether a project records where its data came from. Scored on that card, five of seven attempts in the same field came out at zero on the data-source item.

What these teams are doing fills public documents to overflowing. Which model the answers came out of, and which model they went into, is the part that does not stay with the model. So the question a reader is left with is not who took something in secret. It is what the model we picked up was trained on, and where the fact that our own synthetic data came from another model's output gets written down.

The 300 Documents Reuters Reviewed Are All Public

The exclusive Reuters filed from Shenzhen on 17 November rests on a review of more than 300 documents out of China. The article states its own scope: "A Reuters review of more than 300 Chinese academic papers, patents, corporate disclosures, recruitment notices and government documents reveals how the practice — known as distillation — has expanded beyond academic experiments into mainstream industrial use in China." Five kinds of document, and every one of them sat where anyone could pick it up.

Distillation means asking a large model for answers and using those answers as training material for a smaller one. The technique is not new, and why it leaves the distiller leaning on whoever holds the original data was the subject of an earlier report, You Can't Buy Sovereignty by Distilling It. What this story adds is not the technique but the way the work is now run. The records Reuters read describe Chinese researchers getting answers out of US models, generating code and finding solutions to hard problems, while Chinese companies build software that automates that loop and assign dedicated staff to refine the training material for their own smaller models. That is what "industrial scale" means here. Work one person used to do by hand, query by query, now has someone who owns the process.

Where this meets export controls is plain enough. Withhold advanced chips and compute goes down; copy down the answers another company's model produces and training data goes up. Reuters wrote that widespread distillation lets Chinese developers build capability on US output rather than US silicon, which blunts the effect of limiting chip sales. There are two channels, and the controls sit on one of them.

Compute and output travel different channels. The top one is what chip export controls and the 16 November interim rule on remote access aim at; the bottom one is where those controls have not yet reached. The diagram marks where the controls sit, not the relative volume each channel carries.

How the chip channel gets crossed is something we looked at five days ago, in A Blacklisted Chinese University Buys Nvidia H200 Chips, and Rents More, which collected the records of a sanctioned Chinese university both buying Nvidia H200s and renting server time. Chips get through by purchase or rental, and answers get through by being copied down.

How Washington reads this channel is in the same article. A US official familiar with the Chengdu talks told Reuters that Washington sees industrial-scale distillation as the main reason Chinese models have narrowed the gap. That assessment rests on one unnamed official, and the same article carries the rebuttal. Two people familiar with Chinese developers pushed back, calling distillation one factor among several, alongside engineering, algorithms, data and model design. None of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Alibaba, Meituan, the US Commerce Department or the Cyberspace Administration of China responded to requests for comment.

Three Rungs From a University Lab to a Company Process

Reuters shows the move from experiment to process through three cases. It starts in a university lab in 2024, goes on to training a company model in 2025, and by 2026 reaches the training of a model that coordinates several AI agents. What the three rungs show together has less to do with technique than with where the record lives. All three are written up in papers, and those papers are public.

The three rungs do not rest on equally solid ground, though. The first names a paper we could open in full; for the other two, Reuters wrote only "an April 2025 paper" and "a February 2026 paper", and we could not identify which papers those are. The last column of the table below records that difference as it stands.

This is not the only channel through which China sources training data. In October a state-owned company was founded to have the state itself supply data, which we covered in China's National Data Group Supplies the Data That AI Learns From. One channel produces the data at home and delivers it; the other copies it down from someone else's model. Whether the two sit under the same policy package remains unverified.

The Numbers Reuters Quoted Come From the Paper's Side Experiment

The figures the report gives for the first rung are 5,000 o1 responses, 10,750 bilingual pairs and Qwen2.5-72B. Open the paper and those numbers turn out to sit somewhere other than where the report seems to put them. All three come from §4 of the paper, "Application Beyond Math Reasoning", an experiment appended at the end to see whether a model distilled on mathematics carries over outside mathematics. The paper's headline experiment used a different base model and a different volume of material.

Headline experiment (§2.3, §3) What the report quoted (§4)

Training material Long chains of thought distilled from o1, "tens of thousands of samples" About 5,000 picked from o1 output → translated into Chinese with GPT-4o mini → 10,750 pairs
What it tested Mathematical reasoning on AIME and MATH500 Whether it carries over outside mathematics
Result AIME2024 13/30 (o1-preview 12/30, o1-mini 21/30), MATH500 87.2 (85.5 / 90.0) Of nine metrics, four up, one flat and four down

Source: arXiv:2411.16489 v1 (submitted 25 November 2024, accessed 18 November 2026), §2.3, §3, §4 and Tables 1 and 2. The paper has no v2.

The paper describes the §4 data like this: "From our distilled O1 model outputs, we carefully select approximately 5,000 high-quality samples containing retrospective thinking and self-reflection elements. These samples are then translated into Chinese using GPT-4o mini model, resulting in a balanced bilingual dataset. The final training dataset comprises 10,750 mixed Chinese-English sample pairs." The 5,000 is a count picked a second time out of output that had already been distilled, not a count of the questions put to o1.

The claim on the headline side is worth measuring as well. The paper writes that a base model fine-tuned on tens of thousands of o1-distilled chains of thought surpassed o1-preview on AIME. The value in Table 1 is 13 problems against 12, out of 30 on AIME2024. One problem. In the same table o1-mini solved 21, and MATH500 reads 87.2 against 85.5 (o1-preview) and 90.0 (o1-mini). The paper says as much itself: "there remains a noticeable gap compared to O1-mini in terms of mathematical reasoning performance." The model it caught and the model it trails sit in the same table.

And distillation is not free. Table 2 in §4 compares nine metrics before and after fine-tuning, and since the caption reads "Performance comparison (accuracy) before and after SFT", higher is better in every row. For the metric that measures how well a model resists flattery, the paper notes separately that a higher percentage means holding up better. Here are all nine rows, with none left out.

Four metrics went down, and the largest drop is in Chinese factuality. They translated into Chinese and trained on it in order to build a Chinese-language model, and the test that asks about Chinese facts lost 6.43 points. The paper's own wording is weaker than that, though. The only drop it calls pronounced is WildSafety, and of the three factuality metrics it says they "did not demonstrate significant improvement". The arithmetic in the table and the wording in the prose differ, so both are recorded here.

The Same Paper Criticizes Distillation for a Whole Section

The last section of that same paper is titled "The Bitter Lesson of Simple Distillation". Reuters compressed it into a single sentence: "excessive reliance on distillation could stifle original innovation, leaving developers dependent on breakthroughs made by more advanced model providers." In the original, that warning is not one sentence. It is a section with six subheadings under it.

Subheading What it says
SURFACE APPEALDistillation looks like an elegant solution and performance climbs quickly. But "this convenience comes at a price that may not be immediately apparent"
PERFORMANCE CEILING"Models trained through distillation are invariably bounded by the capabilities of their teacher model… This creates an implicit ceiling effect"
MISSED INNOVATIONIt skips what actually made o1 work, inference-time scaling and search optimization. "we risk creating a widening technological gap"
RESEARCH CULTURE SHIFT"The availability of 'easy wins' through distillation has begun to shift research focus away from tackling fundamental challenges." With no infrastructure, teams lean on distillation; leaning on it, they build less infrastructure, and the loop closes
EROSION OF FUNDAMENTALS"This shift from 'how it works' to 'what works' represents a fundamental change in research mentality"
GROWING DIVIDE"genuine breakthroughs become the exclusive domain of a small number of well-resourced organizations, while the broader community remains trapped in a cycle of incremental improvements through distillation."

Source: arXiv:2411.16489 v1, all six subheadings of §6. Quoted text is verbatim from the paper; the summary around it is ours.

Of the six, the one that matters most to anyone working with data is the ceiling effect. A model trained by distillation is bounded by the capabilities of its teacher model, and that bound acts as a ceiling that nothing on the surface shows. Benchmark scores keep rising all the way up toward the teacher, so the score is not where the signal of having hit the ceiling shows up first.

At the close of the section the authors step back.

"The bitter lesson here is not that distillation is inherently problematic - it remains a valuable tool in our technical arsenal. Rather, the danger lies in allowing the convenience of distillation to divert us from the harder but ultimately more rewarding path of fundamental innovation."

The warning is aimed at the convenience, not at the method, and the same sentence calls distillation a valuable tool.

Reading this section as a Chinese researcher's confession of conscience points the story the wrong way. The ten authors are not only from Shanghai Jiao Tong University, SII and GAIR; New York University is in there too. And what they did was not confess. They built a scorecard and graded the work of their own field with it. That scorecard is what Section 6 below is built on.

Terms of Use Restrict It, a Policy Document Encourages It, and No Law Covers It

No country has a law banning distillation. Reuters wrote that "Distillation is not illegal and is prevalent in other parts of the world." What restricts it is terms of service. The same article puts it this way: "OpenAI's terms restrict using its output to develop competing models, subject to exceptions, while Anthropic similarly restricts training competing systems without permission." The OpenAI restriction carries the qualifier that exceptions apply, and Anthropic's turns on permission rather than on an outright prohibition. Render either as a blanket ban and you have made it stronger than the original.

What moved in place of a law was a warning about enforcement. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent wrote this on X on 22 July.

"We support open-source AI and the innovation it unlocks. But open source is not open season on American IP. When PRC firms conduct covert, industrial-scale distillation attacks that cross the line into IP theft, sanctions and Entity List designations will be on the table."

There are two conditions for sanctions and Entity List designations to be on the table. The conduct has to be covert, and it has to cross the line into IP theft. The sentence reads as requiring both. But the 300 documents Reuters counted this time are papers, patents and job ads. Enforcement is pointed at what was hidden, and what this report counted is what was written down.

Line up the four US records and the mismatch gets sharper. The Chengdu meeting in the first row came to light through this report.

The September advisory is titled "China-Based Artificial Intelligence Companies Conducting Industrial-Scale Distillation Campaigns Against U.S. AI Companies", and it names six firms: DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun and Z.AI. The three agencies wrote that distillation is "not a supplement to these companies' AI model development, but the critical core of it", and that millions of exchanges and requests since late 2024 have pulled out billions of tokens. The route they pointed to was "a large gray market of API proxies, or 'transfer stations'". We covered that covert route separately in September, in Chinese Labs Rerouted Their Own Users' Requests to Claude.

What the 16 November rule closes off is not the chips themselves but remote access. It puts a license requirement on the path by which a controlled entity uses US-made accelerators remotely through data centers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with the threshold set at 12 million TPP in aggregate. We sketched the shape of that opening back in October, in When Tencent Rents AI Chips From Oracle, Where Does the Data Go?. The rule takes effect on publication in the Federal Register, though, and publication is set for 19 November, so as of this writing it has not taken effect. The accurate statement is not that the path is closed but that a rule to close it has been issued.

There is a document on the other side as well. Reuters wrote that the "AI+ manufacturing" policy package (《"人工智能+制造"专项行动实施意见》) issued in January by eight Chinese government departments contains guidance on model training and fine-tuning for industrial applications, and guidance that supports distillation. That passage could not be verified against the Chinese original, so it is carried here only as Reuters described it. A technique restricted by the terms of service written in one country is encouraged by a government document in another.

Chinese developers find the boundary unclear as well. Reuters wrote that "Chinese AI developers have in recent months asked Beijing's industry regulator for guidance on the boundaries between ordinary use of foreign models and organised collection of output for training". What they put to the regulator is where ordinary use of a foreign model ends and systematic collection of its output for training begins, and the source for it is one person familiar with the discussions. The report came out in the same week as the APEC summit in Shenzhen, and a bilateral meeting between President Trump and President Xi Jinping is scheduled for 19 November. No outcome of that meeting was available at the time of writing.

What They Do Is Written Down, What They Built It From Is Blank

Every record so far has been the same kind. We do distillation, and this is the method we use. Call that a record of the activity. It sits in papers, in patents and in job ads, which is how Reuters could count 300 of them. But the record anyone working with data needs is a different kind. This model's output went into that dataset and came out as this model. Call that a record of lineage. It belongs in the model card and the dataset metadata, and that is where it is blank.

The left column rests on the Reuters report, the right column on Table 4 of arXiv:2411.16489 v1. The two figures count different things, so subtracting one from the other or binding them into a ratio would blur what each one means.

What turned the right-hand column into a number was that same paper. In §5 the authors built a scorecard they call the Technical Transparency Index and graded o1 replication attempts out of 100: 14 points for data, 33 for methodology, 24 for evaluation and 29 for open sourcing. Here is what the first item, Data Transparency, asks.

"This aspect evaluates whether the origin of the data is clearly specified, including detailed descriptions of the datasets used and their respective sources. It considers whether the dataset names, providers, or publications from which the data is derived are explicitly mentioned. This applies to all datasets used in downstream tasks such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning (RL), or search algorithms and becomes even more crucial when the datasets serve as seed data for synthesizing long thought data."

The two sub-items under it are Data Source and Data Selection Process. And the last sentence points straight at what this article is about: the item matters most precisely when the data is the seed for synthesizing long chains of thought. That caveat was written in 2024, and the 300 documents Reuters counted in 2026 are exactly that case.

There is one more layer to this. In the prose the authors describe their own work as "achieving a perfect transparency score", while the total written for them in the same table is 79. Ten on data and 12 on open sourcing, so neither item is at the maximum. The side that built the scorecard did not fill in its own scorecard either. On top of that, one independent review noted that the index is self-scored and that the reported scores cannot be verified from the manuscript alone. The party that designed the index and its weights is the party that assigned the scores. The "five of seven" this article cites carries that same limit.

Is the reason nothing gets written down simple laziness, then? Not really. It is closer to a mismatch between where the writing happens and where the model travels. The derived model this paper produced was never released at all, as a footnote to the abstract states: "Per OpenAI's Terms of Use, our distillation of the OpenAI O1 series models is strictly for research purposes and will not be fully disclosed publicly." The writing happened in the paper, and the model never came out. This is not a case of a model card leaving out the teacher model's name. It is a case of there being no model.

The blank is one layer below that. The model card for Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, the model this experiment rode on top of, has a base-model field but no datasets field, and no passage describing where the training data came from. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, on the same hub, does the opposite. Its metadata gives Qwen2.5-32B as the base model, and its body states that it was fine-tuned on 800,000 reasoning samples generated by DeepSeek-R1. The teacher model's name and the sample count are both on one card. We opened both on 18 November 2026.

The place to write it already exists. Writing it is simply optional. One hub carrying the card that fills it in alongside the card that leaves it empty is the proof that the place is there.

The blank is not confined to this one card. The proposal to record training data on a model card dates to 2019. "Model Cards for Model Reporting" put training data among nine standard items, and the hub's base-model and dataset fields carried that proposal into practice. Both are recommendations, though, and the entry is made by the owner. A study that audited the whole hub reported, accordingly, that the entries are uneven. Even quantized models, which by definition rest on a base model, often left that field unfilled, and 1,416 cases put a model name where a dataset belongs.

What about the obligation side? The strongest demand for training-data disclosure is the public summary of training content under Article 53(1)(d) of the EU AI Act. But that template asks for data sources by category. It asks whether the data is a public dataset, a crawl, user data or synthetic data, and it does not ask whose model output that synthetic data came from. When we compared the summaries actually filed, in Big Tech Left Half of the EU's Training-Data Summaries Blank, the contested points were user data and crawling practice, not distillation. And the Chinese companies in this report are not subject to that obligation in the first place.

7

Why This Matters to Pebblous

Pebblous builds data that AI can use. Where this report touches that work is not which country did what, but the blank in the record. A structure in which one model's output becomes the next model's training data is already the global default, and it is not a Chinese peculiarity. What changed is that the work has turned into a process.

7.1A Process Needs a Record Format

Work one person did by hand, query by query, needs no format. It changes once software runs the collection and dedicated staff are attached to it. Which model, what queries, how many items, filtered by what rule, translated into what. The scope of what Pebblous calls AI-Ready Data widens to take that in. And those questions come from the scorecard in Section 6. Its first item asks two things, the source and the selection process, and the questions above are those two written out as units of work.

7.2Data Provenance Is a Debugging Tool, Not a Compliance Document

The paper itself left the evidence, and Section 3 already showed it. In Table 2 the steepest fall of the nine rows lands on Chinese factuality, and a Chinese-language model is what that bilingual training set was built for. If nobody wrote down which model's output went into the mix and how much of it, then once the score drops there is no telling what to take out to bring it back up. That is why recording provenance is quality work rather than audit preparation. It is the same reason DataClinic asks how a dataset was generated before it asks anything else.

7.3If You Deploy an Open Model, You Are Not a Bystander

When you take an open-weight model and put it in a product, what that model learned from is usually not written on its model card. The card for Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, the one Section 6 opened, is one of those. If there was terms-of-service risk upstream, the risk travels downstream, and if there was a quality defect upstream, the defect travels too. We looked at one model shipped with its training-data sources left blank in Perceptron's Open-Weight Factory Robot Model Leaves Its Data Sources Blank. Three lines are worth adding to a procurement checklist. Does this model's training data include another vendor's model output; if so, which model; and in which document is that stated. And for the synthetic data we make ourselves, we are the ones who have to write those same three lines.

7.4A Record Cannot Be Made After the Fact

Pebblous does not belong on either side of a for-or-against argument about distillation. It belongs on the side that makes the generation path travel with the data, whether that path is synthesis or distillation. The place for it already exists. The hub has a base-model field and a dataset field, and there are teams that do write the teacher model's name and the sample count onto the card. The reason it sits blank is not that it is impossible but that it is not required. The scorecard was built in 2024, and the 300 documents Reuters counted two years later show why it was needed. Provenance cannot be restored later by forensic examination. It survives only if it is attached at the moment the data is made.
