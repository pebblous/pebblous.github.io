## LinkedIn (KO)

닷새 사이에 같은 정리를 두고 글 여섯 편이 나왔고, 「이것을 AI가 찾았다」는 문장은 그 안에서 일곱 군데 다른 자리에 적혔다.

10월 26일 오픈AI가 지수 시간 가설은 거짓이라는 논문을 자사 저장소에 올렸고, 약 여섯 시간 뒤 앤트로픽이 같은 결론의 논문을 올렸다. 1999년에 제기돼 이후 수많은 하한 증명이 전제로 깔고 쓴 계산 복잡도 추측이다. 두 논문 모두 저자란에 사람 이름이 없다. 한쪽은 회사 이름을 적었고, 다른 쪽은 모델 이름을 적었다.

오픈AI 논문 67쪽 전문에는 Lean 도 Claude 도 발견 날짜도 한 번 나오지 않는다. 발견 경위는 전부 논문 바깥의 발표문에 있다. 앤트로픽은 반대로 1절 끝에 출처라는 이름표를 붙인 문단을 뒀다. 핵심 알고리즘은 10월 16일 내부 연구 모델이 출력 토큰 1,400만 개로 사람 입력 없이 찾았고, 논문의 제시 방식은 연구자들이 그 모델과 함께 다듬었다. 두 문장으로 갈라 적은 것이고, 하나로 줄이면 거짓이 된다.

사람이 자기 이름으로 쓴 글 넷도 같은 고지를 달았다. 다른 것은 고지의 유무가 아니라 놓은 자리였다. 본문 맨 끝 번호 없는 문단, 1쪽 제목에 달린 별표 각주, 깃허브 이슈 본문, 댓글 맨 끝 한 줄. 논문 양식에는 저자란이 있고 감사의 말 자리가 있고 참고문헌 자리가 있는데, 「이 결과를 모델이 찾았다」를 적는 칸은 없다. arXiv 와 출판윤리위원회는 공통으로 생성 AI 를 저자로 올릴 수 없다고 적지만, 이 두 논문은 arXiv 에 올라가지 않았다. 규정이 깨진 것이 아니라 규정이 서 있지 않은 자리에서 일이 일어났다.

닷새치 기록에서 가장 믿을 만한 문서는 가장 많이 주장한 쪽이 아니라 자기가 확인하지 않은 것을 적은 쪽이었다. 앤트로픽 논문의 부록 한 절은 증명이 기계를 통과한 경로를 네 겹까지 적어 두고, 같은 절에서 두 정의가 뜻하고자 한 바를 말하는지는 어떤 기계도 확인할 수 없다고 적는다. 페블러스는 DataClinic 으로 AI 학습 데이터의 품질을 정량 진단한다. 그 진단서에도 점수 옆에 같은 칸이 필요하다. 무엇을 어떤 기준으로 쟀고, 그 기준이 어디까지 닿지 않았는지.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Six write-ups appeared over five days on the same theorem, and the sentence saying an AI found it sits in seven different places across them.

On October 26 OpenAI posted a paper to its own repository claiming the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false, and about six hours later Anthropic posted one reaching the same conclusion. The hypothesis dates to 1999 and a great many lower bounds in complexity theory are proved on top of it. Neither paper carries a human name in the author field. One put a company there, the other put a model.

Across 67 pages, OpenAI's paper never once says Lean, never says Claude, never gives the date of the discovery. That account lives entirely outside the paper, in the company's announcement. Anthropic went the other way, closing its first section with a paragraph labelled as provenance: the core algorithm was found on October 16 by an internal research model, with 14 million output tokens and no human input, and Anthropic researchers then worked with the model on how the paper presents it. Compress those two sentences into one and the claim becomes false.

Four more write-ups followed, each signed by a person, and every one of them carried the same disclosure. What differed was where it went: an unnumbered paragraph at the very end of the body, a starred footnote on page 1, the body of a GitHub issue, a single line at the bottom of a comment. A paper has a field for authors, a place for acknowledgements, a place for references, and no field at all for "a model found this result." arXiv and COPE both state that generative AI cannot be listed as an author, and neither of these papers went to arXiv. No rule was broken. The work happened where the rules do not yet stand.

Across those five days the documents that read as most trustworthy were not the ones that claimed the most. They were the ones that wrote down what they had not checked. Anthropic's appendix lists four layers of machine checking the proof passed, and in the same section states that whether the two definitions say what they are meant to say is something no machine can check. Pebblous quantifies the quality of AI training data with DataClinic. A diagnostic report needs that same column beside the score: what was measured, against what standard, and where that standard stopped reaching.

## Facebook (EN)

Open the properties pane on a PDF you have just downloaded and there is a field called Author.

Usually a person's name goes there.

On a 49-page mathematics paper released a few days ago, that field reads Claude.

Across October 26 and 27, less than a day apart, OpenAI and Anthropic each released a paper reaching the same conclusion: that a complexity conjecture posed in 1999 is false. Neither paper carries a human name in its author field.

The author field was not the part that stopped me.

Over the five days that followed, four more write-ups appeared on the same theorem, each signed by a person. An exposition by a university professor. A simplified proof by a researcher. A bridge, posted to GitHub by one individual, joining the two proofs. A certificate produced by a third party.

All four carried a sentence saying a model had been used.

What differed was where each one put it.

An unnumbered paragraph at the very end of the body. A starred footnote on page 1. The body of a GitHub issue. A single line at the bottom of a comment.

A paper has a field for authors. It has a place for acknowledgements. It has a place for references.

It has no field for "a model found this result."

So seven people chose seven places.

What was missing was not diligence. It was somewhere to write it.

After reading all five days of it, the document I trusted most was not the one that claimed the most.

One appendix section of Anthropic's paper sets out, in four layers, the route the proof took through the machines. Which kernel accepted it, which independently implemented kernel walked it again, and on which pinned versions.

Then, in the same section, it says this.

"Whether the two definitions say what they are meant to say … no machine can check; that is for the reader of the listing to judge."

The document that wrote down what it could not do was the one that carried furthest.

Pebblous quantifies the quality of AI training data with DataClinic. The harder part is never the score. It is the column beside it: what was measured, against what standard, and where that standard stopped reaching.

Leave that column empty and the report is saying everything was checked.

"In our own reports, which line is the sentence about an AI finding it written on?"

And beside it, I would look for the line listing what we did not check.
