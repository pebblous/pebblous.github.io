Korea Opened 100 Public Datasets a Year Early. The Licence Change Was a Different List.

On 27 August, Korea's second Data Ministers' Meeting decided to finish opening 100 high-value public datasets by 2027 instead of 2028. Thirty-five datasets scheduled for 2028 were pulled into 2027. That is the sentence the coverage carried, and it is accurate.

There was a second item on the table that day, from different ministries and covering different material. The science ministry and the culture ministry had reviewed 102 public datasets and works, and 37 of them will be relabelled with the KOGL AI type or an equivalent, the licence marking that permits use as AI training data.

The two lists do not reference each other. One changed a date. The other changed the terms of use. For anyone assembling a training corpus, only the second one changes what is possible.

The column nobody quoted

Pages 5 and 6 of the press release carry an annex titled "Major Datasets Scheduled for Release." It has 46 rows, numbered without gaps. The last column is for remarks, and it is mostly blank. Twelve rows are not.

Sorted by what they actually say, the notes fall into three groups:

  • Copyright constraint (3 rows) — "works whose copyright has expired will be released first." All three belong to the culture ministry: the National Library catalogue, the performing arts collection, and the Korean film multimodal dataset.
  • User constraint (1 row) — Korean body measurement data carries a note about "reviewing measures to prevent unauthorised foreign use." The national data portal does not ask downloaders for a nationality, so how that review becomes a mechanism is not yet on paper.
  • Substitution or deferral (8 rows) — a similar dataset will be released instead, the data will be standardised before wider release, the holding agency is under review, a pilot release comes first.

What these phrases share is that the row may deliver something other than what its title names. Of the four news reports read in full, none quoted this column. The two numbers that travelled were 100 and 2027.

What the AI licence permits, and what it asks in return

Korea added the AI type to its public licence framework in January 2026. On its face it is permissive: no attribution requirement, commercial use allowed, derivative works allowed. The conditions arrive in the next paragraph. Free use applies only where the work is used as AI training data, and three obligations attach:

  • Technical measures to prevent outputs that are identical or substantially similar to the source work.
  • Technical measures for attribution where an output quotes the source work directly.
  • Commercial use of a model trained on the work is permitted, but resale of training datasets built from it is prohibited.

Two of the three land on whoever serves the model, not on whoever downloads the file. They are judged on what the output resembles, not on how the source was handled during training.

There is a quieter structural point. The licence guidance says the AI type is chosen alongside one of the existing types 1 through 4, and that the AI type and Type 0, the fully free mark, cannot be attached together. Read as written, that ranks them. For AI training specifically, the mark that leaves a user with no obligations at all is Type 0. An item carrying the AI type is, by the same rule, an item that is not Type 0.

Where 37 came from, and the layer underneath it

A footnote on page 3 shows the funnel. Of 102 items reviewed, 64 can be supplied to the national foundation model teams, and 37 of those can convert to the AI type. That leaves 27 items available to designated teams but not relabelled for general use. They are neither open nor closed. Counted on an open/closed axis, that layer disappears entirely.

The body of the release describes the 37 as clear of copyright and personal data issues. The header of the annex table says something else: subject to change in detailed consultation, and some items require de-identification. Neither statement is false. They are different statements, and a procurement team has to read both.

The part that has been measured

Managing terms of use through per-item labels rests on those labels being right. That assumption has been tested. The Data Provenance Initiative re-annotated 1,858 text datasets with legal experts and found licence fields on hosting sites frequently blank, and, when present, frequently wrong. The bias in the error has a direction: labels tend to be more permissive than the author intended.

That direction is the expensive one. Data mislabelled as more restrictive than it is goes unused. Data mislabelled as more open enters the pipeline and becomes a problem after the fact.

Other jurisdictions put the licence somewhere other than the label. The EU's high-value dataset regulation caps licensing at CC0 or CC BY 4.0 and requires terms of use in both human-readable and machine-readable form. US federal works default to the public domain by statute. Japan wrote a general exception into copyright law for uses that are not aimed at enjoyment of the work. Korea chose per-item marking, which is the most finely adjustable option and the one most dependent on each mark being correct.

Why we read this twice

Pebblous works on a distinction this release makes unusually visible: data being available and data being usable for training are not the same claim. The 100-item list manages availability. The 37-item list manages permission. They sit in different ministries and do not cross-reference.

There is also a quality argument hiding in the copyright notes. Releasing the expired-copyright portion first is a reasonable ordering, but expired usually means old. Filter a film or performing arts collection down to its expired portion and the surviving sample skews in time. Nobody touched the data, and yet what reaches the corpus is a different distribution. A licence filter is a sampling operation, which is why terms of use belong in a quality pipeline as a measured field rather than a legal footnote.

Metrics for how much has been opened are mature. There is still no name for the share of open data that carries documented grounds for training use, and no agreed way to count it.
