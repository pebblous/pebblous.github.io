아폴로 글로벌 매니지먼트 수석 이코노미스트 토르스텐 슬로크가 10월 24일 낸 차트 다섯 장 가운데, 그가 결론으로 지목한 S&P 493을 실제로 측정한 것은 두 장이다.

"빅테크 밖 미국 기업에 AI가 가져다준 재무 성과는 0"이라는 첫 문장이 주말 사이 세계를 돌았다. 차트에 찍힌 값은 전부 맞다. 어긋난 자리는 차트와 헤드라인 사이에 있다. 세 번째 장은 미국 경제 전체이고, 네 번째와 다섯 번째 장은 법인카드 회사 램프의 고객사 거래다. 대부분 중소·중견기업이고 S&P 500 기업이 아니다.

널리 인용된 "55만 달러에서 출발"도 선의 출발점이 아니다. 매그니피센트 7의 1인당 매출은 2019년 1분기 약 69만 7천 달러에서 시작해 3년을 내려온 뒤 2023년 1분기에 바닥을 찍는다. 보도가 출발점으로 쓴 자리가 그 바닥이다.

선의 출발점에서 재면 연 1.6%, 바닥에서 재면 약 39% 상승이다. 같은 선, 같은 끝점, 두 배 넘게 다른 숫자다. S&P 493은 같은 기간 연 0.9%였으니 빅테크가 약 1.8배 빨리 오른 것은 맞다. 다만 그 격차와 "한쪽은 0"은 서로 다른 주장이다.

집중도 표도 끝까지 읽을 만하다. 상위 10%가 모델 서빙·추론 지출의 99.5%를 가져갔다는 줄은 일곱 줄짜리 표의 맨 위다. 아래쪽 세 줄은 AI가 아닌 항목이고, 거기서도 상위 10%가 평균 89.3%를 가져간다. SaaS와 고객관리 소프트웨어가 거기 있다. 지출이 소수에 몰리는 것은 AI의 특징이라기보다 기업 소프트웨어 지출의 평상시 분포에 가깝다.

아흐레 전에는 뉴욕 연방준비은행의 제러미 피어스가 같은 이름의 지표를 상장사 공시로 다시 계산해 반대 결론을 냈다. 1인당 매출 증가율이 2010년대 연 0.7%에서 2019~2025년 연 2.2%로 올랐다는 것이다. 두 사람 중 누구도 자료를 잘못 보지 않았다. 갈린 것은 물가 보정 기준과 모집단, 그리고 증가율을 계산했는지 차트 위의 기울기를 읽었는지다.

"효과가 0"과 "전사 손익계산서가 그 크기를 분해하지 못한다"는 산술이 다른 문장이고, 지금 자료가 떠받치는 것은 뒤쪽이다. 어떤 AI 성과 수치를 받아 들든 네 칸부터 보면 된다. 어느 시점부터 재는가, 무엇을 그 이름으로 부르는가, 안 쓴 쪽을 같이 기록했는가, 누구를 재는가. 페블러스가 데이터를 진단할 때 점수보다 먼저 적어 두는 것도 같은 종류의 조건이다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Of the five charts Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok published on October 24, two measure the group his conclusion names.

The opening line, "zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech," traveled the world over the weekend. Every figure under it is correct. The gap sits between what the charts show and what the headline says. Chart three covers the entire US non-farm economy. Charts four and five come from Ramp, a corporate card company, and cover its customers, most of them small and mid-sized businesses rather than S&P 500 members.

The widely quoted "starting around $550,000" is not where the line starts either. Revenue per worker at the Magnificent 7 opens in Q1 2019 near $697,000, declines for three years, and bottoms in Q1 2023. The quoted starting point is that trough.

Measured from the start of the line, the series grew about 1.6% a year. Measured from the trough, it climbed roughly 39%. Same line, same endpoint, two numbers that differ by more than double. The S&P 493 grew about 0.9% a year over the same span, so big tech did compound about 1.8 times faster. But that gap and a zero are different claims.

The concentration table rewards reading to the bottom. The row showing that the top 10% of firms took 99.5% of model serving and inference spending sits at the top of a seven-row table. The bottom three rows are not AI at all, and the top 10% take an average of 89.3% of those as well. SaaS and customer relationship software are among them. Spending that pools in a few firms looks less like a property of AI than like the ordinary shape of corporate software budgets.

Nine days earlier, Jeremy Pearce at the New York Fed recomputed a metric of the same name from public company filings and reached the opposite reading. Revenue per worker grew 2.2% a year between 2019 and 2025, against 0.7% through the 2010s. Neither economist misread the data. They differed on the deflator, on the population, and on whether to compute a growth rate or read the slope of a level.

"Zero payoff" and "the corporate income statement cannot resolve a signal this small" are different arithmetic, and the second is what the current data supports. Whatever AI payoff number lands on your desk, start with four cells: when the measurement begins, what is being called by that name, whether the non-adopting side was recorded alongside, and who is being measured. ## Facebook (EN)

"Zero AI payoff."

That line went past my feed several times over the weekend, and I copied it down the first time without pausing.

It came from a five-chart note published on October 24 by Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management.

Five charts is a small enough number to check. So I downloaded the originals and read the gridlines.

Nothing in them was wrong. Every figure Slok printed holds up.

What did not hold up was the distance between the charts and the sentence on top of them. The note closes by saying the AI story will be decided in the S&P 493, and two of the five charts actually measure the S&P 493. The third covers the whole US non-farm economy. The last two come from the customers of a corporate card company, most of them small and mid-sized businesses.

Nine days before that note, Jeremy Pearce at the New York Fed recomputed a metric of the same name from public company filings and arrived at the opposite conclusion.

Same metric name. Same kind of source data. Nine days apart. Opposite readings.

Neither of them made a mistake. What differed was the deflator they chose, the population they counted, and whether they computed a growth rate or read the slope of a line.

So what I took from this argument is not a verdict. It is four cells. When does the measurement begin? What is being called by that name? Was the non-adopting side recorded alongside? Who is being measured?

"It is working at our company." Before that sentence goes out, how many of those four cells are filled in?

When Pebblous diagnoses a dataset, conditions of that kind get written down before the score. State them and a result can be reproduced. Leave them out and the same source data yields opposite conclusions, which is what October showed in public.

The cell left empty most often is the control. And a control is not something an analyst can add later. It is a column that has to be kept when the data is collected.

If those three non-AI rows had not been printed in the same Apollo table, none of this rechecking would have been possible from the outside. Apollo is the one that printed them.
