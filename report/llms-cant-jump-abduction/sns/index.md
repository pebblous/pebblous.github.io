사람이 거의 다 푸는 문제를, 최신 AI는 1%도 풀지 못한다. 무엇을 풀어야 하는지부터 스스로 알아내야 하는 ARC-AGI-3 이야기다.

구글 딥마인드 연구자 톰 자하비의 포지션 페이퍼 『LLMs can't jump』는 이 격차를 아인슈타인의 발견 도식으로 읽는다. 아인슈타인은 1952년 친구에게 보낸 편지에서 과학을 관찰과 이론 사이의 순환으로 그렸고, 그 사이에는 "논리적 다리가 없는" 직관적 도약이 하나 있다고 적었다. 오늘의 언어모델은 패턴을 요약하는 귀납과 가정에서 결론을 끌어내는 연역은 잘하지만, 100여 년 전 철학자 퍼스가 '귀추'라 부른 그 가운데의 도약만은 비어 있다는 것이다.

논문의 대표 반례는 일반상대성이론이다. 아인슈타인은 데이터를 더 잘 압축한 것이 아니라, 데이터가 거의 없는 상태에서 시공간 곡률이라는 개념 자체를 발명했다. AI가 물리 방정식을 91.6% 복원한다지만, 그것은 이미 알려진 공식의 재발견이지 새 개념의 발명이 아니다.

오해는 짚고 가자. 이것은 딥마인드의 공식 입장도, "LLM은 영원히 발견 못 한다"는 선고도 아니다. 자하비 본인이 그 해석을 공개적으로 바로잡았다. 논문의 제언은 텍스트 너머의 세계를 직접 겪는 신체화된 멀티모달 world model이다.

발견의 다음 병목은 모델 크기가 아니라 세계를 담은 데이터의 질이다. 페블러스가 데이터 품질을 진단하고 Physical AI 데이터를 붙들어 온 자리도 여기에 있다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Humans solve them almost every time. Today's frontier AI solves fewer than 1 percent.

That gap comes from ARC-AGI-3, a benchmark that withholds the goal itself: a system has to work out what the problem even is before solving it. A new position paper by Google DeepMind researcher Tom Zahavy, "LLMs can't jump," reads that gap through Einstein's 1952 diagram of discovery. Einstein drew science not as a ladder from observation to theory but as a cycle with one gap logic cannot cross: the intuitive leap from experience to a new axiom. Peirce called that leap abduction.

Today's language models handle the two familiar steps well, summarizing patterns (induction) and deriving conclusions from assumptions (deduction). The missing step is the one in the middle. The paper's headline example is General Relativity: Einstein did not compress existing data more efficiently, he invented the concept of curved spacetime with almost no data to lean on. AI can recover known physics equations with over 90 percent accuracy, but that is rediscovery, not the invention of a new principle.

One clarification matters. This is not DeepMind's official position, nor a verdict that LLMs will never discover; Zahavy corrected that reading himself. The paper's proposal is embodied, multimodal world models that experience the world beyond text. The next bottleneck in discovery is not model size but the quality of data that captures the world.

That is the same coordinate Pebblous has worked from: diagnosing data quality and building data for Physical AI.

## Facebook (EN)

In 1952, an aging scientist drew a small diagram by hand in a letter to an old friend.

It was his picture of how science actually happens.

Most of us learned science as a ladder: observe, gather data, find the rule, and a theory follows.

What Einstein drew was not a ladder but a cycle, and inside that cycle sat a gap logic could not close. The place where you leap from experience to a new idea. He wrote a single word there: the intuitive jump.

A researcher at Google DeepMind recently reached back for that old diagram.

Today's language models, he argued, are remarkably good at summarizing what they observe and deriving conclusions from assumptions. It is only the step in the middle they cannot take. A century ago the philosopher Peirce gave that leap a name, abduction: the act of inventing a new hypothesis to explain what you have seen.

One figure stayed with me.

On a test that hands you no goal, where you must first work out what the problem even is, people solve nearly all of it and today's most advanced AI solves less than one percent. Not because it is weak, but because a different kind of reasoning is being asked of it.

What lingered was the line that came next. To teach the leap, you may need data drawn from actually living in the world, not just reading about it. The bottleneck for discovery is not the size of the model but the quality of the data that holds the world. That is the same ground Pebblous has been standing on, diagnosing data quality and building data for Physical AI.

AI keeps getting better at answering the questions we pose. But inventing the question itself, whose work is that, still?
