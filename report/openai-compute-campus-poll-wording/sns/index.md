조례는 다른 자로 쓰여 있다. 11월 3일 투표에 오른 데이터센터 안건 41건이 세는 것은 낱말이 아니라 메가와트와 달러다. 공개된 것 가운데 가장 낮은 문턱은 오하이오 어버나의 7.5MW다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

The phrase OpenAI wants to drop is written into a poll question.

Axios reported on November 1, 2026 that OpenAI will argue the sites it is building should be called AI compute campuses rather than legacy data centers. That is a plan to argue, not a change of official designation. A day later, on November 2, the company used the phrase in its own grid announcement.

The phrase sits inside the measuring instrument. In its national survey Quinnipiac has asked one sentence all year without altering a word: would you support or oppose the building of an AI data center in your community? Opposition among U.S. adults ran from 65% in March to 72% in September. Change the wording and that line stops being one line.

The case for renaming is thinner than it looks. The Annenberg Public Policy Center asked about data centers without the word AI at all, and opposition there rose too, from 49% to 61%. The two sample designs differ, so the gains cannot be subtracted from one another, but dropping AI plainly did not hold opposition down. Field experiments on salient, contested issues find that reframing tends to harden existing positions rather than move them.

Local ballots are written in a different unit. Forty-one data center measures reached ballots across ten states on November 3, and what they count is megawatts and dollars, not nouns. The lowest published threshold, in Urbana, Ohio, is 7.5 MW.

So the cost of the change is not persuasion. It is measurement. What goes missing the moment a name changes is not public opinion but the basis for comparing it. Pebblous tracks the quality of AI training data with DataClinic.

## Facebook (EN)

A ballot question in Urbana, Ohio, on November 3 did not name a kind of building. It named a number.

7.5 megawatts. A measure to stop anything above that line from being built.

The same week, the naming side produced news of its own. Axios reported on November 1 that OpenAI will argue the sites it is building should be called AI compute campuses rather than legacy data centers. A day later the company used the phrase in its own grid announcement, in a sentence saying households and small businesses should not be asked to subsidize that electricity demand.

Wanting a more accurate name is a reasonable thing. What complicated it for me was seeing where the old name currently sits.

It sits inside the instrument.

In its national survey Quinnipiac has asked one sentence all year without altering a word, and the noun phrase AI data center sits whole inside it. That fixity is the only reason there is a line to look at: 65% opposition among U.S. adults in March, 72% in September.

Change the question and the line stops. Even when the new name is the better one.

The stranger part is what happens when you check whether a new name would move the number at all. The Annenberg Public Policy Center asked without the word AI at all, and opposition there rose too, from 49% to 61%. The sample designs differ, so the two gains cannot be set against each other. What can be said is that leaving AI out did not hold opposition down.

"On the day we renamed that metric, was there a single stretch where the old definition and the new one ran side by side?"

It is called a split ballot: inside one study, half the sample gets the old wording and half gets the new one, and the gap between the two is written down before the switch. With that record, a broken line can be rejoined later. Without it, the series simply becomes two series.

The ballots never run into this. What an ordinance counts is megawatts and dollars, not nouns. Whatever the building ends up being called, 7.5 megawatts is 7.5 megawatts.
