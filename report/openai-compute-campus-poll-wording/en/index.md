# The Data Center Label OpenAI Wants to Drop Is Written Into the Poll Question

_Quinnipiac kept the phrase AI data center fixed in its question all year, drawing a line from 65% to 72% opposition among U.S. adults_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> OpenAI will argue that the facilities it is building should be called AI compute campuses. Axios reported the plan on November 1, 2026, and a day later, on November 2, OpenAI was already using the phrase in a release of its own. Rather than relay that news, this article follows the words OpenAI wants to drop and asks where those words currently sit.

> They sit inside an instrument. Quinnipiac, a U.S. polling house, has carried "an AI data center" inside one item of its questionnaire, and not a letter of that item changed through 2026. From national adults came one line; from Pennsylvania registered voters came a second. Change that wording and both lines break. Yet the evidence that a new name would lower opposition turns out to be thin. Annenberg, whose question never used the word AI at all, recorded a steeper rise over the same months, and research on salient, contested issues finds that reframing pushes people further toward what they already believed rather than persuading them. The cost that remains, then, is not persuasion. It is measurement.

> Everything above is what the releases report. What follows is our reading. This article does not say OpenAI is manipulating public opinion. It says this: change can only be measured when the same thing keeps being called by the same name, the name doing the measuring is now in play, and the party proposing the change has an interest in the result. That the column emptying fastest during a 7-point rise in opposition was "don't know" rather than support is a fact in the releases. Calling that the phase in which opinion gets formed is our interpretation.

## One Day From Proposal to First Use

What Axios reported on November 1, 2026 was a proposal. The relevant passage says OpenAI "will argue that the infrastructure it's building, in contrast to legacy data centers, should be called 'AI compute campuses.'" There are two verbs stacked there. It will argue, and the facilities should be called. The report does not say an internal style guide was rewritten or an official name was swapped out.

The proposal did not arrive on its own. The policy push Axios described has four strands: legislation requiring mandatory safeguards on the most capable AI systems, a data center bundle, support for workers in transition, and international AI standards. The naming argument is the third item inside the second strand. The two items ahead of it in that same strand are community benefit agreements and state legislation giving residents a voice in how projects get built and what they receive in return. A demand to rename the buildings and a demand to widen residents' authority travel in one envelope. Either demand read on its own misrepresents the package.

An OpenAI spokesperson explained the reasoning in a statement to Business Insider: "Compute campuses better describes the scale and role of the infrastructure we're building for AI. These are long-term investments in computing, energy infrastructure and skilled work." This statement, too, stops short of saying a decision was made. It says the phrase describes things better. So far the modality of the report and the modality of the statement agree.

A day later, though, one notch moved. On November 2, 2026, OpenAI used the phrase in its own release about the electricity grid, the Grid Protectors Program. One sentence in that release reads:

OpenAI release · 2026-11-02"Families and small businesses should not be asked to subsidize a compute campus's electricity needs."

Where the new phrase first landed in a company document is worth recording: a sentence about electricity bills. When Quinnipiac read three options to respondents who said they opposed such a facility in March 2026 and asked why, electricity costs drew 72%, water use 64%, and noise 41%. Multiple answers were allowed, so the total runs past 100. There is no evidence the sentence in the release was written with those options in view. What can be observed is that the new phrase and the hottest reason for opposition ended up in the same sentence.

The narrowest claim this article can make therefore has two steps. What was reported on November 1 is a plan to argue for the name, and on November 2 OpenAI began using it in a release of its own. No official name for all data centers has changed, and the subject is the company's own new construction.

The words OpenAI wants to drop are also words OpenAI has been using. Sachin Katti, who leads compute infrastructure at the company, wrote on an executive account in March 2026 that "Our flagship Stargate site is one of the largest AI data center campuses in the United States," referring to the site in Abilene, Texas. "Campus" was already there. So was "data center" in front of it. What this proposal would change is one word in that name.

How the verb for a single event wobbles as it crosses from outlet to outlet also deserves recording. In an article about the force of wording, counting the reporting verbs comes first. Below are four renderings of the same event.

| Who said it | Wording used | What it claims |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Axios (original report) | will argue … should be called | It intends to make the case |
| Bisnow (headline) | Plans Data Center Rebrand | A rename is planned |
| Some follow-up coverage | may rebrand / is considering | It might rename, it is weighing it |
| The one-line brief that reached this report | has decided to call them 'compute campuses' | It is settled |

One event, and the verb slides from intends to argue, to plans, to is considering, and finally to settled. The bottom row is not somebody else's problem. That is how the one-line brief handed to this report was written, and it was corrected only after the Axios original was opened. The body of this article uses the top row, the narrowest of the four.

The timing is stated too. Chris Lehane, who runs global affairs at OpenAI, said the company would press these proposals starting with the lame-duck session that convenes on November 9, 2026. Of the current opposition he said, "We think the conversation is good and healthy. It's democracy in action." He added, "You're going to see us talk more and more about the concept of speed limits," and of the next presidential cycle, "AI is going to be the issue in 2028."

A note on transparency. Axios, which broke this story, attached its own disclosure at the foot of the piece: "Axios has a content licensing and technology agreement with OpenAI for local news expansion." A disclosure is no reason to doubt the reporting. That an exclusive about a naming campaign came from an outlet under contract with the company is still something a reader deserves to know going in.

## Quinnipiac Asked the Same One Line All Year

The question Quinnipiac used throughout 2026 is a single line: "Would you support or oppose the building of an AI data center in your community?" That one line contains the noun phrase "an AI data center" whole. It is exactly the phrase OpenAI wants changed. To be precise, the part OpenAI would strip is "data center," not "AI," and "AI" survives in the proposed name.

The house asked twice nationally and twice in Pennsylvania. Within each series the sentence stayed put, and only between the two series is there a slight difference. That is why there are two lines rather than one. The two tables below take their values from the trend tables Quinnipiac publishes itself. Sample sizes and the margins of error that follow from them differ a little between waves, but the sentence asked, the mode of interview, and the weighting procedure do not. This is not an online panel: it is random-digit dialing across landlines and cell phones, with live interviewers asking the question.

The two rows below are March and September 2026, six months apart. The population at both points is national adults, and the sentence asked is the single line quoted above. What changed is the sample size and the margin of error that comes with it. Three things have to hold before the word trendline means anything: the same house, the same sentence, the same population. This table is a case where all three hold.

### 2.3. 72 and 73 are two populations from one interview

Coverage of this story circulates 72% and 73% side by side. Both are real values, and both come from the same interviews, conducted September 24 to 27, 2026. What differs is which column was read. Count all 1,202 adults and opposition is 72%; count only the 1,032 self-identified registered voters inside that sample and it is 73%. These are not two parallel surveys but one questionnaire split across two releases. The question numbers in the registered-voter release skip from 18 to 22 to 28, and every missing number appears in the adult release. The data center question is Q22 in both.

Reconstructed from Quinnipiac releases 3968 and 3969 and the crosstabs attached to each. Before any word is changed, changing who was asked already moves the figure by a point or two.

This distinction gets expensive because of the trendline. The trend table Quinnipiac builds itself carries two values on the adult base, 65 and 72. The 73% is not in it. So writing "opposition rose from 65% in March to 73% in September" produces a line that switches populations between its two points. Both numbers are right and the line is wrong. There is also a second 73% in Quinnipiac's output that same week. The September 30 release is headlined "73% Concerned About Potential Threat To Human Survival," which is a different question entirely and has nothing to do with data centers.

Why different surveys report different opposition rates is a question [an earlier report](/report/datacenter-opposition-reasons-pa-study/en/) worked through. That article looked at the scatter produced when many houses ask in many ways; this one looks at the line a single house has drawn with a single phrase. Cross-sections and time series are fragile in different ways. A cross-section wobbles when questions differ. A time series breaks when a question is revised.

### 2.4. What the rules ask of whoever holds the question still

The house that held the wording fixed knows the word itself has shifted in public debate. Tim Malloy, a Quinnipiac polling analyst, put it this way in Bisnow on November 2, 2026.

Tim Malloy · Quinnipiac polling analyst"Data centers, not long ago just the vague description for a network of massive structures needed for an emerging industry, are now the subject of the 'NIMBY' rallying cry. Voters overwhelmingly are saying 'not in my back yard.'"

What stayed fixed is the surface of the word, not what the word points at. That makes the rules governing question wording stricter, not looser. AAPOR's best practices for survey research put the principle in one line: do not change the ruler if you want to measure change. Knowing whether a gap between two waves reflects a real shift in attitude requires holding question wording, surrounding context, and mode of interview as nearly identical as possible. When the wording is altered, the result either looks like movement where there was none or hides movement that did occur.

> [!callout]
> The same guidance also prescribes what to do when a change really is necessary. It is the split-ballot. Inside one survey the sample is divided, half hears the old wording and half the new, and the gap between the two wordings gets measured before the switch takes effect. With that gap on record, there is a basis for stitching the broken line back together. Skip it and the series simply becomes two fragments on either side of the date it changed.

## The Column That Emptied Fastest: "Don't Know"

Nobody usually reads the "don't know" column. In the national adult survey, though, that is the column that emptied most over six months. The three columns sum to 100, so the arithmetic closes. Support 24, oppose 65, don't know 11 became support 21, oppose 72, don't know 7, which means the 7 points opposition gained equal the 4 points "don't know" lost plus the 3 points support lost. Of the two columns that shrank, "don't know" is the larger.

Values from Quinnipiac's official trend table, redrawn as 100% stacked bars. Whatever the middle block gains, the block at the right end gives up.

Restraint is required here. These figures are marginal distributions from two separate samples, not the same people followed twice. The **fact** is that the "don't know" column fell from 11 to 7 while opposition rose from 65 to 72. The **interpretation** is that newly formed opinion went toward opposition. Writing "people who had no answer turned against it" produces a sentence the data cannot carry. Individual movement is not in this table.

Pennsylvania is composed differently. Opposition gained 6 points there, of which only 2 came from "don't know" and 4 came from support. National and state figures move in the same direction along different routes. That is precisely why one state's numbers cannot stand in for the country, or the reverse.

Reading the no-opinion column as a signal is an old habit in the survey literature. Since Philip Converse raised the idea of non-attitudes in 1964, researchers have confirmed again and again that many respondents hold no real opinion on a policy question and manufacture an answer on the spot. Work in the Jon Krosnick tradition reports that no-opinion responses rise as a question grows harder or more remote from the respondent. Turned around, a stretch in which "don't know" shrinks is a stretch in which the issue has come closer and grown easier. It is the period when opinion is being formed, not the period when it hardens.

The tempting next step is "so now is the ideal moment to change the word." The reasoning runs that opinion has not set yet, so a new name would move the numbers. Where that reasoning runs aground is the subject of the next section.

## How Far Do the Numbers Move When the Name Changes?

That changing a word in a question changes the answers is among the most thoroughly verified findings in survey methodology. How much it changes them, however, varies case by case, and even the textbook examples split on whether they replicate. The table below sets out four cases this article verified, putting effect size and replication status on the same row. Reading the effect sizes alone is how this literature gets misused.

| Word swapped | Effect size | Replication status | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "welfare" ↔ "assistance to the poor" | A gap in support averaging roughly 39 points | Replicated repeatedly across decades | Smith 1987, POQ 51 |
| "global warming" ↔ "climate change" | 67.7% accepted that it is happening when asked about "global warming" against 75.0% for "climate change," a 6.3-point gap. The gap was widest among Republicans at 44.0% versus 60.2% and absent among Democrats at 86.9% versus 86.4% | It vanished in a preregistered direct replication: 88.8% against 88.0%, no significant difference | Schuldt, Konrath & Schwarz 2011 / Soutter & Mõttus 2020 |
| The original authors' reply to that replication | Small but detectable in a 2018 probability sample (Cramér's V 0.09) | Both sides agree at least that the effect has weakened | Schuldt et al. 2020 |
| Change the label and what people picture changes too | 83% associated soup kitchens with "assistance to the poor" against 41% for "welfare" | A critique aimed at the premise of wording-effect research itself | Huber & Paris 2013, POQ 77 |

____

Rows two and three must not be read apart. The 6.3-point gap is widely cited, and the fact that it vanished in a preregistered replication belongs to the same body of literature.

Row four matters most here. Its claim is that the label is not all that changes: the object people picture changes with it. A soup kitchen comes to mind under "assistance to the poor" and largely fails to under "welfare." If so, the two questions are not one thing asked two ways but two different things asked. The critique meshes oddly well with OpenAI's argument. OpenAI says its facilities belong to a different class from the legacy data center, and the wording-effect literature says a different name really does put a different object in people's heads. Is the name false, then, or is it more accurate? That question is the door into the next section.

One further condition attaches. Once an issue has already grown hot, renaming works as mobilization rather than persuasion. Field experiments run by Bechtel, Hainmueller and colleagues during actual referendum campaigns report that on salient, contested issues, frames and cues pushed respondents further toward their existing partisanship regardless of which direction the frame pointed. Data centers in the United States are already such an issue. By AdImpact's count, $173 million went into advertising built around data centers in the 2026 elections, with $95 million of that concentrated in Ohio, Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania. Nearly all of it was negative.

### 4.1. The measurements in this case point the other way

Swapping inference for measurement makes this sharper. Two time series covered the same subject over the same months, one with "AI" in the question and one without. If the word AI inflates opposition, the series that carries it should climb more steeply. The result is the reverse.

| Survey | Noun phrase in the question | Waves | Change in opposition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quinnipiac national (adults) | an AI data center | 2026-03 → 2026-09 | 65 → 72 (+7 pts) |
| Annenberg Public Policy Center (adults) | data center (no "AI") | 2026-02/03 → 2026-06/07 | 49 → 61 (+12 pts) |

Annenberg wave one sampled 1,330 and wave two 1,320 (field June 16 to July 19). The two houses differ in sample design and mode, so the two increases cannot simply be subtracted from one another. What can be said is that the series without "AI" did not rise less.

The internal composition of the Annenberg result also cuts against conventional wisdom. In wave two, opposition peaked at 70% among respondents under 30 and bottomed out at 57% among those 65 and over. That inverts the usual pattern in which younger people are the more permissive toward new technology, and Annenberg's researchers flagged the point themselves. Frequent AI users opposed at 60%, barely different from those who hardly use it at all. The hypothesis that familiarity with AI softens data center opposition does not stand on this evidence. By party, 69% of Democrats, 54% of Republicans and 53% of independents opposed, so a majority opposes on every side.

> [!callout]
> To sum up: words can move the numbers, and how far they move them is unknown. In this particular case the evidence leans toward not much. The cost arrives anyway, because the line breaks whether or not the numbers move. What a rename forfeits at the moment it takes effect is not public opinion but the basis for comparing it.

This blog covered another instance of a split name making things hard to count only a month ago, in [a piece on the National Science Foundation](/blog/nsf-grant-ai-to-si-renaming/en/) offering to change "AI" to "SI" in grant abstracts. That case involved government documents and research funding tallies; this one involves a company and a public opinion series. What they share is a structure in which whoever revises the name and whoever has been counting with it are not the same people.

## Are OpenAI's Buildings Really a Different Kind?

OpenAI's argument names its comparison explicitly. The sentence Axios quoted contains the phrase "in contrast to legacy data centers." Before it is a proposal about what to call something, this is a claim about classification. What the company builds, it says, is a different kind of building from what has been called a data center until now. The claim could be true. So the right move is to split it by axis and test it.

The left column of the table below gives industry benchmark ranges for existing hyperscale cloud campuses; the right column gives published figures for three AI training campuses built between 2025 and 2026: Stargate Abilene in Texas, Meta's Hyperion in Louisiana, and xAI's Colossus in Tennessee. Cells with nothing to report say not disclosed rather than sit blank. The empty cells are results of this table too.

| Axis | Existing hyperscale | AI training campuses (2025–26) | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rack power density | 5–10 kW for traditional builds. The 2026 industry average is 27 kW, and the modal figure in Uptime Institute's data is 11 kW | 40–132 kW for AI training clusters (NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 peaks at 132 kW) | Clearly different |
| Site footprint | 10–40 acres for traditional enterprise and colocation sites. Data center land deals in 2024 averaged 224 acres | Abilene about 1,100 acres; Hyperion 2,250–3,650 acres depending on the report | Clearly different |

Verified November 4, 2026 (KST). Of the three campuses, only Abilene is an OpenAI project; Hyperion belongs to Meta and Colossus to xAI. The 40–132 kW in the density row is a general industry range, and none of the three campuses has published rack density figures of its own.

The result differs by axis. On power density and footprint, OpenAI's claim is close to true. The abatement structure reuses the existing playbook for attracting data centers outright. Permanent employment will not support a comparison at all: benchmarks span a tenfold range from 20 to 200 per 100 MW, and the campus-side numbers differ by three or four times between the contractual minimum and the company's own publicity. Water, the most contested axis of all, has no official figure from any of the three, so there is nothing to judge with. The statement "different from legacy data centers" is true on some axes, unsupported on others, and untestable on the axis that matters most.

> [!callout]
> The knot of this section comes after that, though. A legitimate redefinition breaks the trendline just the same. Even if the new name describes reality better than the old one, comparison with the earlier values ends on the day it is adopted. The price of a more accurate name is the eye that could see change. So the question to ask is not whether the name is wrong but what is being traded for what.

### 5.1. Where two readings came out opposed

The permanent jobs row is where preparing this article produced opposite conclusions twice. One calculation set 500 workers against Hyperion's 5,000 MW, read that as 0.1 per MW, and wrote that it falls an order of magnitude below the usual 1 to 2 per MW. The other converted the same figures to a per-100-MW basis and wrote that they land inside the benchmark range. Each is correct on its own arithmetic. They parted over whether the benchmark is 20–50 per 100 MW or 100–200. When the ruler differs, the same fact yields opposite conclusions. The problem this article is about turned up once more inside its own source material.

### 5.2. One number moves no matter what the buildings are called

Section 1 noted that electricity costs were the reason opponents cited most in Quinnipiac's March survey. That number is already moving, and the name has nothing to do with it. Two figures are the firmest. First, PJM capacity market auction prices rose 833% year over year, and Monitoring Analytics, the independent market monitor, attributed $9.3 billion of the $14.7 billion total to data center demand, or 63%. That is an accounting allocation of market costs rather than an estimate. Second, residential electricity rates in the six states where data centers cluster most have risen 25.6% since 2022, against 23.7% elsewhere. The gap is about 2 points.

Nothing like "sharply wider" should be attached to that second figure. Two points is not a large gap. What the number says is that dense areas rose somewhat more, not that data centers drove rates up. Natural gas prices and the cost of replacing aging transmission were at work over the same years. The direction is clear regardless. Renaming the buildings AI compute campuses changes neither the PJM auction price nor anyone's utility bill. The power-side background is laid out separately in [a report on the power wall](/report/ai-datacenter-power-wall-2026/en/).

### 5.3. The example OpenAI chose is somewhere else

One addition is needed. The project OpenAI offered in the Axios report as its instance of "different from legacy data centers" is not among the three campuses above. It is a site in Pike County, Ohio: the PORTS-Pike Technology Campus, announced August 17, 2026, which SB Energy builds, owns and operates with OpenAI as a 20-year tenant. The first 800 MW arrives in 2028 and completion is set for 2032, and OpenAI has committed $164 million to community and education programs in Ohio. Abilene's figures must not be attached to the Pike County story. On this subject, writing down which site a number belongs to every single time is a matter of accuracy rather than style.

## The Megawatt Thresholds Behind 41 Ballot Measures

Data center measures on U.S. ballots for November 3, 2026 number 41 across 10 states. That is Ballotpedia's count, which Bloomberg recounted and reported as "dozens." If passed, 28 would restrict development and 13 would make it easier. Across the full year the figure becomes 50 measures in 13 states. Six more were already decided before November 3 and three are scheduled after it. Mixing these counts is best avoided. Ballotpedia records that all six of the earlier ones came out on the side of tighter regulation. Forecasting forty-one from six, though, is another matter.

How fast the count grew shows when it is set beside this blog's own record from four months earlier. The [Monterey Park piece](/blog/monterey-park-data-center-ban/en/) from July 2026 recorded the first U.S. city to ban data centers by ballot. There was one of them then, carried with 86% in favor. Four months on there are 41 measures of the same kind.

Where the 41 sit reveals the geography of this issue. More than half are in a single state.

Here is where this article turns. What these ordinances count is not words. The Kansas measure bans facilities above 25 MW, and Urbana, Ohio bans those above 7.5 MW, the lowest published threshold of the set. The 25 MW threshold reached nine separate ballots within Ohio alone in identical language. One Wisconsin measure requires resident approval for projects above $450 million, and the North Dakota measure requires independent expert review and a public hearing for projects over its threshold. Two of the Illinois measures are non-binding expressions of opposition. Every limit in this list is written in megawatts or dollars.

> [!callout]
> Whatever the name on the site plan, 25 MW is 25 MW. A naming campaign can shake a poll question and leave an ordinance threshold untouched. Among measuring instruments, the ones written in words break when the name changes and the ones written in numbers stay where they are. That difference separates two kinds of measurement.

Two notes keep the scope honest. First, all 21 Ohio measures are local. A statewide constitutional amendment banning anything above 25 MW was confirmed on June 19, 2026 as failing to qualify for this year's ballot. Second, the actual text of these ordinances was not obtained in the original. What appears above reproduces the summaries given by the tally sources and the coverage, which is why no clause here is placed in quotation marks.

No results were in at the time of writing. Polls in Ohio closed at 7:30 p.m. local time on November 3, 2026, which is 9:30 a.m. KST on November 4. This draft was finished before that. No outcome is estimated here. Whichever way the results fall, the claims in the sections above hold. Whether all 41 pass or all 41 fail, measuring change still requires calling the same thing by the same name.

## Why Pebblous Cares

This is an article about measurement rather than about public opinion. There is a question Pebblous asks first whenever we open a customer's data: when did the definition of this column change? Without knowing the date of a definition change, a trend is not a trend but two fragments, and a chart that welds the fragments together either shows a change that never happened or erases one that did. This article put that question to a single item on a national survey.

### 7.1. Three quality lessons this story hands over

First, one word in the name of a population changes the number. A single set of interviews produces 72 on the adult base and 73 on the registered-voter base, and the same Q28 splits into 81 and 79. In data work the equivalent is one filter line defining what gets counted. Without knowing where that filter is written, nobody can explain why two reports disagree.

Second, "don't know" is a signal rather than a residual. While opposition gained 7 points, that column lost 4, more than the 3 points support gave up. It plays the same role as the share of deferred judgments in a labeling job. A stretch where deferrals fall is usually a stretch where annotators are settling on a criterion, and where the decision boundary moved in that stretch stays invisible unless the deferral rate is watched on its own.

Third, a new name can be the more correct one and still not join up with the values recorded before it. The same thing happens on the day a schema column gets a name that fits it better, and the dashboard raises no warning that day. Two numbers becoming incomparable because one company counted "revenue" one way and another counted it differently is something we watched play out [at these very companies](/blog/openai-anthropic-annualized-revenue-definition/en/). There the definitions differed between firms; here a definition is about to differ before and after a date.

### 7.2. Four questions for renaming a metric

Four questions fall straight out of this story. They are not specific to polling: they apply just as directly to KPIs, label definitions and schema column names.

- **What changed that prompts the rename?** Has the object itself changed, or is only its appearance being adjusted? For OpenAI, the object really did change on power density and footprint, and did not on tax structure.
- **What breaks if it changes?** Measure the length of the line that breaks, and write down who had been making decisions from it.
- **Can there be a period of overlap?** Survey research already has a name for this: the split-ballot. One sample is divided so the old wording and the new are each put to a half, and the gap gets measured first. And once a change is made, there are only three ways to report it. The Pew Research Center published the framework when it moved from telephone to online, and it sets out all three: break the trend and show only the new values, place the new values inside the old trend with the change point marked, or wait until several years of new values accumulate and then join them gently.
- **Does whoever proposes the change have a stake in the result?** If so, record that fact alongside the change. Recording it is enough, and the rest belongs to whoever is reading.

### 7.3. A record made in advance, not an audit after the fact

Articles of this kind on this blog have mostly been audits after the fact. A bias test [that was actually measuring reasoning ability](/report/benchmark-concept-validity-2026-09/en/), revenue defined differently from company to company, "AI" offered up for replacement by "SI" in grant abstracts: each of those is a record of opening up a metric once the thing had already happened. This article stands somewhere slightly different. It writes down the moment just before the measuring word changes. Written down afterward, the account has to begin by reconstructing what the values were before.

A note on what this article has not done. It reaches no verdict that OpenAI is manipulating public opinion, no verdict that "AI compute campus" is an inaccurate name, and no position on whether these facilities should be built. It estimates no results from the November 3 vote. And every time a number appears, the population behind it appears on the same line: 72 is adults, 73 is registered voters, and the trendline carries only the former. Thank you for reading this far.
