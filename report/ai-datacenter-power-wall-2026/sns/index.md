## LinkedIn (EN)

This summer more than 75 US data center projects worth $130 billion were halted. Not for lack of chips, not for lack of capital. The blocker was electricity.

The trigger was local opposition, but what drove residents to the permitting hearings was the fear of rising power bills and grid strain. A separate, physical squeeze is tightening too: Gartner expects power availability to constrain operations at 40% of new AI data centers by 2027.

AI's binding constraint has moved from capital to chips to power. And when electricity becomes the currency, the unit of account changes with it. The question is no longer how big your model is, but how much useful output you get per watt.

Here is what most miss in that shift: training and inference run on redundant, low-quality data is simply wasted power. Hyperscalers can restart nuclear plants and sign 20-year supply deals. Everyone else has one lever they can pull today, and it is the data already in hand.

Cleaning up your data has quietly become a way to save watts.

## Facebook (EN)

"A data center is moving in, and my power bill is going up."

This summer that worry spread across 142 cities in 42 US states.

The projects it stopped were worth $130 billion. Seventy-five of them. Not because the chips ran out, or the money did. Because of electricity.

AI's bottleneck keeps moving. First it was capital, then chips, and now power. You can buy a GPU in a few months, but a power plant and the lines to carry its electricity take years. That gap is the wall.

And when power becomes the currency, the way we measure changes too. Not how large the model is, but how much useful work comes out of the same electricity. Which quietly reopens an older question.

"What, then, was all that training on redundant, low-quality data?"

It was electricity, burned. Hyperscalers can restart reactors and sign twenty-year supply deals. Most companies cannot. What they hold instead is the data already in their hands.

This is where a question Pebblous has sat with for years comes back. Data efficiency, long spoken in the language of cost and performance, now translates into the language of power and carbon.

The race to build bigger is becoming a race to do more with the same electricity. And its first move may not be a power plant, but a single line of data, cleaned up today.
