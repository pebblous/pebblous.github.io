필즈상 수상자 테렌스 타오가 10월 22일 《네이처》에 쓴 칼럼의 진단은 수학의 위기가 증명이 모자라서가 아니라 넘쳐서 온다는 것이다.

타오가 그린 그림은 단순하다. 문제 풀이는 생성, 검증, 설명, 출판, 정전화의 다섯 단계 사슬인데 AI는 앞의 둘만 빠르게 했다. 뒤의 셋은 사람 속도 그대로라 단계 사이 네 틈마다 증명이 쌓인다. 앞서 올린 에세이가 이 증상에 「증명의 소화불량」이라는 이름을 붙였고, 오픈AI가 수학 원고 722편을 하룻밤에 올린 10월 6일에는 마스토돈에서 처음으로 「수학 2.0」을 입에 올렸다. 네이처 칼럼은 그 넉 달의 압축판이다.

학술지와 우선권 관행, 채용과 승진 기준, 상은 전부 증명이 귀하다는 전제 위에 설계됐다고 타오는 본다. 과잉 앞에서 그것들이 제대로 돌아가지 않는 것은 당연하다는 진단이 거기서 나온다. 처방은 AI를 막는 것이 아니다. 무엇을 수학의 성과로 칠지 기준을 바꿔, 설명하고 심사하고 교과서로 옮기는 일에 비중을 옮기자는 것이다. 사람이 제대로 설명하지 못하는 증명은 형식 검증을 통과했더라도 불완전하다는 것이 그의 경험칙이다.

타오는 이 경고를 순수수학 밖으로 끌고 간다. 신용 승인과 금융 리스크 모델이 그 예다. 그런데 미국 연준은 올해 4월 모델 리스크 지침을 새로 내면서 생성형 AI를 적용 범위 밖으로 뺐다. 이해를 요구하던 규제가 AI 앞에서 비어 있는 것이 2026년 10월의 상태다.

데이터 파이프라인도 같은 사슬이다. 자동 라벨러와 자동 검증기는 앞의 두 단계를 빠르게 했고, 왜 이 라벨인지 적고 팀이 받아들이고 정본으로 남기는 뒤의 세 단계는 사람 속도 그대로다. 페블러스는 DataClinic으로 AI 학습 데이터의 품질을 정량 진단하는 회사다. 그 리포트가 서는 자리가 검증 뒤의 세 칸이다. 검증기를 더 빨리 돌려도 소화의 틈은 좁아지지 않는다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Fields Medalist Terence Tao's October 22 column in Nature argues that mathematics is in trouble because it has too many proofs, not too few.

Tao's picture is simple. Solving a problem is a chain of five stages, generation, verification, exposition, publication, and canonicalization, and AI has sped up only the first two. The other three still run at human speed, so proofs pile up in the four gaps between them. An essay Tao posted earlier named the symptom "proof indigestion," and on October 6, the day OpenAI put 722 mathematics manuscripts online in a single release, he used the phrase "Math 2.0" in public for the first time, on Mathstodon. The Nature column compresses those four months.

Journals, priority conventions, hiring and promotion criteria, and prizes were all designed on the assumption that proofs are scarce, Tao writes, and it should surprise nobody that they work badly under abundance. His remedy is not to ban AI. It is to change what counts as a mathematical achievement, shifting weight toward explaining, refereeing, and writing results into textbooks. His own rule of thumb: a proof that no human can properly explain should be viewed as incomplete, even if it has been formally verified.

He also carries the warning outside pure mathematics, to the AI models already used in credit approval and financial risk. Regulation moved the other way this year. In April the US Federal Reserve replaced its model risk guidance and placed generative AI outside its scope. As of October 2026, the rules that once demanded that a model be understood are empty where AI is concerned.

Data pipelines run on the same chain. Auto-labelers and automated checks have made the first two stages fast, while writing down why a label is what it is, getting a team to accept the data, and leaving it as a reference still move at human speed. Pebblous reads the quality of AI training data with DataClinic, and its reports sit in those three cells after verification. A faster verifier does not close the digestion gap.

## Facebook (EN)

Terence Tao could not find anyone to call.

This was after October 6, when OpenAI put 722 mathematics manuscripts online in a single night. On the radio, Tao said there was nobody at OpenAI he could phone to help him understand the dump.

The proofs were not wrong. Some of them had been checked by machine. Nobody was there to explain them in a seminar, take a colleague's questions, and carry them into the textbooks of the next generation.

Tao draws problem solving as a chain of five stages.

Generation, verification, exposition, publication, canonicalization.

AI has made the first two fast. The other three still move at human speed, and proofs pile up in the four gaps between them. The essay he posted in August called this condition "proof indigestion." The Nature column of October 22 compresses those four months for a general reader.

"If a proof passes formal verification and nobody can explain it, is it finished?"

Tao's answer is no. His rule of thumb is that authors who cannot give a clear, expert-level talk on a result should not publish it.

The sentence I underlined came later in the essay. The success of AI in mathematics stands on a foundation of canonical theories that mathematicians spent centuries writing into textbooks. The training data, Tao writes, is quite literally the output of canonicalization. An undigested proof never becomes canon, and what is not canon never becomes training data for the next model. Indigestion cuts that loop.

The same week the column ran, Tao's first book for general readers appeared, 128 pages on six ideas. It read like a footnote on how far he practices the slowest stage himself.
