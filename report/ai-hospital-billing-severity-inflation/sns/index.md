## LinkedIn (KO)

AI가 미국 의료비를 올렸다는 근거로 인용되는 9억 4,200만 달러짜리 분석에, AI를 관측한 변수는 하나도 없다.

미국 최대 보험자 연합인 블루크로스블루실드협회가 9월 24일 낸 백서다. 지난 2년치 입원 청구에서 복합으로 분류된 비율이 약 37%에서 약 40%로 올랐고, 그 시기가 병원의 AI 코딩 도구 도입과 겹친다고 적는다. 어느 병원이 무슨 도구를 들였는지는 청구 데이터에 적히지 않는다. 백서 자신의 동사도 조건부에 머문다. 시기가 겹친다, 기여하는 것으로 보인다고 쓴다. 그 조건부를 인과로 굳힌 쪽은 헤드라인이었다.

근거로 내놓은 장면은 영리하다. 2025년 장 수술 청구에서, 복합 코딩이 가장 빨리 느는 상위 25% 병원은 나머지 병원보다 빈혈을 38% 더 자주 찾아냈다. 그런데 그 빈혈 환자에게 수혈한 비율은 16.9%로 나머지 병원의 19.3%보다 낮았다. 진단이 있는데 따라와야 할 처치가 없다는 이 판정 장치는, 데이터 품질 작업에서 정합성 규칙이라 부르는 것과 같은 계열이다.

문제는 그 규칙이 서 있는 땅의 넓이다. 대조표가 나온 장 수술 계열은 9억 4,200만 달러의 6.5%뿐이고, 같은 표의 재수술률은 방향이 반대다. 규칙을 만든 쪽은 돈을 내는 당사자였고, 규칙은 데이터가 생긴 뒤에 만들어졌다.

도구를 넣기 전과 넣은 뒤를 직접 비교한 연구는 따로 나와 있다. 진료 대화를 받아 적는 도구를 도입한 조직에서 건당 문서화 진단이 3.0개에서 4.1개로 늘었다는 보고다. 가장 큰 숫자를 가진 문서와 가장 단단한 식별을 가진 문서가 서로 다르고, 인용된 쪽은 앞의 것이었다. 메디케어 어드밴티지에서 같은 논쟁이 20년 걸렸는데, 격차를 실제로 좁힌 것은 감사도 고발도 아니라 지불 규칙을 다시 설계한 일이었다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

The analysis behind this month's headlines about AI raising American hospital bills never observed AI at all.

It is a white paper published on 24 September by the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association, the largest federation of health insurers in the United States. Across the past two years of inpatient claims, it reports, the share billed as complex rose from roughly 37% to about 40%, at a cost of $942 million, and the rise overlaps with the period hospitals were installing AI coding tools. Which hospital installed which tool is not written into claims data. The paper's own verbs stay conditional. The rise coincides with adoption, it says, and the technologies appear to be contributing. The headlines hardened that into cause.

The evidence it does put forward is well chosen. Among 2025 bowel surgery claims, the quarter of hospitals adding complex codes fastest diagnosed anemia about 38% more often than everyone else. Those same hospitals transfused their anemia patients less often, 16.9% against 19.3%. A diagnosis that arrives without the treatment it implies is the same family of test that data quality work calls a consistency rule.

The problem is how much ground that rule covers. The bowel surgery family behind the comparison accounts for 6.5% of the $942 million, and the reoperation row in the same table runs the other way. The rule was written by the party paying the bills, and it was written after the data already existed.

Studies that watched a tool go in do exist. One organization reported documented diagnoses per encounter rising from 3.0 to 4.1 after an ambient documentation tool arrived. The document with the biggest number and the document with the firmest identification are two different documents, and the press quoted the first. Medicare Advantage spent twenty years on a version of this argument, and what finally narrowed the gap was neither an audit nor a lawsuit. It was a rewrite of the payment rule.

## Facebook (EN)

Near the bottom of the bill you are handed on discharge, there is a list of condition names.

I have no memory of ever reading that list all the way down. I also did not know, until now, that every name on it carries a price.

An inpatient stay in the United States is not priced by adding up the services delivered. It is priced by which patient group the stay falls into, and what sorts the stay into one group rather than another is the set of diagnoses written alongside it. On the same bowel operation, one more line that counts as a major comorbidity can nearly triple what is paid.

A diagnosis code is the name of an illness and a price tag at the same time.

On 24 September the largest federation of health insurers in the country published three pages. Over the past two years, it said, more stays have been billed as complex, and that shift cost $942 million. The pages also noted that the rise overlaps with the period in which hospitals installed AI coding tools. The headlines went straight to AI is raising your medical bills.

Yet AI is not in those pages. What was observed is claims data, and claims data does not record which hospital installed which tool.

One scene from the paper stayed with me. The hospitals writing complex codes fastest were also the ones finding anemia at a much higher rate. And the anemia they found went to transfusion less often than anemia found elsewhere.

Same operation, but the record got sicker. That is the question being asked.

The hospitals have an answer with evidence behind it. They may simply be writing down diagnoses they used to miss. Research showing that claims capture fewer comorbidities than the chart itself has been around for a long time.

So the question I kept circling while writing was this one. "Can you tell a record getting more accurate apart from a record getting more expensive, working only from that record? And should the party paying the bill be the one to decide?"

This is close to the shape of what Pebblous does in data diagnostics every day. You take one set of records and work out where the reality ends and the trace left by the measuring begins. Two things are out of place here. The party with a stake wrote the rule, and it wrote the rule after the data already existed.

This argument has been staged once before in the United States. Medicare Advantage took twenty years, and what finally narrowed the gap was not a verdict on who was behaving badly. It was a redesign of the payment rule.

Wherever a label carries a price tag, the label moves.

There are ways to measure that movement after the fact. But if nobody wrote down what would be measured before the tools went in, the yardstick will always be one that arrived late.
