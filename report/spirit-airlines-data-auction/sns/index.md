## LinkedIn (EN)

A bankrupt airline's 100 million internal emails now carry a public price: $10 million.

Google won Spirit Airlines' corporate data at a virtual auction on August 14. The lot bundled mail and Teams records from 80,000 Microsoft 365 accounts, source repositories, payroll and timekeeping, and employee records reaching back to 1986.

What is new here is not the price but the one-page asset schedule attached to the contract. It lists system names, row counts, and an included-or-excluded ruling for every line. Customer profiles carrying names and contact details, 97.5 million of them, were excluded. Employee records were almost entirely included. The privacy design was cut to a consumer shape, and the payload is employee-shaped.

The flight attendants' union is not arguing that records can be re-identified. Its filing states plainly that it makes no such claim. Its point is that deidentification answers a different question: whether a record can be traced to a person, not whether its contents are confidential.

The court has approved nothing. The hearing is set for September 9, and the deidentification the contract requires has not begun. What Google won is a bid, not a dataset.

## Facebook (EN)

Picture a single line someone typed into Teams. A complaint about an unfair schedule, sent to a colleague back when the airline was still flying.

That line now sits inside a row of an asset schedule filed with a bankruptcy court.

Spirit Airlines' internal data went to auction, and Google won it for $10 million. One hundred million internal emails, Teams records, payroll and timekeeping, employee records going back to 1986, all in a single lot.

What kept me rereading was not the price.

On the same schedule, customer profiles carrying names and contact details were marked "not included." Employee records were almost all marked "included." The privacy machinery had been cut to a consumer shape from the start.

The flight attendants' union does not argue that anyone could be re-identified. Its filing says so outright.

What it points at is somewhere else.

"If the name is gone, is the content of a disciplinary letter gone with it?"

The contract asks the deidentification agent for one thing in particular: to work "while preserving referential integrity across the data set." The joins have to survive for the corpus to be worth training on. And the property that creates the value is the same property the union is worried about.

Twenty-three years ago, when Enron's mail became a research corpus, employees had somewhere to send a deletion request. The distribution page still notes that some messages were removed at their request.

This contract has no such door.

The reason we stayed with this story is that one-page schedule. System names, row counts, an included-or-excluded ruling per line. It is the document an AI-readiness assessment tries to give a living company, and Spirit produced it only after it had died.

Could your organization write that page today?

The court, for now, has approved nothing.
