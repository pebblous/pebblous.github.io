미국 정부가 학습 데이터 문제를 세 칸으로 갈라 놓은 뒤, 가운데 한 칸만 변호했다.

2026년 9월 1일 뉴욕 남부지법에 스무 쪽짜리 문서가 들어갔다. 판결이 아니라 28 U.S.C. § 517에 근거한 이해관계 진술서이고, 수신처는 OpenAI를 상대로 한 저작권 소송이 모두 모인 통합 사건 전체다. 정부는 언어모델 개발을 취득·학습·출력으로 나눈다. 그리고 학습 단계의 복제가 공정이용이라는 주장에 스무 쪽을 다 쓴다. 취득 단계에 대해서는 이 문서 어디에서도 방어하지 않는다.

비워 둔 그 칸에는 이미 값이 매겨져 있다. 앤트로픽 사건에서도 학습은 공정이용으로 인정받았다. 15억 달러가 물린 자리는 섀도 라이브러리에서 책을 내려받아 온 취득 경로였다. 저작물 한 건당 약 3,000달러다.

하루 뒤 채플힐에서 채택된 G20 혁신장관 합의문에는 "학습 데이터"라는 말이 한 번도 나오지 않는다. 판단은 각 회원국의 법 절차로 돌아갔다. 유럽은 같은 질문을 제출 서류의 문제로 바꿔 놓았다. 8월 2일부터 그 서류에 벌금이 붙었고, 상한은 €1,500만과 전 세계 매출 3% 중 큰 쪽이다.

세 트랙은 결론도 판단하는 사람도 시점도 다르다. 그러면서 셋 다 같은 것을 먼저 요구한다. 어떤 저작물을 어디서 어떤 경로로 얻었는지 작품 단위로 적어 둔 기록이다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

The U.S. government split language model development into three stages, then defended exactly one of them.

The twenty pages filed in the Southern District of New York on September 1 are not a ruling. They are a Statement of Interest under 28 U.S.C. § 517, addressed to the entire consolidated docket of copyright suits against OpenAI. The filing separates acquisition, training and output, then states that it will argue only whether copying at the training stage is fair use. Acquisition is defended nowhere in the document.

That blank box already carries a price. In the Anthropic case training was held to be fair use as well; the $1.5 billion settlement attached to where the books came from, roughly $3,000 per work.

A day later, at the G20 innovation ministerial in Chapel Hill, the phrase "training data" does not appear in the consensus statement once. The question was sent back to each member's own legal processes. Europe turned the same question into a filing instead, and since August 2 that filing carries a fine capped at the greater of €15 million or 3% of global revenue.

Three tracks, three different judges, three different clocks. All three ask for the same thing first: a per-work record of what was obtained, from where, and by what route.

## Facebook (EN)

"Where did this data come from?"

If you have ever been asked that with a training set already sitting in front of you, you know the few seconds that follow. It is rarely a silence of not knowing. It is a silence of having nothing on file that could answer.

On September 1, the U.S. government filed twenty pages in the Southern District of New York, arguing that copying protected text to train a language model is fair use.

Open the document, though, and the first thing it does is divide model development into three boxes. The box where data is gathered, the box where the model is trained on it, the box where the model answers a user.

Then it defends the middle box only.

For the first box there is not one line in twenty pages.

That empty first box already has a price on it. Anthropic's $1.5 billion settlement received final approval in July, and in that case training was held to be fair use too. The money attached to where the books had come from.

A day later, the G20 statement adopted at Chapel Hill does not use the phrase "training data" even once. The question went back to each country's own legal processes. Europe turned the same question into a filing instead, and since August that filing carries a fine.

Three tracks, with different conclusions, different judges, different clocks.

And all three begin by asking the same thing.

"What was obtained, from where, and by what route?"

This is why I have stopped treating provenance as metadata and started treating it as a quality attribute. Data whose origin cannot be traced is not a blank field waiting to be filled in. It is closer to a defect.

If the same record is what every direction asks for first, is there a reason to wait for the ruling? There is really only one thing to decide now, and it is what unit that record should be kept in.
