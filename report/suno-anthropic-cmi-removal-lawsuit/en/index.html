Executive Summary

This article reads the two complaints music publisher Reservoir filed against Suno and Anthropic in the Northern District of California on 21 October 2026. Together they claim $337.78 million. What is worth looking at is not the total but the fact that the bill arrives in three separate layers. Alongside copyright infringement and circumvention of technical protection, a unit price has been attached to one further act on its own: taking the title, the songwriter, the publisher and the other copyright management information off the work. Erasing the provenance of a piece of data now carries a price tag of its own, set apart from the infringement.

The opening for these complaints was cut five weeks earlier. On 16 September the Ninth Circuit threw out a claim under the same section aimed at what Copilot generated, holding that the section reaches the act of taking the information off a copy that already carried it. In the same ruling it declined to decide whether removal during training counts, and left that question open. Reservoir took exactly that open door, in a court the ruling governs directly.

The unit price, though, is a ceiling rather than a figure. The statute sets a band of $2,500 to $25,000 a work, and the complaints wrote in the top of the band. So this article does not read the amount as a victory. It reads it back as a data question. Where in a training pipeline does the credit fall off, and which is larger, the cost of carrying it or the price of having erased it? The facts here are the amounts claimed, the work counts, the filing date and the structure of the counts, all of them on the face of the complaints. The reading is that keeping provenance is moving out of the column marked good practice and into the column that carries a number. The second half belongs to this article.

On 21 October the Bill Came in Three Layers

On 21 October 2026 the music publisher Reservoir filed two complaints in the Northern District of California. The plaintiffs are Reservoir Media Management, its UK entity and Shapiro, Bernstein & Co.; the defendants are the music generation service Suno and Anthropic. The case numbers are 3:26-cv-12305 for Suno and 5:26-cv-12308 for Anthropic. Plaintiffs' counsel is Richard Busch, who ran the 'Blurred Lines' case for the Marvin Gaye estate.

The two complaints aim at different objects. The Suno complaint identifies 1,050 musical compositions — the work that the music and the lyrics make together, not the sound recording. The Anthropic complaint identifies 853 sets of lyrics, which is to say text. The defendants produce different things, so the rights at issue differ too, and adding the two figures into "1,903 songs" mixes the units. The schedules include Joni Mitchell's 'Both Sides Now' and 'Big Yellow Taxi', Johnny Cash's 'Ring of Fire', Madonna's 'Papa Don't Preach', Miles Davis's 'So What' and Glenn Miller's 'In the Mood'.

The money does not arrive as one lump. Each complaint builds three counts on three different provisions, and for each count it multiplies the per-work statutory maximum by the number of works and writes the product down separately. The table below is those three layers. We redid the multiplication and the addition ourselves, and the results match the complaint to the last digit.

Count Provision Max per work Suno (1,050 compositions) Anthropic (853 lyrics)
Direct copyright infringement 17 U.S.C. §504(c) $150,000 $157,500,000 $127,950,000
Circumvention of technical protection 17 U.S.C. §1201 $2,500 $2,625,000 $2,132,500
Removal or alteration of copyright management information 17 U.S.C. §1202 $25,000 $26,250,000 $21,325,000
Total per defendant — $177,500 $186,375,000 $151,407,500

How the two Reservoir complaints are costed. The Suno complaint states the three amounts in its damages paragraphs (¶83, ¶88 and ¶94); the Anthropic complaint's PDF is behind a wall, so the figures there are the ones four independent outlets reported, matching each other down to the dollar. The two defendants together are being asked for $337,782,500.

The row this article is here for is not the middle one. It is the second from the bottom, in orange. Pull out the money attached to the single act of taking the copyright management information off, and it is $26.25 million from Suno and $21.33 million from Anthropic, roughly $47.58 million together. That figure is charged for erasing the label the work carried, independently of whether the work was used without permission.

Two amounts get mixed up here constantly. Summaries that went around right after the filing paired the "$25,000 a song" unit price with "about $28.9 million from Suno," and those two do not belong in the same equation. The $28.9 million is the whole DMCA figure, access-control circumvention and information removal added together; removal on its own is $26.25 million. Put an equals sign between them and the work count stops reconciling when a reader divides back out.

$28,875,000 ÷ $25,000 = 1,155 works — which does not match the 1,050 compositions the complaint identifies
$26,250,000 ÷ $25,000 = 1,050 works — this is the information-removal claim on its own
$2,625,000 (circumvention) + $26,250,000 (information removal) = $28,875,000 (DMCA total)

Dividing the two amounts back out shows which one the "$25,000 a song" unit price belongs to. From here on this article uses only the information-removal figure.

Building a claim by multiplying the statutory ceiling by a work count is not something these complaints invented. Nine months earlier, on 29 January 2026, music publishers including Concord Music Group and Universal Music sued the same Anthropic over more than 20,000 scores, lyrics and compositions, seeking more than $3 billion. Divide the two reported figures and you land around $150,000 a work, which is the ceiling the infringement provision sets. What is new in the Reservoir complaints is not the multiplication but running it separately against three provisions, which is what makes the information-removal layer visible as a number.

The scale bears pointing out as well. Reservoir holds more than 150,000 publishing copyrights and 36,000 master recordings. These two complaints identify 1,050 compositions and 853 sets of lyrics; set aside the unit mismatch and any overlap, add them anyway, and you are under 1.3% of the catalog. This is not a suit claiming the whole catalog was infringed. It is a suit that pulled out only what can be proved, and why that choice matters comes back in section 4.

One caveat covers everything below. This is the pleading stage, and no court has found a single fact. The filing date, the court, the case numbers, the amounts claimed and the work counts are on the face of the complaints and can be stated flatly, but whether there was infringement, and whether anyone knowingly took the information off, is something nobody has decided. Where this article carries over an allegation from the complaints, it says so.

Section 1202 Asks Who Made It, Not Who Used It

Section 1202 of the US Copyright Act bars anyone from casually removing or altering the tag attached to a work. The copyright management information the statute describes covers the title, the name of the author, the copyright owner, the terms of use and the identifying number assigned to the work. Translated into music, that is the song title, the composer and lyricist names, the publisher, and the international standard numbers assigned separately to the recording and to the composition.

The question this provision asks is not the question the infringement provision asks. Infringement asks whether the work was used without permission. Section 1202 asks whether the record of who made it was erased. That is why the two claims sit side by side in one complaint and get costed separately. The table below puts the two questions next to each other.

Item Infringement claim Information-removal claim
The question it asks Was the work copied or used without permission? Was the label the work carried taken off?
Provision 17 U.S.C. §106 · §504(c) 17 U.S.C. §1202 · §1203(c)(3)(B)
Statutory damages per work Up to $150,000 $2,500 – $25,000
State of mind Willfulness raises the ceiling Acting knowingly is the element itself
What the money counts One work infringed One violation of removing the information

The two claims live in one complaint but ask about different facts and are costed separately. They are not fully independent, though. Section 1202 requires that the removal was done knowing it would induce or conceal infringement, so how the infringement side is decided bears on the fate of this claim.

The element is what matters. Section 1202 reaches removal or alteration done "knowingly" or "having reasonable grounds to know." It is not a provision that punishes metadata lost by accident, which means a plaintiff cannot stop at "the tag is gone" and has to plead circumstances supporting "they took it off knowing." That is why the Reservoir complaints spend so long on the details of the collection process, in a place that has nothing to do with the fair use fight.

The complaints plead three strands of such circumstances. Everything in this paragraph is an allegation no court has ruled on. First, the complaints assert that leaked internal Suno source code still contains workflows for scraping YouTube Music and the lyrics site Genius. Second, that commercial proxy services and CAPTCHA-defeating tools were used to get past gated sections. Third, that tracks were run through stem separation — splitting a recording into components such as vocals and backing — and that covers were trained alongside originals. None of the three is about what the model learned. All three are about how the material was taken and what got dropped on the way.

The defendants' side is recorded here as well. Anthropic issued a statement the day after the filing.

"We disagree with the allegations and intend to defend this case vigorously." Source: Anthropic spokesperson, quoted in Billboard, around 22 October 2026

Suno did not respond to requests for comment from several outlets. On the plaintiffs' side, Reservoir chief executive Golnar Khosrowshahi framed the filings as support for technological progress coupled with a commitment to keep protecting the industry's core creative work. Both sides are quoted here for a simple reason. What this article is trying to read is not who wins, but which item got a price attached to it.

Where in the Pipeline the Credit Falls Off

Put the conduct the complaints describe into data terms and it narrows to one question. At which point in a training pipeline does the credit fall off? Walking through music preprocessing stage by stage to answer that produces an uncomfortable picture. It is less that the pipeline loses the metadata than that it never accepts it in the first place.

Where provenance breaks in a music training pipeline. It is not that each stage throws the metadata away; it is that the next stage has no vessel to receive it in.

Start with decoding. The moment a compressed track is unpacked into an array of numbers by a tool like ffmpeg, the tags riding on the file container have nowhere in the output to go. What the model eats is a waveform, not a file. Nobody erased anything; the format changed.

Stem separation cuts the thread once more. Demucs, the widely used source separation model, takes a single stereo mix and returns four files: drums, bass, vocals and other. The only information that travels with them is the filename. What the original track was, who wrote it, on what terms it can be used — none of that is written anywhere. Once the song is no longer a unit, there is no tag left to take off.

There is one more symptom of how far this field sits from metadata. Demucs's own training data was labeled by filename heuristics. A track called 'Lead Guitar.wav' goes into the 'other' bucket, and the ambiguous cases were settled by a person listening to them. With no structured metadata available, file names had to do the job.

So the conclusion of this section is not that the stages which improve quality shave off provenance. More precisely: the pipeline was never designed with a place to carry provenance in. Preserving it means lifting a separate record out at each stage and moving it along with the data — keying off a hash of the waveform, say, and writing down the original track number, the rights holder, the terms of use and the parameters the separation was run with. Without that design, provenance disappears with nobody acting in bad faith.

And here a counterexample turns up, from inside the same industry. Stable Audio 3 preserves the metadata it receives from AudioSparx and Freesound and builds its training prompts out of it. Fields such as description, title, keywords, beats per minute, genre, mood and instrumentation are sampled at random and strung together into a text condition. In that design the metadata is not a cost but the signal that makes the model useful. Build it a place to ride along and performance can go up.

That counterexample carries legal weight. The mainstream proposal in the law review literature on applying section 1202 to generative AI is that liability should not extend to cases where keeping the information is technically impossible or unreasonably burdensome. For that proposal to persuade, "keeping it was impossible" has to be true. In music, another model in the same industry carries the metadata and trains on it. If cutting the thread was a design choice rather than a limit of the technology, it starts to meet the "knowingly" element that section 1202 requires. Nowhere else in this article do the data question and the legal question sit this close together.

4

Music Was Already Data That Arrived With Labels

This is the point where it becomes tempting to blame cost for the broken chain. If attaching a label and carrying it all the way through were expensive, it would be hard to fault whoever dropped it. But music happens to be the data where that excuse works least well. The industry has run permanent per-work identifier schemes for decades, and the cost of issuing one more number rounds to zero.

Identifier scheme What it attaches to Registration cost Marginal cost per code
ISRC Sound recording (one released track) $95 once for a registrant code (for life, up to 100,000 a year) Under one cent
ISRC (via a distributor) Sound recording Usually provided free 0
ISWC Composition (music and lyrics as one work) Free, bundled with collecting society registration 0

What it costs to issue a music identifier. The price of attaching one is near zero, and the markings still vanished from the training data. The expense is not in issuing the number but in designing a process that preserves and hands it on across every stage of the pipeline.

A complication particular to music sits on top of that. Rights in a single song come in three layers. The composition that the music and lyrics make together, the lyrics as text, and the recording of one particular performance can each belong to a different owner. That structure is why one Reservoir complaint identifies 1,050 compositions and the other 853 sets of lyrics. Suno produces sound and Anthropic produces text, so the layer each one targets differs. Tracking provenance in music means tracking three layers separately, which is why preserving only one of them still leaves the other two cut.

If the markings vanished even from data where identifiers are nearly free, there is no need to ask about data with no identifier scheme at all. Text and images scraped off the web carry no work number, no rights-holder registry and no terms-of-use field. What happened in music should be assumed to be happening, more severely, across the much larger territory outside it.

Work to fill that gap outside music has already started, though. On the image and video side there is a standard that signs provenance information into the file at the moment of capture or creation, and it is genuinely in use. More than 6,000 companies and institutions have joined the Content Authenticity Initiative behind it, TikTok has applied the label to more than a billion videos, and OpenAI applies it to everything its image generators produce. Sony, Canon, Nikon and Leica have built in-camera signing at the point of capture. Against that, this article's research turned up no case of the same standard being applied to music or audio. The medium that has run per-work numbering longest stands furthest back in the move to embed provenance into what gets generated.

4.1Strip the Tags and the Training Use Still Shows

Read this far and the conclusion tilts toward resignation. The tags fell off and only waveforms remain, so what is there left to prove? A study published in May 2026 overturns that premise. Without looking at model parameters or training logs, it decides by querying alone whether a given track was used in training. Across several recent music generation models it reports accuracy as high as 98.6%, a 1.9% rate of wrongly flagging unused tracks, and a 1.0% rate of missing used ones.

One thing must not get mixed in while reading those numbers. What the method measures is whether the track was used in training, not how closely the model reproduces it. Figures measuring memorization and reproduction are something this article could not obtain, so it does not fold the two questions into one.

It does answer the question deferred in section 1. Why did Reservoir identify under 1.3% of the 150,000 works it holds? Because this is not a suit over everything but a sample suit over what can be proved. Lay those two facts over each other and the section's conclusion appears. Provenance does not disappear because someone erased it. The cost of reconstructing it merely shifts from the party that erased it to the party it was taken from, and that cost is falling fast.

5

The Cost of Keeping It Against the Price of Erasing It

The two sides can now be set next to each other. On one side is the cost of attaching provenance and carrying it all the way through. On the other is the price attached when that is not done. One caution applies to the right-hand side. The $25,000 a work written into the complaints is not a settled figure but a ceiling.

The statutory band runs from $2,500 to $25,000 a work, and Reservoir wrote in the top of it. If a court picks the floor, Suno's share is not $26.25 million but $2.625 million, a tenfold drop, and a violator who establishes it did not know can have the court reduce the award or remit it entirely. On top of that the provision itself turns on acting knowingly, so reaching the ceiling means carrying that element all the way to the end.

Amounts that have actually been realized sit an order of magnitude away. Bartz v. Anthropic, the largest AI copyright settlement disclosed to date, came to $1.5 billion over 482,460 works. Per work, that converts to somewhere between $2,200 and $3,100 depending on which stage of the calculation is used: lowest on the first distribution, higher once attorneys' fees are set aside, highest if the gross settlement is simply divided. Whichever stage is counted, it stays around 10% of the section 1202 ceiling. That case, though, was an infringement settlement in the book market rather than an information-removal case, and it is a realized settlement rather than an amount claimed. Both qualifiers travel with the figure every time.

So what has a court set per song in music? As of October 2026 that number does not exist. The Munich Regional Court did order Suno to pay damages in July of this year, but the amount was deferred to a follow-on proceeding and the case covered only six songs. Section 6 returns to that ruling. There is therefore not a single realized music figure to place on the right of the chart below, and the one realized figure available for comparison is borrowed from the book market.

The chart below puts the per-work amounts in this article on a single logarithmic scale. The scale is logarithmic because the left side and the right side are separated by orders of magnitude.

Per-work amounts on a single logarithmic scale. The left is the cost of documenting a whole bundle of datasets; the right is what gets charged against a single work.

The left side comes from an earlier Pebblous report. Auditing the license and provenance of a single public dataset takes 3.5 to 15 hours, and a program bundling five to ten datasets takes 40 to 160 hours. In money, the cheapest case is under $1,000 and the most expensive, with legal review attached, runs to about $30,000. Set those figures beside the right side and the contrast sharpens. Documenting ten datasets end to end costs about what the information-removal ceiling comes to for one or two songs of music.

Then why was nothing written down? Because not writing it down was not a choice these two companies alone made. In an audit of 1,858 public datasets, missing license information ran above 72% and mislabeling above 50%. What is worth noticing is that the audit itself pulled the "unknown" share down from the low 70s to 30%. The information had not vanished from the world; nobody had written it down. An audit looking at audio datasets alone found 36% sourced from web scraping and 35% likely to contain material that requires a purchased license. Documentation debt is closer to the default setting than to an exception.

5.1Provenance Is Becoming a Condition Outside the Courts Too

Courts are not the only channel through which a price gets set. Contracts moved first. The deal Universal Music struck with Udio in October 2025 added, on top of artist opt-in and a walled-garden output model, an obligation on Udio to run content identification — to filter outputs substantially similar to Universal's catalog. Royalties were reported to be tied to how closely an output resembles the training data. It amounts to a contract whose accounting does not run at all unless provenance can be traced. Suno in turn reached the first major-label licensing deal, with Warner Music, in November 2025.

No per-song rate can be read out of these contracts, though. Every music generation AI licensing deal concluded in 2025 and 2026 kept its financial terms confidential. So the comparison "what would a license have cost per song" cannot be made from public material, and the impossibility of making it is itself the current state of this market. It also means the only public channel where a price gets set is still the court.

6

But Will This Claim Survive?

The claim that a price has been set needs discounting here, by this article itself. Being charged and collecting are different things, and section 1202 claims have a poor record in AI litigation so far. But stringing those dismissals into "this provision does not work against AI" misses where these complaints stand. What got dismissed was mostly claims aimed at the output. What Reservoir aims at is the training stage.

The ruling that drew that line came five weeks before the filing. On 16 September 2026 the Ninth Circuit declined to allow a section 1202 claim against output in the GitHub Copilot case. Its reasoning rests on the words of the statute: "remove" and "alter" presuppose an existing copy that already carried the information, so producing a new output without that information in it is not removal. In the same ruling, the identicality the district court had treated as an element was not recognized as an independent requirement. Identicality is merely circumstantial support for removal having occurred.

The part that matters for this case is the part the ruling did not reach. It did not decide the input-side theory — the claim that the information was taken off during training — because the plaintiffs there did not press it to the end. The opinion went a step further and indicated that a claim asserting the stored copies had been served back without the information could have been the stronger one. Thirty-five days later Reservoir walked through the door left open, in the very court the ruling binds.

The table below sorts the public record of section 1202 claims by what each one aimed at. The empty cell at the far right is the message of the table.

Case Court and date What it aimed at Outcome
Raw Story · AlterNet v. OpenAI S.D.N.Y., November 2024 Output Dismissed — on standing, removal without dissemination is not a concrete injury
Doe 1 v. GitHub Ninth Circuit, 16 September 2026 Output Dismissed — if it was not taken off an existing copy, it is not "removal"
NYT · Daily News · CIR v. OpenAI and Microsoft S.D.N.Y., March 2025 Output Split by plaintiff — only the Daily News and CIR claims survived; the Times's own claim was dismissed
Seven section 1202 claims against large language models (academic survey) As of October 2025 Mostly output In all seven, at least one section 1202 claim was dismissed
Reservoir v. Suno · Reservoir v. Anthropic N.D. Cal., 21 October 2026 Training stage (input) Before any response

The leading cases in which a section 1202 claim has been litigated. Dismissals dominate, but the places where they landed are mostly on the output side, and no US court has yet ruled on a section 1202 claim that squarely contests the training stage.

One case sits outside the table. It is not a US matter, so it is not listed, but a ruling three months ago in Germany covered the same defendant and the same collection method. On 31 July 2026 the 42nd Civil Chamber of the Munich Regional Court found Suno largely liable in a case the German collecting society GEMA brought over six songs. Two findings overlap with this one. The court treated the copying that happens during training as an infringement of the reproduction right and held that the text and data mining exception does not stretch that far, and it found that Suno circumvented stream protection measures when pulling audio from YouTube. Conduct that the Reservoir complaints plead as allegation has already been written down as fact by a court in another country.

That ruling should still not be read as a trailer for this case. The law applied was German copyright law, and removal of copyright management information was not at issue there. The judgment is not final, and Suno said it is considering an appeal, calling the decision a fundamental misunderstanding of its technology. Damages were ordered but the amount was pushed into a follow-on proceeding. So, as section 5 put it, no court has yet set a per-song figure in music generation AI.

A fact the table does not show belongs here too. US AI copyright suits had piled up to somewhere around 150 by October 2026, and eight new ones arrived in the week of this filing, the most in any single week — but nobody has published a count of how many of them include a section 1202 claim. So this article uses no percentage. What can be confirmed is that section 1202 claims against large language models numbered at least seven as of October 2025, and that federal court opinions citing the provision between 2020 and 2024 came to 330, more than 1.5 times the preceding five years. How often the provision gets litigated is going up.

Rather than predict how this case ends, then, it is more accurate to put it this way. The door Reservoir walked through has never been closed, and nobody has ever passed all the way through it either. This case is the next test of whether section 1202 reaches training data, and until there is a result, $25,000 a work stays a ceiling on negotiating leverage rather than an award. For the record, the Elvis Presley estate filed a separate suit against Anthropic over 274 songs in the same week; it is built on different issues, so this article does not take it further.

7

Why Pebblous Is Watching

There is a reason Pebblous spent this long on two complaints filed in a US court. What happens across the preceding six sections is not a product of circumstances peculiar to music. It takes the same shape anywhere data is gathered and fed to a model.

7.1An Item of Good Practice Just Got a Number

The definition of AI-Ready Data that Pebblous has been using always included "provenance can be traced." Until now that was a recommendation, not a line item with money on it. These complaints attached a band of $2,500 to $25,000 a work to it. Just as DataClinic counts missing values, duplicates and label errors when it diagnoses a dataset, there is now a basis for making the presence of a provenance field, and whether it survives to the end, diagnostic items of their own. For products that generate data and ship it out, embedding provenance in what gets shipped moves from an optional feature toward a delivery requirement.

7.2Quality Metrics and Provenance Metrics Run on Different Axes

Copyright management information is metadata, not content. The accuracy, completeness and consistency that international standards measure are all metrics on the content axis, and "has the record of where this data came from been preserved" lies outside that axis. As section 3 showed, the heart of the problem is not that the pipeline discards provenance but that it was never given a place to carry it. Decode and the tags fly off; split the stems and the song stops being a unit. Preserving provenance is therefore only possible by designing each stage to lift a record out and move it alongside, which is work on a different axis from raising a quality score. This is exactly the place to say that a provenance metric has to be stood up next to the quality metrics.

7.3Neither the Buyer Nor the Supplier Knows

Contracts for buying and selling training data rarely contain a clause saying provenance metadata will be preserved and handed over with the data. The receiving side takes delivery of data with a broken chain and does not know it; the supplying side drops the chain in preprocessing and does not know that counts as a defect. The 98.6% from section 4 adds urgency. "We erased it, so nobody will know" no longer holds. Here is what can be picked up and used now.

  • When bringing in a dataset, ask whether the original identifier, the rights holder and the terms of use survive at the record level. "There is provenance" and "it is attached to every record" are different answers.
  • Put a clause in the supply contract requiring identifying information to be preserved and passed on after preprocessing. Where a stage makes preservation impossible, get that stage and the reason in writing.
  • Stand up a provenance field in the AI bill of materials, not just the model and the dataset. Record which transforms ran and what dropped at which stage, and the question can be answered afterward.
  • For a pipeline already in production, measure once what disappears on either side of the decoding and splitting stages. Usually it was not recorded rather than impossible to record.

7.4The Point Where a Norm Turns Into a Price

Several articles on data provenance have accumulated on the Pebblous blog: Suno's Copyright Case Is Auditing the Provenance of 60,000 Training Songs, Music Publishers Ask a Court to Order an Accounting of Claude's Training Data, 21 Million Songs Trained AI — and a Newsroom Named the Source, and The Real Invoice for Free Data. This one is the next in that line and also shifts what the line is doing. The earlier pieces argued that provenance ought to be traceable. This one reports that somebody has calculated what erasing provenance is worth, filed the bill in court, and done so through a door that opened five weeks earlier. Getting to the point where a norm turns into a price before anyone else does is what this article is for.
