Physicists Re-Graded 250 Wrong Answers on Physics Benchmarks. Twelve Were the Model's Fault.

A model was asked how long an electron takes to travel a given path, and offered three choices: 330 nanoseconds, 66, or 33. It picked none of them. "It cannot be determined from the information given," it wrote. "No option can be uniquely selected." The grader recorded the response as wrong.

A physics doctoral student who later reopened that item left a one-line note: the problem does not give enough information. The model had diagnosed the defect in the question precisely, and lost the point for it.

That scene comes from an audit posted to arXiv on September 11, 2026 by a team of 51 authors based mainly at Yale. They opened six widely used physics benchmarks, pulled out responses the graders had recorded as wrong, and had human reviewers re-grade them one at a time.

Three ways to be wrong

The useful part of the method is not the headline arrows. It is the taxonomy. For every wrong answer, reviewers assigned blame to exactly one of three places.

  • Model error — the question is well posed, the reference answer is right, and the model got it wrong. The paper's phrasing: this alone is a genuine model failure.
  • Grader error — question and answer key are fine, the model answered correctly, and the automatic grader marked it wrong anyway.
  • Item defect — the problem statement or the reference answer is itself broken. Wrong keys, contradictory conditions, ambiguous wording, missing assumptions.

Of the 250 responses re-graded, 143 were item defects and 95 were grader errors. Twelve were cases where the model actually got the physics wrong.

Read the denominator with that. The 250 are responses already marked wrong, not a random slice of the benchmarks, and the sample was deliberately narrowed to items a model had missed on every attempt. Nobody measured that 95% of physics questions are broken. What was measured is that when someone reopens the responses a benchmark filed as failures, most of them turn out not to be failures of physics reasoning.

The failures are concrete and small

One model wrote T = P/(3√6) where the key said T = (√6/18)P. Rationalize the denominator and those are the same expression. Score: zero, including on the rubric designed to award partial credit. Another wrote F = 5mRω² against a key reading 5mω²R, the same scalar product with the factors in a different order. Also zero.

Elsewhere the key itself was wrong. A reference solution set up the algebra correctly and then botched the final arithmetic, printing −√2 where the answer is 2−√2. The model had written 2−√2. In a condensed-matter item, the Hamiltonian was never given, leaving it undefined whether a coupling constant attached to Pauli matrices or spin operators; the two conventions differ by a factor of four, so the ground-state energy has no unique value.

Every benchmark said its keys were checked

We went out to the original papers behind all six benchmarks to see how their authors described their own validation. All six say they validated. Expert-designed, unambiguous, machine-verifiable, rigorously screened.

Humanity's Last Exam is the sharpest case. Its abstract promises answers that are "unambiguous and easily verifiable." Its own review section discloses that reviewers "were not asked to verify the entire correctness of the provided solution if it took over five minutes," and closes by welcoming community feedback. The limitation was published. Nobody read it as a warning. This audit is less an exposé than the first reply to that invitation.

Isolate the graders and the ranking inverts. The two benchmarks that advertised the most rigorous automatic marking posted the highest grader-error rates in the audit. The pipeline that made no claim about marking accuracy posted the lowest, at 4.08% — still one wrong call in twenty-five, on a figure nobody had been measuring at all.

Which arrow to quote

Correcting the keys raised scores sharply, and this study will mostly be cited as an arrow. But each arrow bundles three different operations: repairing keys, re-grading responses, and dropping items that could not be repaired. The paper does not decompose them.

The most quotable jump runs from 47.3% to 78.7% — on a benchmark that also shed 86 of its 202 items, and the dropped items were the ones a model had failed four times running. The cleanest comparison is elsewhere. CMT-Benchmark was audited in full, 30 of its 50 items were found defective, 29 were repaired and exactly one was dropped. The score moved from 61.0% to 87.2%. That is the one arrow where "they deleted the hard questions" cannot be the explanation.

The floor under every score

The finding that outlives the numbers sits in the discussion section. Every benchmark carries some share of defective items, and on those items a model is recorded as wrong whatever it answers. So no measured error rate can fall below the defect rate of the test. While models are weak, that floor hides inside their real mistakes. Once a model's true error rate drops below the defect rate, most of what a scoreboard records as model error belongs to the benchmark instead.

The audit is candid about its own weak joints, in an appendix. Reviewers disagreed on 28.57% of the items two people saw, and the disagreements that mattered most sat exactly on the boundary between "the test is wrong" and "the model is wrong." Some items were seen by one reviewer only, for lack of staff. And every reviewer worked with an AI-generated preliminary review on screen — a design whose bias risk is documented in the annotation literature and unmeasured here.

Two more things belong in the reading. This is an unreviewed preprint. And its saturation claim is scoped to closed-form textbook problems: the same team attached a research-agent configuration to several open problems in theoretical physics and solved none of them.
