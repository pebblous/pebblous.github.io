## LinkedIn (KO)

어느 나라인지 말해 주지 않고 예절을 물었더니, 프런티어 모델 넷이 모두 미국에 가까운 답을 냈다.

스톡홀름 미래연구소의 키모 에릭손 연구팀이 9월 말에 공개한 논문의 실측이다. 정답지는 90개 사회 25,422명에게 일상 장면의 적절성을 직접 물어 둔 국제조사다. 모델에게는 "저 사회 사람들이 매긴 평균 점수가 얼마겠는가"를 맞히게 했다. 프롬프트에서 사회 이름을 지우자 네 모델의 답이 전부 그 모델 자신이 그리는 미국 쪽으로 붙었다. 미국은 네 모델 가운데 셋에서 가장 가까운 사회였고 나머지 하나에서 두 번째였다. 90개 사회를 평균한 자리보다 가깝다.

이름을 넣어 주면 답이 움직이기는 한다. 다만 사회들 사이의 실제 간격 가운데 절반이 채 살아나지 않는다. 현지 설문 언어로 물어도 구도는 거의 그대로다. 같은 아랍어 설문지를 쓴 알제리와 사우디아라비아는 네 모델 전부, 두 언어 전부에서 갈라지지 않았다.

이 실패가 까다로운 이유는 오답처럼 보이지 않기 때문이다. 한 사회 안에서 무엇이 더 부적절한지는 모델이 잘 배열하고(상관 0.83~0.87), 답의 폭도 설문보다 오히려 넓게 잡는다. 지금 쓰이는 문화 벤치마크는 정답을 맞혔나 틀렸나로 채점하니, 평균 근처로 몰아 답할수록 점수가 나빠지지 않는다.

그래서 해외 모델을 그대로 얹은 서비스에 물을 것은 어느 모델이 문화를 더 아느냐가 아니다. 우리 쪽 사람들의 답이 실제로 얼마나 벌어져 있는지를 담은 기준선이 손에 있는가다. 모델이 그 폭을 얼마나 좁혀 답하는지는 기준선이 있어야 비로소 보인다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Asked about everyday manners without being told which country, four frontier models all answered like the United States.

The measurement comes from a paper released in late September by Kimmo Eriksson's group at the Institute for Futures Studies in Stockholm. The answer key was an international survey of 25,422 people in 90 societies. Each model was asked what respondents in a named society had rated a given scene, on average. With the society's name stripped from the prompt, every model's answer sat closer to its own picture of the United States than to the average of all 90 societies.

Naming the society does move the answer. It recovers less than half of the real distance between societies. Prompting in the local survey language barely changes that. Algeria and Saudi Arabia, which took the same Arabic questionnaire, never came apart for any model in either language.

The failure is hard to catch because it does not look like error. Within a single society the models rank scenarios well, at correlations of 0.83 to 0.87, and they spread their answers wider than the survey does. Today's cultural benchmarks grade answers right or wrong, so a model that huddles near the global average loses nothing.

The question for anyone deploying a foreign model at home is therefore not which model knows culture best. It is whether a baseline exists that records how far apart your own users' answers actually sit, and whether anything checks how much of that spread the model gives back.

## Facebook (EN)

I pasted an unfinished message into a foreign AI service and asked whether it sounded rude.

The answer came back immediately. It never said whose standard it was using.

A paper released in late September pushes directly on that spot. Kimmo Eriksson's group at the Institute for Futures Studies in Stockholm had an international survey built by asking people in 90 societies directly. They made it the answer key and handed the same grid to four frontier models. The question was not "do you find this appropriate" but "what average score did people in that society give it?"

The experiment the team saved for last is the clearest one. They deleted the society from the prompt and asked again.

The answers that came back with no society named sat closest to the United States in three of the four models, and second closest in the fourth. Closer than to the average of all 90 societies.

'Etiquette from nowhere' is the name I would give it. It wears the face of belonging to no one in particular, and opening it up you find one place.

There was a pattern in what the models did get right. Behaviours people argue about out loud, like kissing in public, they track across societies reasonably well. How quietly you may chat with the person beside you in a library, they barely track at all. The first kind gets written into guidebooks and fought over in comment threads. The second kind you learn by living somewhere, and almost nobody writes it down.

The team also tested whether asking in the local language fixes things. Answers do spread out a little more. They do not spread out more accurately. Algeria and Saudi Arabia, which share an Arabic questionnaire, never separated in either language.

Pebblous records distributions per axis rather than answering for a dataset with a single score, out of much the same worry. Missing values are visible and outliers set off alarms, while a flattened value arrives looking like perfectly ordinary output.

What I actually want checked now is closer to home. "How far apart do answers here in Korea sit, and who has ever measured that?"
