## LinkedIn (KO)

기술이 일자리를 흔들면 정치는 보상을 논한다. 정치경제학이 오래 전제해 온 순서인데, 33개 나라 의회를 실제로 세어 보니 그 순서가 나타나지 않았다.

암스테르담의 연구자 두 명이 2023년 1월부터 2026년 4월까지의 본회의 발언 151만 건을 훑어 AI와 노동을 함께 다룬 5,317건을 골라내고, 그 발언들이 무엇을 하자고 말하는지 하나씩 코딩했다.

응답 프레임 언급 가운데 보상은 2.3%였고, 가장 큰 칸인 촉진·투자가 55.2%였다.

의회의 대립축은 피해를 어떻게 갚을지가 아니라 이 기술을 밀어줄지 붙잡을지에 놓여 있었다. 규제 쪽 칸을 열어 봐도 가장 큰 것은 일터의 알고리즘 관리가 아니라 저작권과 창작 산업 보호다. 데이터를 만든 사람을 말할 때 정치가 쥐고 있는 것은 소유권의 언어이고, 대가의 언어는 아직 쥐고 있지 않다.

두 가지 단서를 함께 읽어야 한다. 각 칸의 내부 구성은 논문이 통계 모형 밖에 둔 탐색적 분석이라 방향까지만 읽는 게 맞고, 저자들도 이 증거로 대의 격차를 확정할 수는 없다고 못박았다. 촉진이 크다는 사실도 의회가 산업계 편에 섰다는 뜻은 아니다. 그 칸의 상당 부분은 국가와 공공부문의 역량을 키우자는 이야기다.

데이터를 사고 만드는 조직에 이 자료가 주는 것은 규제 전망이 아니라 일정이다. 제도가 대가 규칙을 써 줄 때까지 기다리는 계획을 세울 근거가 여기에는 없다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Political economy has long assumed that when technology unsettles work, politics turns to compensation. A count across 33 national parliaments does not find that sequence.

Two Amsterdam researchers read 1.51 million floor speeches delivered between January 2023 and April 2026 and drew out the 5,317 that tied AI to work, coding what each one proposed doing about it.

Compensation took 2.3% of response-frame mentions; enablement and investment, the largest category, took 55.2%.

The dividing line is not how to repair the damage but whether to speed the technology up or hold it back. Open the regulation category and the biggest topic inside it is copyright and creative-industry protection, not algorithmic management in the workplace. When politics speaks about the people who made the data, it holds the language of ownership and not the language of payment.

Two limits belong with that reading. The internal composition of each category comes from an exploratory clustering the authors deliberately keep out of their statistical models, so it carries direction rather than precision, and they state plainly that this evidence cannot establish a representation gap. A large enablement share also reflects state and public-sector capacity as much as support for private firms.

For anyone designing a data supply chain, the finding is about timing rather than regulatory forecast. Nothing here supports a plan that waits for legislatures to write the rules on paying for data.

## Facebook (EN)

Somewhere right now, a person is drawing boxes around objects in photographs and tagging sentences so that a model can learn from them.

Ask where that person's share gets decided, and there is no obvious room to point to.

Two researchers in Amsterdam went through three years and four months of floor debate in 33 national parliaments.

They pulled out the 5,317 speeches that connected AI to work, and counted what each one asked the state to do.

Compensating people for what they lose came to 2.3% of the mentions. The largest category was the one urging adoption forward.

Open the regulation category and it gets stranger.

The biggest topic inside it is not algorithmic management on the shop floor. It is copyright.

When politics talks about the people who made the data, it is holding the language of ownership. It does not yet hold the language of payment.

"'Whose is it' can be answered somewhere. Where does 'how much is owed' get answered?"

There is a country that has in fact written wage rules for data annotation and content moderation work. Kenya.

But those rules sit in a ministry policy paper rather than in parliament, which is exactly the ground this study's instrument does not reach.

That is close to why provenance matters to us.

What carries no price tends to leave no record, and what leaves no record cannot be priced later.

When the rules finally arrive, the first thing they will ask for is the record of who put in what. The timing is unknowable from this data. That part is not.
