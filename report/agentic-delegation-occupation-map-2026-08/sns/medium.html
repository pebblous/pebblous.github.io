What 53,000 Agent Configuration Files Say About Who Actually Delegates to AI

Almost every published measure of AI's reach into work answers the same question in a different way. Felten and colleagues asked what the technology can do in principle. Eloundou and colleagues asked which of those capabilities became a usable product. Anthropic's economic research counted what shows up in actual conversations. Capability, availability, observed use: three layers, one underlying question. How far does AI reach into this occupation's work?

A preprint from KAIST, posted to arXiv on 19 August 2026, proposes a fourth question. Each existing measure, the authors write, captures how far AI could or does touch an occupation's work, not whether a worker has committed the work to it.

Why a configuration file and not a chat log

The obvious way to see what people hand over is to read the logs. The authors argue this does not work. A conversation session does not map cleanly onto a task; one session holds several jobs and one job scatters across several sessions. And a prompt carries no commitment. A single question may be an experiment never repeated.

An agent configuration file is different on four counts. A user wrote it. It describes the work at roughly the level of abstraction of an occupational task statement. It costs effort to produce. And once written, it runs again every time it is invoked. The last two are what matter: effort means nobody writes one casually, and repetition means it is not a trial.

After deduplication and filtering, 53,515 specifications from open repositories entered the analysis. Each was embedded and compared against 17,951 O*NET task statements, all against all, and the similarities were averaged per task, then weighted by O*NET importance into a per-occupation score.

That construction has a consequence worth stating, because it is the easiest thing to get wrong here. The index is a mean similarity, not a count and not a proportion. Values run from 0.041 to 0.192 across 748 occupations, because a single perfectly matched agent disappears into the average of 53,000 unrelated specifications. The authors say plainly that the measure is designed for comparison across occupations rather than interpretation on an absolute scale. An index of 0.19 does not mean 19% of the job is automated.

The new map does not overlap the old one

Split the 748 occupations at the median, compare the upper half against Frey and Osborne's 2017 high-risk list, and the Jaccard overlap is 0.05. That number needs a reference point. By our own calculation, two lists drawn independently, one of them exactly half the population, would land between 0.23 and 0.33. The measured value sits more than four times below the bottom of that range. The two maps do not merely disagree; they overlap less than random tagging would produce.

The rankings show why. Delegation concentrates in management analysts (0.192), technical writers (0.182), natural sciences managers and computer programmers. It bottoms out in oral and maxillofacial surgeons, paperhangers and automotive glass installers. The 2017 list was built around repetitive physical work; what agents are reaching into now is analysis, documentation and coordination. The same risk band held both kinds of job, so the orderings came apart.

Availability beats wages and degrees

The paper runs three employment-weighted regressions. Wages and education alone explain 34.9% of the variation in delegation. A single variable, whether tools already reach that work, explains 57.8% on its own. Everything together reaches 62.1%.

This cuts against a settled expectation in the economics of technology. General-purpose technologies are supposed to pay off only after firms reorganise processes and retrain people, which is why use normally lags capability. Here availability alone carried more than half. The authors suggest why: agents are configured in natural language, so the distance between noticing that a task could be automated and building the thing is short. The files they studied were written by individuals, with no procurement, no integration project and no specialist intermediary.

The gap at the top that does not close

Delegation does not rise monotonically with pay or schooling. The curve peaks around bachelor's-degree work and falls on both sides. What the regressions do is separate the two falls.

At the lower end, the gap for high-school-or-less occupations shrinks from −0.011 to −0.002 and loses statistical significance once availability enters the model. The explanation is complete: the tools do not reach that work yet. At the upper end, the gap for master's-and-above occupations goes from −0.026 to −0.018 and stays. The authors note that what makes this coefficient notable is not its contribution to fit but its persistence. Against an index range of 0.151, a shortfall of 0.018 is roughly 12% of the full spread.

On why, the paper deliberately stops. These occupations may be unable to specify their work in advance, or they may be able to and decline, since professionals have both the autonomy and the incentive to control the pace at which their work is codified. The authors state that their data cannot distinguish constraint from choice. They do offer a test for later: if the shortfall narrows as agents improve, constraint was the story; if it holds while capability climbs, choice looks stronger.

Independent surveys point the same way without settling it. Thomson Reuters found enterprise-wide AI adoption in professional services nearly doubling to 40% in a year, while organisations that had actually deployed agentic tools stood at 15%. Professionals use AI. They have not handed the work over.

Specifiability is another name for AI-ready

Pebblous works on the state data is in before a model can use it, and this paper reads like the same proposition restated on the labour side. A model can only work on material that has become data. An agent can only take on work that has become a specification.

An agent configuration is, in practice, a schema for a job. It has a goal, a procedure, designated tools and inputs, and a trigger. Being able to write one means the work already has structure. Being unable to write one is not a verdict about difficulty. It says the work has not yet taken a form anything else can act on. David Autor's 2015 answer to Polanyi's paradox was to regularise the environment so that inflexible machines could operate semi-autonomously; writing a specification is that same move, performed in software.

Which reframes what the map shows. Not a chart of jobs AI has consumed, but a line between work that has already become data and work that has not. On that reading, the unexplained hole at the top is not a safe zone. It is the next thing to be described well enough to be handed over.
