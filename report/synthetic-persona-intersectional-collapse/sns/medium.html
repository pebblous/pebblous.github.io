Give a Synthetic Respondent Two Identities and It Will Use One

Hand a large language model the profile of a 45-year-old Black woman and ask it to answer a survey question. What comes back mostly reflects one of those attributes. Not a blend, not a weighted compromise. One.

Seating a model in a respondent's chair instead of recruiting a person is a shipping product, sold under the name silicon sampling since Argyle and colleagues named the practice in 2023. Vendors sell it on specificity and price: describe your target participant in as much detail as you need, get stable trends for rare groups, pay $2 to $60 for what a fieldwork agency charges $100 and up. The first job it usually gets is the one that pitch implies, which is filling in cheaply the thin demographic segments a real study would have had to cut.

A paper posted to arXiv on 24 August 2026 by Rennard and Xypolopoulos measured what actually happens in those cells. The setup ran eight models from five organizations against 15 waves of the Pew Research Center's American Trends Panel, 21.1 million simulated responses in total.

How you measure whether an identity was used

The method needs no mathematics to follow. Give a model a profile carrying one identity, say "woman," and measure how far its answer distribution shifts from the overall average. That shift is the fingerprint the identity leaves. Now give it a two-identity profile and predict the observed shift two ways: once by adding both fingerprints, once by using only one. Count which prediction wins, cell by cell, where a cell is one profile meeting one question.

For the representative model, the single-attribute prediction won 78.4% of two-identity cells. Across all eight models the range was 75.3% to 81.2%. That figure is a share of profile-by-question cells, not a share of subgroups, and the distinction matters when the number gets quoted.

The number needs a scale before it means anything

Read 78% on its own and the natural inference is that the other 22% was fine. It was not. This kind of win rate has a chance level, and the chance level is not half. The authors simulated both extremes to build a ruler. In a world where the two identities add perfectly, the single-attribute prediction still wins 40.3% of the time. In a world where only one identity is ever used, it wins 84.3%. Rescale the observations between those endpoints and the eight models land at 0.83 to 0.95 on the way to total collapse.

The same discipline applies elsewhere in the paper, and it is the part most worth stealing. Pair the bias fingerprints of two axes that have nothing to do with each other and the median cosine similarity is already 0.84. On that scale, the additive prediction scores 0.94. Without knowing where the floor is, 0.94 is not a good score. It is not a score at all.

People do the addition

The obvious defence is that combining two identities is simply hard, and people would not do it cleanly either. The authors closed that door by running the identical rule on the real respondents.

Humans reach about 90% of the ceiling this measure allows, against 31% for a representative model. Expressed as an identity budget, where 1.0 means one identity fully spent, real respondents spend 1.83 and the models spend 0.98. Opinions genuinely do sharpen as identities overlap, by roughly 2.5 times from one identity to three. But the surplus beyond simple addition measures −0.0008, which is to say zero. The sharpening is arithmetic, not emergence.

That result is worse for the models, not better. Had real people shown deep interaction effects, model failure could be defended as failure at something people cannot do either. Instead the models were handed the easiest form of combination that exists, adding two slopes, and slipped on it.

The dropped axes are the same two

Which identity survives is close to a coin flip, but the residue tilts one way. Gender survives 12.5 percentage points more often than its weight in the human data warrants, and income 3.0 points more. Religion survives 5.0 points less, and race 9.5 points less. Race was under-preserved significantly in all six prior-generation models where race combinations appeared often enough to test, across all 15 survey topics, and the gap was widest in the waves that were about race.

The tempting explanation is alignment training teaching models to tread carefully around sensitive attributes. On open weights that is testable, and it fails. Split Llama-3.1-8B into pretrained, instruction-tuned, and chat-templated conditions and the race deficit is −10.8 points in the pretrained weights, with instruction tuning contributing +0.6 and the chat template 0.0. The property was there before any post-training touched it.

Not a prompting problem, and not a next-generation problem

Instructing the model explicitly to integrate both identities moved the collapse rate from 79.4% to 79.5%. Chain-of-thought moved it to 79.0%. One model went the wrong way: Claude Haiku 4.5 rose from 79.7% to 85.5% once told to reason step by step, using fewer identities the more carefully it was asked to think.

Nor does the next model fix it. GPT-5.5 cut prediction error by 34 to 38 percent against GPT-4o-mini while its collapse rate stayed at 79.1%. Getting more accurate and using more of the identity turn out to be different capabilities. One improves with scale; the other does not follow.

What this leaves for anyone auditing synthetic data

Pebblous works on the state data is in before it reaches a model, so the useful output here is not the collapse rate but the procedure built to measure it, which the paper proposes as an audit standard: a noise floor, null calibration, and split-half confirmation. None of those are survey-specific. They are the work of deciding what a number has to be compared against before it means anything, which is what any tool that attaches a quality verdict to a dataset has to do.

The gap they expose is a documentation gap as much as a technical one. AAPOR's May 2026 task force report classifies synthetic respondents as the riskiest use of AI in survey research, warns specifically about filling sparse demographic clusters, and never uses the word intersectional across roughly 310,000 characters. Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission guide caps utility validation at one-dimensional distributions and two-dimensional pairwise correlation. Generative diversity metrics measure whether sparse regions of an embedding space were covered, without knowing which demographic combination any region corresponds to. Each stops at the layer where this failure begins.

So the checklist item writes itself. Beyond per-axis distribution match, ask for combination-cell comparison against an external reference, and ask what the identity budget was: of the attributes you conditioned on, how many actually reached the output. A dataset that reports 1.0 where people report 1.83 has told you something no marginal distribution ever will.
