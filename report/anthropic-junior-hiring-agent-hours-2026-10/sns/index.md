저자들이 자기 한계를 지우지 않았다는 점은 따로 적어 둘 만합니다. 도구 채택이 무작위가 아니라는 점, 채택자들이 채택 전부터 이미 가파른 상승세였다는 점, 코드가 늘었다고 더 좋은 코드는 아니라는 점이 모두 본문에 있습니다. 권고 끝에는 이런 문장이 붙어 있습니다. 이미 안에 있는 사람을 지키는 고용 보호는 애초에 뽑히지 않은 사람에게 아무 도움이 되지 않는다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Anthropic opened its own personnel data on 16 October and reported that the junior share of new technical hires fell from 27% to 9%. Nowhere in the document is there a count of how many juniors that is.

The publisher is the Anthropic Institute and the lead author is Nathan Wilmers, who teaches work and organizations at MIT Sloan. The team pulled internal hiring records and AI usage logs and added seventeen staff interviews. It is rare for a company to open its own personnel ledger and its agent logs inside one document.

A share has a numerator and a denominator that move together. The same report states that the company tripled in size over the past year. That the distinction is not a quibble is something the report demonstrates itself: the technical share of total staff fell from 61% to 56%, and the very next sentence explains that technical hiring kept growing in absolute numbers while other functions grew faster and pushed the share down.

What fills the gap on the junior side is not a headcount ledger but an April internal survey. About a third of machine learning managers and a fifth of software engineering managers said they hired less because of AI. That is evidence of a different kind. A ledger answers how many fewer people came in; self-reporting answers what the people doing the hiring thought they were responding to.

The second widely quoted figure is the line that by August, agent work hours outnumbered employees six to one. Read quickly, it puts one person at a desk with six agents working alongside them.

A footnote under the chart unsettles that picture. The agent side was measured from session logs. The human side was headcount multiplied by an assumed 40-hour week. Whether employees worked longer or shorter hours over that period, the denominator does not move. What sets the ratio is the measurement rule rather than agent performance.

The authors did not delete their own limits, and that deserves saying. Tool adoption was not random. Adopters were already on a steeply upward trajectory before they adopted. More code does not necessarily mean better code. At the end of the recommendations sits this line: employment protections for incumbents will not benefit those not hired to begin with.

Pebblous gauges the quality of AI training data with DataClinic. What held our attention in this report was not training data but the data an organization uses to measure itself. Almost every published value is a share, the definitions those shares rest on survive only as a line beneath a chart, and the labels came from Claude reading its own session logs. The questions do not change when the subject does.

A share can fall simply because the organization grew. A headcount cannot.

Six agents working beside one person.

That is the picture the most quoted line from Anthropic's new report leaves behind. By August, it says, agent work hours outnumbered employees six to one.

I went looking for how the two sides of that ratio were counted.

The answer sits in a footnote under the chart, not in the body.

The agent side was measured from session logs, with any wait longer than thirty minutes stripped out. The human side was headcount multiplied by an assumed forty-hour week.

One side was measured. The other was assumed.

Whether people worked longer or shorter hours over those months, the denominator never moves.

The report came out on 16 October, and the line that travelled furthest was a different one. The junior share of new technical hires fell from 27% to 9%. More than ten outlets carried it within three days.

I looked for the headcount behind it and there is none. The document publishes shares and never people.

That matters because the same report states the company tripled in size over the past year.

In an organization that grew threefold, a group's falling share can mean the group shrank, or it can mean the bands above it grew faster.

This is not a quibble invented from outside. The report demonstrates it: the technical share of total staff fell from 61% to 56%, and the next sentence explains that technical hiring kept rising in absolute numbers while sales and finance rose faster.

Anthropic did not leave the junior gap blank, though. Where a ledger would go, there is an April survey of its own managers. About a third in machine learning and a fifth in software engineering said they hired less because of AI.

Evidence, of a different kind. A ledger answers how many fewer people came in. A survey answers what the people doing the hiring believed they were responding to.

Without that list, every quarter returns a different number and no one can say what changed.
