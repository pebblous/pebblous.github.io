Executive Summary

This report reads "How Employment is Changing at Anthropic," published by Anthropic on 16 October 2026, down to its denominators and its reference dates. One line from it — the junior share of technical hiring falling from 27% to 9% — reached more than ten outlets within three days. The figure is sharp, and the organization that compiled it is the same one that published the report.

What the document releases, though, is shares and never people. The same text records that the company tripled in size over the past year, so a falling share on its own cannot separate "fewer juniors came in" from "the bands above them grew faster." Anthropic did not leave that gap untouched. In place of a headcount ledger it put a survey of its own managers. That is evidence, but evidence of a different kind.

The six-to-one figure for agent work hours stands in the same position. A footnote in the report states plainly that one side was measured from session logs while the other was assumed — headcount multiplied by a 40-hour week. So this report does not rule on whether entry-level jobs are disappearing. It separates what the document holds from what it lacks for that ruling, and it leaves anyone who wants to ask the same question of their own organization with a list of what has to be defined first.

Inside the Ledger Anthropic Opened

The publisher is the Anthropic Institute and there are four authors. The lead author, Nathan Wilmers, is an associate professor of work and organization studies at the MIT Sloan School of Management. The material came from inside the company: employment records, internal AI usage logs, output metrics, employee surveys, patterns in internal chat, employee calendars and hiring review notes, bound together and supplemented with seventeen staff interviews. It is rare for a company to open its own HR ledger and its agent logs inside a single document.

Two numbers traveled widely, but the report's opening chart, "Decline of entry-level technical hiring at Anthropic," plots three lines at once: the junior share of new hires, the junior share of people on the books, and the share of job postings open to junior candidates. These are three separate metrics with three different denominators. The headcount share fell from 21% to 14%, seven percentage points or 33% in relative terms; the hiring share fell from 27% to 9%, 18 percentage points or 67% in relative terms. With slopes that far apart, folding both into one sentence as "they fell by a similar amount" is simply wrong.

Each line has its own denominator. The new-hire share is divided by all technical hires in the period, the headcount share by everyone on the books in technical functions, and the posting share by all technical job postings. The three values therefore cannot be added or averaged. Source: Anthropic, "How Employment is Changing at Anthropic," p. 3 and its opening chart.

The same chart carries a control panel for non-technical functions on its right, and most coverage left that panel out. In non-technical functions the three shares barely move. Without that contrast there is no way to separate "the whole company turned cautious about hiring" from "only junior technical roles were squeezed." The table below puts the figures the press carried next to the ones it did not, with each value's denominator and reference window on the same row.

The last row is the bridge into the next section. Technical functions dropped from 61% to 56% of company headcount, and the report explains that drop in its very next sentence: technical hiring kept growing in absolute terms, while hiring in sales, finance and recruiting grew faster still and pushed the share down. That is the report demonstrating, in its own words, that a falling share and a shrinking group are not the same thing. So what about the junior side? There, the document does not answer in people.

What Filled the Space Where the Headcount Should Be

Nearly every employment figure in this report is a share. How many juniors there were, and how many there are now, appears nowhere. A share moves when its numerator moves and also when its denominator moves, so a falling value on its own cannot tell you which of the two happened. And at this company the denominator moved a great deal. The sentence is on page 16: "It has tripled in size in the last year."

If the junior share of headcount went from 21% to 14% inside a company that tripled, at least three different stories can sit underneath. Juniors may genuinely have been cut. The junior count may have held steady while the bands above it grew. Juniors may have grown too, with everything above them growing much faster. The diagram below sets the three side by side. All three produce the same falling share, and what happened to juniors is opposite at the two ends.

Three cases that a share alone cannot tell apart. Bar lengths are chosen for illustration and are not Anthropic's actual headcount. Only the bottom row's "×3" matches a figure stated in the report; the other two are placed there for comparison.

The natural objection follows: the share fell only because the company grew. That objection is blocked once inside the report — but the thing that blocks it is not a headcount ledger. What Anthropic puts in that place is a timeline of internal discussion plus a manager survey. Talk of falling demand for junior technical roles surfaced first in core technical functions in January 2026, the adjustment was actually made in April, and in a survey that same month the managers answered as follows.

"By April, engineering and research teams had deemphasized hiring for lower band technical positions. When surveyed in the same month, a majority of managers described hiring for a different set of skills due to AI. Around a third of ML and a fifth of SWE managers reported hiring less due to AI."

"How Employment is Changing at Anthropic," p. 3. Two claims sit in that passage: a majority of managers are hiring for a different skill set, and a minority are hiring less.

That passage weakens the reading in which the falling share is merely a shadow cast by a swelling denominator, because managers are saying outright that they hired less. It is also, at the same time, evidence that cannot carry the weight of a headcount. Self-reports depend on what a respondent attributes their own decision to, and these respondents work at a company that builds AI. Explaining a smaller hiring plan by pointing at AI is the most available explanation inside this organization. That is not a claim that they are wrong; it is a statement of where this evidence stands.

The surveyed sentence actually says two things at once: that managers hire for different skills, and that some hire less. Only the first of the two has a second record behind it, outside the survey. On page 15 the authors ran hiring review notes for thousands of people hired since 2024 through an automatic classifier. These are the assessments interviewers wrote at the time, not answers recalled later because somebody asked. In 2024, notes mentioning a candidate's track record with AI tools appeared in about 5% of technical reviews and 7% of non-technical ones. By 2026 the technical figure had tripled and the non-technical one had quadrupled. The steeper climb is on the non-technical side.

The same classification shows where the center of gravity in those notes moved. In technical interviews the sharpest growth was in references to a candidate's autonomy and judgment, while the portion of reviews dealing with domain expertise shrank. In non-technical interviews, references to customers and mission grew and references to execution fell. The report does not connect this passage to the slowdown in junior hiring. It records that what interviewers look for has changed; how that change landed on junior candidates is, once again, a question the shares cannot answer.

The facts end there. The report carries no absolute junior headcount, and the manager survey, the internal timeline and the review notes are what occupy that empty space. Interpretation starts after that. These three sources answer a different question from a headcount ledger. A ledger answers "how many fewer people came in." The others answer "what the people doing the hiring were weighing." This report holds the second kind. Asking the first question and then reading the second kind of answer puts the argument out of joint. Outside the survey, the only record supporting the "hired less" reading is the line from section 1 about the junior share of postings, and that line counts how many doors were opened, not how many people walked through.

Four months ago Pebblous wrote about the same company, arguing that the proposal to levy a jobs fund on AI revenue had no measurement infrastructure under it. What was missing then was a denominator to levy against; what is missing now is a denominator to read a share against. The company opened its data, and the blank simply changed seats.

Six to One: What Each Side of the Ratio Measures

One line in the key-findings list at the front of the report says: "By August interactive AI agent hours outnumbered employees' 6 to 1." This is the sentence that traveled furthest in the press, and reading it conjures an image of one person working alongside six agents. But six to one is one number divided by another, and that image only holds if numerator and denominator were measured with the same ruler.

Underneath the chart that plots this figure, the report sets out its measurement rules. Four sentences, and the central fact of this analysis is inside them.

"We limit to employee-directed interactive sessions and pool subagents. Working time concept is agent runtime, including inference and active tool use, but excluding any wait longer than 30 minutes. Human work hours are calculated as headcount and assumed 40 hours per week."

Footnote to the chart titled "Agent hours overtake human hours," where the measurement rules for both sides are set out.

The last of those four sentences decides half of the number. The agent side is a value actually measured from logs. The human side is a value that was never measured at all, because it was built by multiplying headcount by a constant of 40 hours a week. The ratio therefore carries no information about how much people worked. Whether staff put in longer or shorter weeks over the same period, that denominator does not budge. The diagram below separates the two sides.

The two numbers that make one ratio were produced in different ways. The left side is measured from session logs under stated conditions; the right side is headcount times a constant. Both rules are printed in the report's own chart footnote.

Three nearby figures are easy to confuse with this one, so they are worth separating here. The first is the "crossover" date. The body of the report states that agent work hours first passed the human side in March 2026, but the numerator there combines autonomous runs with interactive sessions. The six-to-one figure is a separate calculation restricted to interactive sessions. The two are not two points on one trend line.

The second is idle time. Page 11 reports that when idleness is measured by subtracting active time from the full wall-clock duration of an interactive session, roughly 80% of Claude sessions sit empty, waiting for a human input. The move from there to "so shouldn't 80% come off the six times" is a mistake. Six to one was measured with waits longer than 30 minutes already removed, and the 80% is a separate calculation that applies no such cutoff. The two numbers include and exclude different spans, so no arithmetic joins them.

The third is reading time. Right after the idle discussion, the report asks how long it would take a person to read everything Claude produced in interactive sessions, and answers by converting output tokens per session into words. The conversion uses two constants: 0.75 words per token and 250 words per minute. The result is that technical functions saw output soar over the past six months, and that even non-technical functions would need more than half of a work week to read through the output of their interactive sessions. The chart is titled "Reading Claude as a fulltime job." That result scales directly with both constants. Assume a faster reading speed and "more than half" shrinks by the same proportion on the same logs. Printing the constants in a footnote was the right call, and a reader who never reaches the footnote receives half a number.

The figures gathered here point at a similar phenomenon, and every one of them was taken with a different ruler. Set the wait cutoff at five minutes instead of thirty and the agent-side total shrinks; replace the 40-hour assumption with actual activity records and the denominator moves. The same logs then yield a different multiple, and what decides that multiple is the measurement rule rather than the capability of the agents. That is why the most consequential line in this report sits in a chart footnote rather than the body.

4

The Lines the Authors Drew Around Their Own Findings

After junior shares and agent hours, the subject that takes the most space in this report is output: how much more each person produces, measured function by function, with commits as the headline metric. The body states that "code commits per engineer have risen the fastest, coming in at 5x their velocity as 6 months ago." Commits per engineer are running at five times the velocity of six months earlier, and of all the functions measured this one rose fastest. The accompanying chart plots other functions as well, and the body names them: queries written by data scientists, contracts handled by legal, invoices cleared by accounts payable, deals closed by sales, all rising. The per-function multiples, however, live only as gradations inside the chart and were never carried into the body as numbers. What survives in prose is the ordering — engineers rose fastest.

This five is not the same kind of number as the six in section 3. Six to one is work hours divided by work hours. Five times is per-person output rebased to an index at a reference date, and then the growth of that index. The chart footnote notes that the plotted line is not raw data either but a smoothed curve fitted to the index. Setting the two numbers side by side in one sentence blurs both, which is why the output figures are unpacked separately here.

At this point it is tempting to settle on "it is all their own bookkeeping anyway," and summarizing it that way sells the report short. The authors did not present the output growth as a simple before-and-after. They took 192 engineers who adopted coding agents and 74 who did not adopt over the same period, and compared people inside the same company in the same month who differed only in tooling. Treatment timing was set at each person's first month with five or more agent sessions; person and month entered as separate fixed effects; standard errors were clustered at the person level. For an internal analysis, that is careful work.

Original Pebblous diagram. The slopes illustrate the structure of the design, not Anthropic's actual output curve. The authors wrote that adopters were already on a steep upward trajectory before adoption, an admission that the parallel pre-trend assumption is shaky.

4.1The People Who Ran the Design Also Wrote Down Its Weaknesses

And the authors put into the same document what that design does not prove. These are sentences a report pushing for causality would have deleted. Four of them, side by side.

  • They state flatly that adoption was not random: "Note however that tool adoption is not random," and the sentence runs on into the example they supply themselves — if someone picked up the tool because a large project was coming, the estimate is biased.
  • They record that adopters were already climbing steeply before they adopted: "Those who adopt were already on a steeply upward trajectory compared to non-adopters." That is an admission that the parallel pre-trend assumption a quasi-experimental design rests on is shaky.
  • They write that more code is not the same as good code or business results: "More code does not necessarily mean better code or business impact."
  • They attach the same warning to the gains in sales. Whoever adopted automation first may have been the account executive with the richest pipeline to begin with.

The code-quality passage calls for particular care. The report's quality panel shows six proxy measures: the share of lines removed within 90 days for any reason, the share of those removed in bug fixes, whether a commit was explicitly reverted in full, newly added lint-suppression comments, newly added notes flagging something to be fixed, and tests marked as skipped, retried or allowed to fail. But the range over which the body explicitly asserts "no difference" covers only the first two of the six, removals and reverts. That assertion also arrives immediately after an admission: "There are many dimensions of code quality, and AI errors may be harder to identify than human ones. But we find no difference in rates of lines of code subsequently removed or reverted." The sentence comes after conceding that quality has many dimensions and that AI errors may be harder to spot than human ones. Rendering that as "quality held up" states it more strongly than the report does.

4.2The Sharpest Self-Limit Sits at the End of the Recommendations

On page 18 the report offers three measures other companies might borrow: sharing the gains broadly through equity ownership, running an early-warning system that detects which roles AI is destabilizing, and moving affected employees into other internal roles rather than out into the external labor market. Then it closes the section with this.

"None of these actions will prevent powerful AI from disrupting jobs. And indeed none directly address the slowdown in junior hires documented in this report. Employment protections for incumbents will not benefit those not hired to begin with."

"How Employment is Changing at Anthropic," p. 18, closing the section that proposes the three measures.

All three measures face the people already inside, and the people this report's own title points at are outside. The authors wrote that mismatch down themselves. No sentence in the whole document cuts deeper.

4.3The Caveats Were Not Erased, They Were Scattered

Did any of these caveats reach the outside? Opening the five stories published within three days and matching what each carried against what each dropped, "nobody passed the caveats along" turns out to be false. What survives differs from outlet to outlet, though, so a reader who saw only one of them received a single fragment of the line the authors drew. The table sets out the comparison.

Read the table down its columns and the shape of the blank appears. Three outlets carried one fragment each and two carried figures only, and in none of the five is there a question about how many fewer juniors there were. A missing headcount is a hard kind of gap to call out, because it is not a wrong number but an absent one, and absent things attract no comparison and so draw no attention. That is where this analysis stands.

Can One Company's Interior Be Read as the Economy?

The report positions itself in its opening paragraph: as a company at the leading edge of AI adoption, Anthropic may be a leading indicator for the wider economy. On page 18 it also argues the opposite side. "There are many parts of Anthropic's employment practices that cannot be replicated across the economy." One document claiming both leading indicator and special case means the question has to be settled against outside material.

5.1The Report Itself Says This Is Not the First Case

One misreading has to be cleared away first. This is not the first time a company has produced such a result from its own data, and page 16 says so outright: "Prior work also finds that AI-native start-ups and AI-adopters are flatter and employ fewer entry-level employees." That prior work is concrete. The Harvard Business School working paper "AI-Native Firms" linked roughly 2,900 Y Combinator startups and venture-backed companies to workforce data and found that AI-native firms are smaller than comparable peers of the same vintage, carry a lower junior share, and run flatter hierarchies.

What is new in this report, then, is not the finding but the layer at which it was measured. The earlier work scraped workforce data from outside; this document is one company opening its HR ledger, its session telemetry and its hiring review notes at once. You can call it the first record of this pattern seen from inside. You cannot call it the first sighting of the pattern.

5.2When Outside Figures Are Lined Up, the Denominator Goes on the Same Row

There is no shortage of outside figures showing junior hiring in decline, but placing them beside Anthropic's numbers goes wrong quickly. One is a share inside a single company; the others are employment levels across the whole US economy or a particular occupational group, or the composition of job postings. The table below lists four frequently co-cited sources with their denominators attached. Where two rows have different denominators, their values can be neither added nor compared.

Source Value A share of what

Stanford Digital Economy Lab19% relative employment gap for ages 22–25US payroll records: how far youth employment in AI-exposed occupations diverged from the trend in unexposed ones

PwC Global AI Jobs BarometerAI-exposed entry-level postings +35% against 2019, others −10%Change within a 27-country posting sample (a shift in required skills rather than a loss of positions)

Of the four rows, only Anthropic's denominator is a single company. The other three are the US or a multinational labor market, and Indeed counts postings rather than hires. Anthropic's value cannot be directly subtracted from or divided by any of the other three.

The Stanford figure needs a separate note. Across editions, that study changed its headline metric. Early versions led with regression estimates of 13% and 16% that controlled for firm-level shocks; the August 2026 revision swapped in a simple descriptive gap requiring no modeling and made 19% the new headline. For the same July 2025 data, the old metric reads 13% and the new one 15%. So describing a trend that "grew from 13% through 16% to 19%" is wrong. The 13–16% that Pebblous used in a piece comparing perception against measurement belongs to the old metric that has since been replaced, which makes 19% the figure to cite now.

The counterargument is also still live. The Economic Innovation Group pointed out that if new hiring simply stops with no layoffs, employment among 22- to 25-year-olds falls mechanically a year later purely because the cohort ages out of the denominator, and it offered an interest-rate explanation on the grounds that postings in AI-exposed occupations began falling in 2022, before ChatGPT was released. Stanford replied that rates do depress employment overall, but that this does not explain why juniors in AI-exposed occupations fall further than everyone else. Reading Anthropic as a leading indicator therefore rests on a dispute that has not closed.

5.3A Sister Report Released the Same Day Cools the Temperature

On 16 October the Anthropic Institute put out two reports together. The other is "The AI expectations gap," and the two share a co-author. The employment report carries one line of that comparison in its own body: as of August, Anthropic employees thought AI would be able to do 75% of their work by 2030, against 28% among other Americans. The sister report breaks that gap down further. Restrict the comparison group to computer, engineering and science occupations and it is 33%; restrict it further to people in those occupations who already use AI regularly and it is 40%. Change the comparison group and the size of the gap changes, so anyone carrying these figures has to name the group in the same sentence.

A leading indicator means "gets there first," not "goes through the same thing." What the sister report shows is how far this organization's expectations sit from everyone else's, and different expectations produce different hiring decisions. Whether what happened at Anthropic will happen elsewhere is not a question these two documents can answer. What they can answer is what each organization would have to measure to find out, and that list is the next section.

6

What Your Organization Has to Define First

The most useful way to read this report is as method rather than conclusion. The moment when an executive walks in holding the document and says "let's measure ourselves like this" is not far off. What that moment calls for is a list of definitions, not a summary. As the preceding sections showed, most of the numbers here shift depending on which rule you pick, and those rules are scattered through chart footnotes and methodology notes. The eight rows below invert the footnotes into the things an organization has to settle before it can measure the same thing.

Decide this first What Anthropic did, and what follows from it
Where a session begins and endsEmployee-directed interactive sessions were separated from autonomous runs and used as different metrics. Without that split, two numbers that look alike — the "crossover" and "six to one" — get flattened onto one trend line
The idle-time cutoffOnly waits longer than 30 minutes were cut. Change the cutoff and the same logs yield different work hours. Leave the value out of your documentation and next quarter's figure is not comparable
Where the human hours come fromHeadcount multiplied by an assumed 40-hour week. Switch to measured activity logs and the ratio itself changes. A ratio that does not say which of the two it used cannot be read
The constants that turn tokens into timeConverted at 0.75 words per token and 250 words per minute. Any result of the form "reading all the agent output takes N hours a week" scales directly with both constants
How the automatic classifier is builtTask difficulty and metawork status were assigned by having Claude score the logs. For metawork, the footnote gives a sample of 2,000 and four classification criteria, and does not give a human review rate. Shift the instructions by one notch and the same logs produce a different share
Whether the level bands stayed stableThe report does not say whether the dividing line for "junior" was the same throughout the study window. If the leveling system changes midway, numerator and denominator move together
The as-of date for headcountThe company-wide share is given only for "the last year," with no as-of date. Unless month-end or quarter-end is fixed, a time series does not hold
Whether a control group is available192 people and 74 people in the same company in the same month, differing only in tooling. Without such a pairing, output growth cannot be separated into what AI did and what company growth did

The right-hand column is drawn from the report's chart footnotes and methodology notes. Rows six and seven are items the report does not disclose, which makes them the places Anthropic itself would have to fix before measuring with the same ruler in a later edition.

Only after all eight are settled does a usable question appear. Who counts the agent work hours in your organization, and under which rules? And what else would you need before those hours could inform a hiring decision? The answer to the second question is the same as the cell this report left blank: people, not shares. Unless headcount by level and new hires by level are pinned down month by month, the observation that a share fell will never separate itself from the fact that the organization grew.

One last thing worth reading on its own terms is the report's set of recommendations. Broad profit sharing, an early-warning system and internal redeployment are all reasonable proposals, and they genuinely help the people already inside an organization. But as section 4.2 records, the authors wrote themselves that none of the three is aimed at the slowdown in junior hiring. More protection for the people inside and a narrowing door can happen at the same time, and that simultaneity is exactly what this report documents. An organization that does not measure the two separately will see its redeployment statistics improve while junior intake dries up, with nothing in the numbers to show it.

7

Why Pebblous Is Watching

Start by pulling one footnote from section 3 back out: human work hours were built by multiplying headcount by a 40-hour week. That single line decides half of the most widely quoted number in the report. It sits under a chart rather than in the body, and not one of the five news stories carried it. That is the place Pebblous spent the longest looking.

7.1Data an Organization Uses to Measure Itself Needs the Same Standard

AI-Ready Data usually refers to the data fed to a model. This report shows the phrase traveling one seat over. Anthropic pulled session logs, commit records, calendars and hiring review notes together and built metrics out of them. Nothing stalled for want of data; what stalled was that the definitions those metrics stand on survive only as a line of footnote. The work DataClinic does on a dataset — exposing what came in under which rule and pointing at the cells that are missing — is not an operation reserved for model training data. The same questions attach, unchanged, to the data an organization uses to measure itself.

7.2Derived Metrics Survive and the Raw Counts Disappear

From a data quality standpoint this report is a textbook display of a familiar defect. Almost every published value is a share, so numerator and denominator move together while neither appears in absolute terms. Derived metrics survive and the raw counts are gone. One layer further down sits a labeling problem. The metawork share rising from 10% to 22%, and the share of difficult tasks rising from 10% to 13%, both come from labels Claude assigned by reading its own session logs. Change the labeler and the values change. The question once asked about label quality in training data has simply moved seats to label quality in organizational metrics, and the way to ask it has not changed at all.

Where the boundary of that label falls is printed in a chart footnote. A sample of 2,000 one-line conversation summaries was sorted, and what counted as metawork was: building Claude skills, planning for or reviewing the output of other agents, standing up dashboards and monitors for agent pipelines, and documenting ways of working. The footnote states that those four stand in contrast to the actual work itself. Drop one of the four or add another and 22% becomes a different number. How many cases a human re-checked is not recorded. That the denominator behind 10% to 22% is the average employee's interactive Claude sessions also has to be written down, or the same spot cannot be measured again next quarter.

Original Pebblous diagram. Both the metawork share (10%→22%) and the difficult-task share (10%→13%) pass through this pipeline. The four classification criteria — building Claude skills, planning or reviewing other agents, building pipeline dashboards and monitors, documenting ways of working — appear in the report's chart footnote; the human review rate does not.

7.3The Question Customers Will Bring Is Definitions, Not Conclusions

The eight rows in section 6 are less a checklist than a list of things to bring. Counting agent work hours is easy enough. Making that count sit alongside the human one is not, and that requires settling the session boundary, the idle cutoff and the source of the human hours first. An organization holding those definitions can measure again next quarter with the same ruler; one without them receives a different number every quarter and cannot explain why it moved. In any discussion about entry-level roles the gap between those two positions grows unusually wide, because junior hiring shows up in the metrics long after the decision was made. The other side of this change, where the bar for required skills rises, Pebblous examined separately in a piece on the rising threshold for entry-level work.

Four months ago Pebblous wrote about the same company — that article sits at the end of section 2. What was missing there was a denominator too. That the blank is still in the same place after the company opened its data suggests a problem of measurement design rather than of willingness to disclose. Putting the same question to a new set of facts is why Pebblous keeps following this subject.

Sections 1 through 6 carry what was confirmed in the Anthropic report and its footnotes, the sister report released the same day, and the articles published within three days of release. Section 7 is the interpretation Pebblous adds on top. They are worth reading separately. That is also why every figure here carries its denominator and its reference date on the same line. No share in this analysis travels without both. Thank you for reading this far.

  • 1.Nathan Wilmers, Regina Catipon, Maxim Massenkoff, Peter McCrory, "How Employment is Changing at Anthropic" (The Anthropic Institute, 16 October 2026). anthropic.com · PDF, 20 pp. — the primary oracle for this analysis. The three shares (27%→9%, 21%→14%, postings at 6%), the non-technical control, the technical share of company headcount at 61%→56%, the six-to-one ratio and its chart footnote, the March 2026 crossover, 80% idle time in interactive sessions, commits per engineer at five times, the 192-adopter and 74-non-adopter design, the six quality proxies, the one-third and one-fifth manager survey figures, the company tripling in size, hiring reviews mentioning AI tools tripling and quadrupling, metawork at 10%→22%, difficult tasks at 10%→13%, the conversion constants of 0.75 words per token and 250 words per minute, automation expectations of 75% against 28%, and the three recommendations with their closing caveat all come from here.
  • 2.Szymon Sacher, Tess Cotter, Charles I. Jones, Anton Korinek, Peter McCrory, "The AI expectations gap" (The Anthropic Institute, 16 October 2026). anthropic.com — the occupational breakdown in section 5.3 (33% for computer, engineering and science occupations, 40% for regular AI users within them) comes from this report. It shares a co-author with item 1.

Academic — prior work and outside comparisons

  • 3.Hyunjin Kim, Rembrand Koning, "AI-Native Firms" (Harvard Business School Working Paper 26-090, 2026). hbs.edu — the prior work cited in section 5.1 as grounds for saying this is not a first. It links roughly 2,900 Y Combinator startups and venture-backed companies to workforce data.
  • 4.Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, Ruyu Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence" (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised 12 August 2026). stanford.edu — the 19% in section 5.2 belongs to this edition, and the switch from a regression estimate to a simple descriptive gap is recorded on page 2 of this PDF. The authors state in the abstract that the figure should be read as an early descriptive signal rather than a causal estimate.
  • 5.Zanna Iscenko, Fabien Curto Millet, "Looking for the Ladder" (Economic Innovation Group, January 2026) — the counterargument in section 5.2, covering the mechanical age effect produced by a hiring freeze alone and the interest-rate explanation.

  • 6.PwC, "Global AI Jobs Barometer 2026" (15 June 2026) — the fourth row of the comparison table in section 5.2. It analyzes postings across 27 countries and reports that AI-exposed and other entry-level postings moved in opposite directions.
