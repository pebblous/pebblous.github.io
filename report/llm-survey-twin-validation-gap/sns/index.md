## LinkedIn (KO)

나이와 성별만 알려 준 AI 응답자가, 같은 분야 태도 문항 다섯 개까지 물려 준 AI보다 설문 전체 평균을 더 잘 맞혔다.

컬럼비아 경영대 오데드 네처와 시장조사 회사 TRC 인사이츠의 라잔 샘반담이 9월에 공개한 논문의 실측이다. 태도 문항 108개를 놓고 같은 합성 응답자를 네 가지 잣대로 쟀더니 네 수가 서로 다른 말을 했다. 집계 평균 오차는 정보를 덜 준 쪽이 0.39, 더 준 쪽이 0.48이었다.

그런데 정보를 덜 준 그 조건에서 개인 한 사람의 답은 사실상 못 맞혔다. 108개 문항 가운데 개인 수준 상관이 0.5를 넘긴 것이 하나도 없었다. 두 수는 같은 자료에서 같은 순간에 나왔고 둘 다 참이다. 집계값만 보는 검수라면 한 사람도 못 맞힌 트윈에게 합격을 줄 수 있다는 뜻이다.

한국어 자료에서는 다른 모양의 실패가 나타났다. 성·연령으로 나눈 집단별 오차가 두 모델에서 18.9%p와 15.9%p였다. 집단을 아예 나누지 않고 전체 평균 하나만 써 넣은 기준선은 11.6%p였다. 개인차가 지워진 것이 아니라 없던 차이가 새로 생긴 것이다.

그래서 합성 패널을 살 때 물을 것은 정확도가 몇 퍼센트인가가 아니다. 그 수가 어느 층에서 성립했고, 우리가 내릴 결정이 같은 층에 있는가다. 2025년에 개정된 ICC/ESOMAR 국제 코드는 합성 데이터를 썼다는 사실을 밝히라고 요구한다. 그 정확도를 어느 층에서 확인했는지 밝히라는 조항은 아직 없다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

An AI respondent told nothing but age and gender matched a survey's topline better than one also handed five attitude questions from the same sector.

The finding comes from a September paper by Oded Netzer of Columbia Business School and Rajan Sambandam, president of the market research firm TRC Insights. Measuring the same digital twins against 108 attitude questions with four different yardsticks produced four different answers. Topline error came to 0.39 for the demographics-only condition and 0.48 for the better-informed one.

At the individual level, that same condition recovered almost nothing. Across all 108 questions, not one reached a correlation of 0.5. Both numbers come from the same data at the same moment, and both are true. A review that reads only aggregates can pass a twin that did not match a single person.

Korean-language data surfaced a different failure. Errors computed inside age and gender cells ran to 18.9 and 15.9 percentage points for two models. A baseline that ignored the cells entirely, writing one weighted average into every box, came to 11.6. The personas were not losing differences. They were inventing them.

So the question to put to a synthetic panel vendor is not the accuracy percentage. It is which tier that number was established at, and whether your decision sits at the same tier. The ICC/ESOMAR code revised in 2025 requires disclosure that synthetic data was used. Nothing in it yet requires stating which tier the accuracy holds at.

## Facebook (EN)

Most research proposals put a number on the first page, in bold, and it does its job. You relax a little.

I do too. I rarely go back and ask what that number measured.

A paper published in September pushes directly on the habit. Oded Netzer of Columbia Business School and Rajan Sambandam of the market research firm TRC Insights took about a hundred attitude questions and measured the same AI respondents four different ways.

The four numbers disagreed.

Twins told nothing but age and gender matched the topline of the human sample well. Those same twins recovered no individual's answers, on any of the hundred-odd questions. Both figures come from one dataset, at one moment.

One line from the paper stayed with me. "A twin that knows nothing about a person can still produce a topline a client would accept."

A separate study, run on Korean data, found something stranger. Splitting respondents into age and gender cells produced larger errors than ignoring the cells entirely and writing one weighted average into every box. One model put teenage AI adoption 71 points too high. The folk belief that younger people use more AI had simply become the answer.

A difference erased and a difference invented do not cost the same.

The first makes data quiet. The second makes it confidently wrong.

So the question I kept circling was this one. "What was this synthetic panel built from, and who other than the people selling it has checked the accuracy?"

That is close to why Pebblous keeps quality scores per class instead of issuing one grade for a dataset. A good overall score is only good about the thing it measures.

An average that holds while the people inside it disappear. What should we call that?
