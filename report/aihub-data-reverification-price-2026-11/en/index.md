# AI Hub Reopens the Data It Once Cleared of Personal Information

_A $207K contract to re-screen 150 published datasets closes in December. A de-identification ruling has a shelf life._

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> AI Hub (aihub.or.kr), Korea's national repository of AI training data, is reopening 150 datasets it has already published to look for personal information still sitting inside them. Four separate things happened to this one platform during 2026: a first full census of public-sector training data, a procurement to rebuild the repository itself, a call for private firms and universities to hand over data, and a contract to re-screen data that had already gone out the door. This article lines those four up by following the money attached to each.

> The state is not buying data. It is paying for quality work and de-identification, and taking openness in return. The private-sector notice caps that support at KRW 60 million (about $41,000) per dataset. Yet at the intake desk the quality management report is a document you file "only where it can be submitted," and in the same year a separate contract went out to reopen data that had already entered the repository and been published. De-identification is not a state but a timestamp. It is not a stamp you press once; it is a ruling with a shelf life.

> Everything to this point is written in procurement notices, statutes and press releases. The reading that "a guarantee has a shelf life" is ours. This article is not arguing that the government is failing. Putting out a tender to reopen, of its own accord, data it had already declared de-identified and sent out is rare and is the right move. The open question is whether that work comes with an interval attached.

<!-- stat-card -->
**$41K** — Ceiling on quality and de-identification support for one incoming dataset — The private-sector notice writes KRW 60M per dataset, up to 100 datasets. A ceiling, not a flat fee

<!-- stat-card -->
**150 datasets** — Published data being opened and checked a second time — AI Hub's listing showed 977 datasets in total when checked on November 8, 2026. This is not a re-screen of everything

<!-- stat-card -->
**5 days** — From the August 27 release to the September 1 takedown — Four of the 22 datasets described as quality-checked and privacy-checked came down in that window

<!-- stat-card -->
**2–3 years** — Re-assessment interval for re-identification risk written by Ontario's Information and Privacy Commissioner — No interval of that kind is found in the Korean statutes

## In 2026, four separate jobs landed on one platform

What happened to AI Hub in 2026 was four jobs of four different kinds: counting the data the state already holds (the census), rebuilding the repository itself (the system procurement), taking in data from outside (the call for submissions), and reopening data that had already been taken in and published (the re-verification contract). Each ran on its own notice and its own budget, and each was reported separately. Put them in one place and what this repository did over the year reads as a single line.

AI Hub is the repository that, since 2017, has collected and published AI training data built with Korean government money. It is operated by the National Information Society Agency (NIA) under the Ministry of Science and ICT. Article 15 of the AI Framework Act, in force since January 22, 2026, gave the repository a new legal standing, and its name is changing with it. The formal name is the Integrated Provision System for AI Training Data, shortened here to the integrated provision system. The phrase **integrated provision framework**, which appears in the April coverage and in the name of the re-verification contract, points at the same thing. Original wording is kept only inside quotations.

April's KRW 6 billion and the August notice's KRW 60 million per dataset multiply out exactly, which makes them easy to read as one pot of money. No document says they are. The August notice carries no total budget, and April's KRW 6 billion is attached to 100 datasets to be chosen inside the public sector. This article stops short of adding them and records only this much: **the same "KRW 60 million per dataset" condition is attached to all three intake windows — public, private and university.**

In August 2026 the Board of Audit and Inspection flagged duplicate construction and verification gaps in public AI training data, and that finding sits behind this overhaul. The audit itself is covered in [a separate article](/report/korea-public-ai-data-duplication-audit-2026-08/en/). This one looks at what came after it.

## The state pays to clean data, not to buy it

What the state puts up in the August 3 notice is not a price for data. What the notice lists is technical and financial support — quality work such as consulting, plus de-identification — capped at KRW 60 million (about $41,000) per dataset for up to 100 datasets. Press-release distribution and media promotion are on the list of support items too. A company receives not cash but hands to work on its own data. And in exchange for those hands, what the state receives is not ownership but **openness**.

The university-sector notice states that condition more plainly.

"Quality verification and de-identification measures may be applied for by an institution on condition of release."  
Call for submissions on university-sector AI training data provision, registration and quality support, posted September 1, 2026. Translated from the Korean original.

The whole bargain fits in one sentence. Agree to open the data and the state covers the cost of cleaning it; decline, and the support goes away. From 2017 through 2024, what the government mostly did was **build new data**. The Board of Audit's tally for that period puts newly built public datasets at 908, worth $1.13 billion (KRW 1.63 trillion). The windows that opened in 2026 are a different animal. The money is now attached to fixing data somebody already holds and moving it into a published state. What building costs and what cleaning costs are gathered into a four-row table in Section 5.

So what happens to data too sensitive to open? The notice points at two controlled spaces. One is the **Safe Zone** run by AI Hub, where the data never leaves and can only be used inside a designated cloud environment. Access goes through a review that the notice says takes up to two weeks, plus up to five days to set up the environment. The other is the **Data Safety Zone** established under the Data Industry Act. The two are separate schemes with different enabling laws and different operators, and the similar names get them mixed up often. Four new Data Safety Zones were designated in May 2025, bringing the count to 14 zones across 11 institutions; any change since then is beyond what this article verified. On the Data Safety Zone side, the K-ICT Big Data Center reopened on October 1, 2026 as the AI Data Safe Use Center, taking on the role of central hub.

> [!callout]
> That is the design of the front door. The state pays, private firms and universities open their data, and what cannot be opened goes into a controlled space. The design itself looks complete. The problem lies in **what gets written down at that door**, which is the next section.

## The quality report is filed 'only where it can be submitted'

Here is the list of what a participating company must hand in, copied from the private-sector notice. Read the list alone and it looks as though the state has pinned down the definition of AI-ready in the form of paperwork. But one more line sits underneath the list.

"– Data specification sheet, 1 copy  

                            – Sample data (see Annex 5 of the attached plan)  

                            – Related deliverables (dataset construction report, quality management report, data description document, labeling guide, metadata definition document, etc.)  

                            ※ Submit only those materials that can be submitted"  
Guide to participating in the private-sector call for AI training data provision, August 3, 2026, AI Hub notice. Translated from the Korean original; the final line reads "제출가능한 자료에 한해 제출."

Count the lines and only two are compulsory: the data specification sheet and the sample data. The remaining five sit inside a single bundle called "related deliverables," trailed by an "etc.," and the whole bundle carries the caveat "submit only those materials that can be submitted." In other words, **the quality management report is optional, not required.** The construction report that records how the data was built, the labeling guide and the metadata definition document all sit in that same box.

The university side is simpler still. The submission method the notice gives is to fill in the data specification form (Annex 3) and send it by email, and no support amount appears in the body of the notice. That does not mean there is no money; it means the body of the notice does not say. The private window's mandatory paperwork is two documents; the university's is one form.
