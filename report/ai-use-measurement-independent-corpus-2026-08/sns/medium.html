A Work Filter Cut Half the Conversations Out of AI Use Statistics

Almost everything the public knows about how AI is actually used comes from a single kind of source: a report a model provider wrote about its own logs, from a sample the provider selected. The numbers travel far. They land in policy memos, investment decks and safety assessments. What almost never travels with them is the rule that decided which conversations were counted in the first place.

In August 2026, a group of researchers ran an experiment on exactly that rule. The AI Observatory, a preprint from Shayne Longpre of MIT, Anka Reuel of Stanford, Dayeon Ki of the University of Maryland and collaborators at a dozen institutions, assembled seven public conversation corpora with documented consent, annotated them under one shared taxonomy, and then did something no vendor report can do for itself. It borrowed a vendor's published measurement rule and ran it over independent data.

Borrowing a rule and running it somewhere else

The rule came from the March 2025 edition of Anthropic's Economic Index. That pipeline works in two stages: a binary filter keeps only conversations judged to be occupational, and whatever survives is mapped onto O*NET task clusters. Anthropic had published the system prompt and the taxonomy, so the researchers reimplemented the gate and applied it to their own corpus.

Of the seven sources, six could be tested; the seventh sat inside a data-use agreement that allowed only pre-agreed aggregate annotations to leave its enclave. Across the 22,956 pooled conversations that remained, 47.9% were classified as non-occupational and stopped at the first stage. They never entered the task distribution at all.

The paper is careful about what this does and does not mean. It says outright that the figure is not an estimate of Claude.ai traffic or of representative AI use. Its actual claim is narrower and sharper: the gate is not a neutral preprocessing step.

The discarded half was not random

Dropping non-work conversations from a work statistic is ordinary practice. The problem is what came out with them. Holding the sources and annotations fixed and splitting the corpus on the gate alone, the researchers compared what the rule removed against what it kept:

  • Health and relationships: 44.2% of removed conversations, 31.2% of those kept
  • Harassment and hate: 27.5% versus 5.6%
  • Sexual content: 15.7% versus 2.4%
  • Adult and illicit topics: 7.9% versus 2.1%

The conversations that safety policy and risk assessment most need to see were cut before the work frame counted anything. As the paper puts it, a taxonomy can look comprehensive while systematically filtering out uses that are socially significant, policy-relevant, or disproportionately linked to harm. What is missing is invisible from inside the scheme that removed it.

The same rule, different answers

47.9% is a pooled average, and it hides a wide spread. Run the identical gate source by source and the non-occupational share ranges from 34.2% on one corpus to 61.9% on another. Not a line of the rule changed. Only the data it met changed.

Time moves the answer too. On WildChat, the one source with multi-year timestamps, average prompt length rose more than tenfold between April 2023 and July 2025 while responses doubled. Yet returning users, examined individually, did not intensify their use; they narrowed it. The corpus grew because its user population turned over, not because anyone leaned in harder.

Anthropic's own data now says something similar. Its sixth Economic Index report, published in June 2026, replaced the seven-day samples every previous edition had relied on with continuous telemetry. The first thing continuous sampling revealed was a weekday effect: personal-purpose conversations sit near 35% on weekdays and climb toward 50% on weekends. Where a seven-day window happens to fall can move a headline number by roughly fifteen points.

The part that is easy to get backwards

It would be a misreading to conclude that vendor statistics are untrustworthy and independent ones are not. Vendor logs remain unmatched in scale and coverage, and a 23,000-conversation corpus does not replace them. The Observatory is not a new authority. It is a second, comparable vantage point, and the paper documents its own weaknesses at length: annotation agreement with human labels is imperfect, the annotations are not deterministic even at temperature zero, and the validation set was built by the same people who designed the taxonomy.

There is also an irony worth stating plainly. The researchers reimplemented Anthropic's rule not because Anthropic is uniquely suspect but because it was the only rule published well enough to rerun. Anthropic released the March 2025 data under CC-BY and the code under MIT. From that moment the numbers stopped being promotional material and became something a stranger could check. A company that keeps its classification rules private cannot be criticized this way at all. Asked to comment on the study, Anthropic said supporting independent external research matters; OpenAI did not respond.

What this means for anyone building usage statistics

Pebblous works on the state data is in before it reaches a model, so this study reads less like industry news and more like a familiar failure mode arriving in a new place. The lineage problem people accept for training data turns out to apply just as strictly to usage data.

The most reusable finding is almost mundane. The Observatory could run its temporal analysis on only one of seven sources, because only one carried stable user identifiers alongside enough time history. Fields that are not recorded at collection time cannot be reconstructed later by any method. Timestamps, model identifier and version, a stable user identifier, language, consent basis, redistribution rights, and the annotating model and version: any organization building an internal AI usage dashboard should confirm it is capturing these now.

And when you next meet a sentence of the form "X% of AI users do Y", four questions decide whether it means anything. What is the denominator. What was discarded before counting. When was it collected, and over how many days. And can the same rule be run against different data. A number that fails the fourth has not yet been checked by anyone.
