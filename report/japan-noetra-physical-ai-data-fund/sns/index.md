일본이 1조 엔을 건 곳은 또 하나의 언어 모델이 아니었다.

소니·소프트뱅크·NEC·혼다가 세운 컨소시엄 노에트라, 그리고 44개 투자기관이 함께 선 이 판의 목표는 공장·의료·매장의 센서로 움직이는 로봇의 두뇌다. 소프트뱅크 측은 "일본 산업이 쥔 데이터가 경쟁력의 원천"이라고 못박았다.

이유는 데이터의 성격에 있다. 웹 텍스트는 누구나 긁어 가지만, 접촉과 모션과 실패의 기록은 실제 현장에서만 쌓이고 사올 수도 스크래핑할 수도 없다. 전 세계 로봇 조작 데이터를 다 합쳐도 텍스트와는 자릿수가 여러 단계 벌어진다.

그런데 1조 엔을 부어도 품질과 큐레이션 없이는 그 데이터가 로봇의 두뇌를 세우지 못한다. 잘 고른 500개 시연이 부실하게 학습한 거대 모델을 앞선다는 것이 지금 로보틱스 진영이 도달한 결론이다.

소버린 AI의 전선이 언어에서 물리로 옮겨갔고, 사올 수 없는 현장 데이터가 국부가 되었다. 데이터를 진단하고 다듬어 온 페블러스가 이 발표에서 눈을 떼지 못한 이유도 여기에 있다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Japan just committed up to ¥1 trillion (about $6.16B) over five years — not to another large language model, but to a robot's brain.

The money backs Noetra, a consortium founded by Sony, SoftBank, NEC and Honda and joined by 44 investors. Its target is an AI that runs on sensor data from factories, hospitals and stores rather than on scraped web text. SoftBank put it plainly: the data Japanese industry already holds is the source of its edge.

The logic sits in the nature of the data. Anyone can crawl the open web, but records of contact, motion and failure only accumulate where a robot actually works, and they can't be scraped or bought from a broker. Add up every robot manipulation dataset on Earth and it still trails text by several orders of magnitude.

Yet money alone won't build the brain. Without curation the data stays inert, and today's robotics consensus is that 500 well-chosen demonstrations beat a sloppily trained 70B model. The bottleneck isn't compute. It's whether scattered field data can be made usable.

Sovereign AI's front line has moved from language to the physical world, and field data that can't be bought has become national wealth. That is exactly the question Pebblous, which spends its days diagnosing and cleaning data, has been working on.

## Facebook (EN)

Picking up a cup.

For us it's nothing. A robot learns it by dropping the cup a few thousand times and reaching again. And that record of failure isn't for sale anywhere.

A few days ago Japan placed up to ¥1 trillion, over five years, behind a consortium called Noetra: Sony, SoftBank, NEC and Honda.

What stayed with me wasn't the number. It was the direction. The money isn't chasing another language model. It's aimed at a robot's brain, one that runs on the sensors of factories, hospitals and stores.

SoftBank said the data Japanese industry already holds is the source of its edge.

The odd word there is "holds." You can't really hold web text, since anyone can crawl it. But the trace a robot leaves as it bumps, drops and grips again belongs to whoever stood on that floor.

So a question keeps catching on me.

"Whose is the experience that moves a robot?"

Four companies each pour in their own field data, yet who owns it and who may reuse it hasn't been made public.

There's a quieter turn underneath. Even ¥1 trillion won't raise the brain if the data isn't made usable. The field's own conclusion now is that 500 well-chosen demonstrations beat a bigger, sloppier model. Quality decides it, not scale.

That is why a company that has spent its days diagnosing and cleaning data couldn't look away from this announcement.

The logs in your factory, the sensor records in your store: someday, to someone, they may be the asset money can't buy. Whether they are truly yours might be worth asking now.
