## LinkedIn (KO)

줄표가 많으면 AI 초안으로 본다는 규칙을 아직 돌리는 조직이 적지 않다. 마케팅 분석 회사 Graphite의 최신 측정에서 그 규칙은 반대로 틀린다. Claude Opus 5.5가 쓰는 줄표는 1,000단어당 0.015개이고, 같은 코퍼스에서 사람이 쓴 글은 3.14개를 쓴다.

통념이 처음부터 틀렸던 것은 아니다. 같은 표 위쪽에서 GPT-4.1과 GPT-5는 사람의 두 배 넘게 쓴다. 사람들이 줄표를 AI의 표지로 익히던 무렵 눈앞에 있던 모델이 바로 그 둘이다. 지금 쓰이는 모델 넷은 전부 사람 아래로 내려갔다. 신호가 흐려진 것이 아니라 가리키는 방향이 반대로 돌아섰다.

사라진 것은 줄표지 AI 티가 아니다. 같은 연구가 센 tell 가짓수는 2,666개에서 2,548개로 4% 줄었을 뿐이다.

빠진 자리에는 다른 버릇이 들어왔다. Opus 5.5가 사람보다 가장 두드러지게 많이 쓰는 표현은 "this matters"이고, 사람의 116배다. 줄표는 글자 하나라서 세기도 지우기도 쉽지만, 문장을 어떻게 맺을지는 그만큼 쉽게 지워지지 않는다.

이 수치가 참인 조건은 좁다. 비교 대상인 사람 글은 2022년 11월 이전에 발행된 웹 기사이고, AI 글은 프롬프트 하나로 쓴 SEO형 기사다. 대화창은 재지 않았다. 같은 Opus 5를 다른 과제로 잰 독립 재현 둘에서는 같은 피처 값이 다섯 배까지 달라진다.

고치기 더 어려운 쪽은 사람 기준선이다. 챗GPT 공개 직전에 고정돼 있고 갱신할 방법이 없다. 그 뒤의 웹에는 이미 양쪽이 섞여 있어 새로 뜨려는 순간 기준선이 오염되기 때문이다. 탐지기가 세대 교체에도 잘 버틴다는 것을 보인 평가들을 열어 봐도 사람 쪽 표본은 하나같이 2022년 이전 것이다.

그러면 '사람이 썼다'는 것은 관측된 사실이 아니라 유효기간이 붙은 판정이 된다. 출처를 기록하라고 만들어진 규격 넷을 열어 봤다. 무엇이 만들어졌는지 적는 칸은 넷 다 있었다. 그 판정을 언제 어떤 잣대로 내렸는지 적는 칸은 하나도 없었다. 죽은 신호는 정확도가 떨어져 눈에 띄지만, 뒤집힌 신호는 확신에 찬 채로 조용히 틀린 라벨을 계속 만든다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Plenty of editorial guidelines still treat a run of em dashes as a sign of an AI draft. In Graphite's latest measurement, that rule now points the wrong way. Claude Opus 5.5 averages 0.015 em dashes per 1,000 words. Human writing in the same corpus averages 3.14.

The rule was not wrong when people learned it. Higher up the same table, GPT-4.1 and GPT-5 sit at more than twice the human rate. Those were the models in front of readers when em dashes became shorthand for machine writing. All four current-generation models in the table now fall below the human line. The signal did not fade. It changed sign.

What left was the punctuation, not the tells. The same study counts 2,548 words, phrases and frames that Opus 5.5 overuses against human writing, down 4% from 2,666 one version earlier.

The vacancy was filled. The phrase Opus 5.5 uses most disproportionately is "this matters," at 116 times the human rate. An em dash is a single character, easy to count and easy to strip. How a model closes a sentence is neither.

The conditions are narrow. The human baseline is web articles published before 30 November 2022, the AI side is single-prompt SEO articles, and chat was never measured at all. Two independent reruns that gave Opus 5 different writing tasks put the same feature up to five times higher.

The harder asymmetry is on the human side. That baseline is pinned to the weeks before ChatGPT and cannot be refreshed, because everything published since is already mixed. Even the evaluations that show detectors holding up across model generations draw their human samples from before 2022.

Which makes "written by a person" a verdict with a shelf life rather than an observed fact. We opened four provenance specifications. Each has a field for what was made. None has a field for when the verdict was reached, against which reference corpus, or with which classifier version. A dead signal shows up as falling accuracy. An inverted one keeps producing confident, wrong labels.

## Facebook (EN)

"Three em dashes in one paragraph. Did a model write this?"

A note like that came attached to a draft a colleague sent me this year, and it was not the first one.

I used to pause at the same thing.

Then I opened the two studies the marketing analytics firm Graphite published last month and this one, along with the raw feature file they released.

The instinct turned out to be a generation out of date.

Claude Opus 5.5 averages 0.015 em dashes per 1,000 words.

Human writing in the same corpus averages 3.14.

Roughly one to 205.

The old rule was not wrong when people learned it. The GPT models in front of readers back then really did use more than twice as many em dashes as human writers.

Nobody changed a word of the rule. The rule simply started doing something else. Today it leans toward flagging human writing as machine writing.

The part I keep sitting with came after that.

"Compared against what, and measured when?"

Saying that a piece of writing sounds machine-made is a comparison between two distributions. The machine side moves wholesale with every release. Consecutive versions of one model share less than half of their tells.

The human side does not move at all. It is pinned to ten thousand articles published before ChatGPT, and the web since then is already mixed, so there is no clean way to take a fresh reading. I have started calling it a frozen baseline.

Even the evaluations that show detectors holding up across model generations draw their human samples from before 2022. In this field, "definitely written by a person" has quietly come to mean "published before 2022."

This is where the study reaches our own work. DataClinic exists to ask what state a dataset is in before it goes into training, and training-data curation runs on a two-cell label, human or machine. Nobody touched the cell. The baseline it was filled against aged underneath it. That is not a missing value, a duplicate, or an out-of-range entry, so routine quality checks never see it.

We opened four provenance specifications as well. Each has a field for what was made. None has a field for when the verdict was reached, against which reference corpus, or with which classifier version.

So this is the question I now ask first when a dataset arrives.

The label that says a person wrote this: when was it applied, and against what?

Without that answer it is still a label. Nobody can say how long it stays true.
