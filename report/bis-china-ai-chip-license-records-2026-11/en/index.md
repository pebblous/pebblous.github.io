# China AI Chip Export Licenses Are Approved, and Not One Has Shipped

_In Brian Mast_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> The House Foreign Affairs Committee has released the Commerce Department's record of China AI chip export licenses application by application. Chairman Brian Mast used his authority under the Export Control Reform Act to publish a single four-page table on 19 November 2026. Each application carries its submission date, the exporter, the receiving party, a chip count and a status. The table, though, is current to 11 May 2026, not to the day it was released. Six months sit in that gap.

> Outside the table, the record was summarized as "879,000 approved." Yet all 21 approval rows carry a two-word status: **"Approved, not shipped."** The column that removed the most chips goes by a different name. It is returned without action, and across the whole table there is exactly one rejection.

> The line about 30.9% of approvals belonging to four companies on the Pentagon's Chinese military companies list also deserves a second reading. That list changed four weeks after the table's cutoff date, and it is not a list that gates export licenses in the first place. So read this table to the end and the question changes. From whether the US gave China chips, to what state the number we copied down is in. And whether our own record has a column for that state.

## What was released is one four-page status table

The document the committee posted is titled **"H200 License and Equivalent Application Status as of May 11, 2026."** It runs four pages, and every page carries a header beginning **"CUI//SP-EXPT."** That marking says the pages hold trade secrets protected by law and that further disclosure is barred absent a national-interest determination, and it cites Export Control Reform Act **50 U.S.C. 4820(h)(2)** as the basis for that determination. Every page also carries a diagonal "Chairman Mast" watermark. Mast called the release the most detailed disclosure in the history of the Export Control Reform Act.

The same committee has done this before. In October 2021 it released Huawei and SMIC license statistics obtained from the Bureau of Industry and Security. That set covered six months, November 2020 through April 2021, so it runs about as long as this one. What differs is the unit of tally. The earlier release grouped approvals, rejections and returns without action by company, with dollar totals; this one gives every application its own row. Whether the chairman's assessment holds against the full history of the Export Control Reform Act is something this article could not verify. Set against the nearest precedent, though, the unit of tally has plainly dropped one notch, from the company to the application.

Read the date in the title first. The release came on 19 November, but the state the table holds is the state as of **11 May 2026**. What happened in the six months since is not in it. That is why every figure this article quotes from the table carries an 11 May tag.

The table has seven columns: date submitted, exporter, data center operator or ultimate consignee, value, chip count, status, and the chips' final location. There are 54 rows, and the chip counts sum to **3,444,160**. Reading that number as China's requested volume gets it wrong, though. Mixed into the same table are five rows for data centers in Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand totaling **1,118,500** chips, 32.5% of the whole. How those five rows were handled comes up separately in Section 3.

The exporter column names three companies, not two. Alongside Nvidia and AMD, **Semsotai USA** appears on four rows. All four are applications filed on behalf of Sharetronic Data Technology, for 150, 32,000, 200 and 500 chips, and all four are still under review. Summarize the table as "applications from Nvidia and AMD" and those four rows disappear.

At the foot of the table sits a sentence rather than a row. The Bureau of Industry and Security authorized two licenses covering H200s and other chips for Lenovo, and those authorizations did not extend to sending the chips to Lenovo's facilities inside China. The stated use is building servers outside China and doing research and development on new products such as data center racks. Conditional licenses exist outside the 54 rows too.

### 1.1. Three questions the table does not answer

What is absent matters as much as the arrival of a per-application record. Three questions cannot be answered from this table, and all three are the questions most often asked of it.

| What is blank | What the table actually shows | The question it therefore cannot answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value | The value column is blacked out on all 54 rows | "How large is this deal in dollars?" |
| Approval date | The only date column is the submission date | "When was this license approved?" |
| Chip model | Only the exporter is named; there is no model column | "Is this row an H200 or an MI325X?" |

Source: the four-page table released by the House Foreign Affairs Committee on 19 November 2026. What state each of the three columns is in was confirmed by reading the table itself, and what the committee redacted (value) differs in kind from what was never a column at all (approval date, chip model).

The missing third column bites immediately in practice. The chips at issue are two models, Nvidia's H200 and AMD's Instinct MI325X, and the table does not say which row is which. So the sentence "879,868 H200s" does not hold. The 879,868 figure covers both models, and it does not split row by row. Some Korean coverage noted the same thing, that the table does not specify which chip each company applied for.

Nor is the table the whole of the applications. The committee said it holds applications not fully captured here, citing as an example an Nvidia application for 445,000 chips on behalf of Tencent filed on 19 December 2025. The Tencent row in the table with the same date and the same exporter carries a chip count of 75,000. How the two numbers relate is not settled by the public record, so this article will not guess. One thing is certain. **3,444,160 is the sum of this table, not the sum of the applications.**

Eleven days earlier the same House sent AI companies a letter asking about their incident reporting, and we recorded our reading of that questionnaire in [The House AI Incident Reporting Letter Asks Fourteen Things Every Time](/blog/house-ai-incident-report-letter-schema/en/). That was a document in which Congress **asks**; this is one in which Congress **releases**. Move from the asking side to the releasing side and the reading technique changes with it. With a questionnaire you look at what it did not ask. With a status table you look at what it did not record.

## Not one of the 21 approvals had left by 11 May

The status column on an approval row holds two words, not one: **"Approved, not shipped."** Approved, and not yet out the door. There is no exception among the 21. As of 11 May, of the 879,868 chips Commerce had licensed, not a single one had sailed.

This caveat separating approval from shipment is not new. When the 2021 statistics came out, the Bureau of Industry and Security attached the same note, that an approved license does not mean a shipment took place. What has changed is where the note sits. Then it was explanatory text outside the table. Now it is inside the status column, on every row.

The approved chip count and the shipped chip count come from the same 21 rows. Put them on one axis and only one bar has any length.
