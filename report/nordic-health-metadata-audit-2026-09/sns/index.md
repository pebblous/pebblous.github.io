## LinkedIn (EN)

Not one health dataset description in eleven Nordic catalogues carries all eight properties the European Health Data Space makes mandatory.

A researcher pulled 2,811 health dataset descriptions out of the national catalogues that the European data portal harvests from five Nordic countries, then opened those eight columns one at a time. The three health-specific fields came back at exactly zero, and applicable legislation, which records the legal instrument governing access to a dataset, reached 0.75%.

The empty columns follow a rule. Title, description and publisher, the fields someone types into a publishing form, were almost universally filled. The fields whose values have to be fetched from a controlled vocabulary or a legal register and bound to it were the ones sitting empty. The line falls between typing and looking something up, not between generic and health.

Reading this as a Nordic failure gets it wrong. We re-ran the same measurement across all 209 European catalogues and found that zero per cent compliance is the European median. The study is an unreviewed preprint by a single author who declares a commercial interest in the field he measured, so we only cite the headline figures we could re-query and reconcile ourselves.

Which leaves a question that is not about anyone else's catalogue. When your pipeline marks a field as passing, does that mean a value is there, or that an address was dereferenced and came back 200?

점검 리포트에 초록색 체크가 줄줄이 뜬 화면을 볼 때, 저 체크가 정확히 무엇을 확인한 것인지 되묻게 됩니다.

유럽 데이터 포털은 북유럽 다섯 나라의 국가 카탈로그 11곳을 수집해 둡니다. 거기서 보건 데이터셋 설명 2,811건을 꺼내, 필수 여덟 칸을 하나씩 열어 본 연구자가 있습니다.

여덟 칸을 다 채운 설명은 없었습니다.

흥미로운 건 어느 칸이 남고 어느 칸이 비었는지였습니다. 제목과 설명과 발행기관은 거의 다 채워져 있었습니다. 사람이 발행 폼에 손으로 적는 칸입니다. 반대로 통제어휘나 법령 식별자에서 값을 찾아와 묶어야 하는 칸은 비어 있었습니다. 저는 이 둘을 '타이핑하는 칸'과 '묶는 칸'으로 나눠 부르게 됐습니다.

카탈로그는 데이터가 어디 있는지 알려 주는 지도입니다. 지도에 지명이 적혀 있어도 그 이름이 어느 색인에도 없는 이름이라면, 지도를 읽는 기계는 그 자리를 찾아가지 못합니다. 핀란드 국가 포털이 그런 경우였습니다. 주제를 단 설명이 1,146건 있는데 전부 포털 자체 식별자를 가리키고 있어서, 유럽 보건 필터로는 그 1,146건 중 한 건도 걸리지 않습니다. 정작 그 포털 검색창에 health를 넣으면 57건이 뜹니다.

"우리 카탈로그의 '통과'는 값이 있다는 뜻입니까, 200을 받았다는 뜻입니까?"

## Facebook (EN)

The word that keeps catching on me lately is "pass."

When a validation report fills the screen with green checks, I find myself asking what each of those checks actually confirmed.

Someone went and opened the eight mandatory columns on 2,811 health dataset descriptions, drawn from the eleven Nordic catalogues that the European data portal harvests.

Not one description had all eight.

What held my attention was which columns survived. Title, description, publisher: almost universally filled. Those are the fields a person types into a form. The fields whose values have to be fetched from a controlled vocabulary or a legal register and bound to it were the empty ones. I have started thinking of them as "typed fields" and "bound fields."

A catalogue is a map of where the data is. A place name printed on a map still leads nowhere if the name belongs to no index, and a machine reading that map will not arrive. Finland's national portal is in exactly that position. It has 1,146 themed descriptions, every one of them pointing at the portal's own local identifiers, so the European health filter returns none of them. Type "health" into that same portal's own search box and 57 results come back.

The part I stayed with longest came after. The EU's authority vocabulary server returns HTTP 200 for strings it has never defined. On 1 September we put a string we invented on the spot into the same position. The same 200 came back, and the document that arrived with it held no concepts at all. To a validator that reads only the status code, that invented string is a valid theme.

"When our catalogue says a field passes, does it mean there is a value, or that something returned 200?"

That is why this is hard to file away as someone else's problem, sitting where Pebblous sits, diagnosing data and issuing quality certificates. The answer this paper reached was to record a verdict not as a property hanging off the dataset but as an observation carrying who made it, when, and against which requirement. Look it up and the idea turns out to have been standardised by the W3C back in 2016. Nothing to copy there, only something to adopt.

Counting green lights and writing down what those lights confirmed seem, on reflection, to be two different jobs.
