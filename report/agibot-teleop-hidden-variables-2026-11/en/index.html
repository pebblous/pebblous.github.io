Executive Summary

A batch of successful demonstrations records two things besides the job itself. One is the detour that exists only because of that equipment. The other is what the operator looked at before deciding. A team holding hundreds of thousands of hours of teleoperation logs turned around and examined its own collection setup — not through those logs, but through controlled experiments of 30 trials per condition. The AgiBot teleoperation team posted the work to arXiv on 17 November as a technical report, not a peer-reviewed paper.

The first of those two crosses into the policy almost intact. A policy trained on demonstrations laced with detours retraces about 90% of the extra distance the human covered. The shakiness of doubling back, though, is more than half gone: only 45% of the added jerk carries over. The defect comes out looking like smooth, confident motion, which is why watching rollout video does not catch it. The second thing never gets recorded at all. When the operator saw the block with their own eyes and picked the right arm, the policy trained on those demonstrations chose arms at chance.

And the defect may not be inside the data at all. Training data with mixed calibration batches held up in the configuration the report tested. What collapsed was the condition where the collecting robot and the executing robot disagreed on joint zero, and there all 30 trials failed. Nowhere in the dataset files is that mismatch written down. One question is left for the reader: which part of our data is the phenomenon, and which part is the way we collected it?

1

A ledger of 207,030 hours, and experiments of 30 trials per condition

The number this report leads with is a ledger. In its own words: "Our records contain about 20,253 task entries totaling 207,030 collection hours." (§3.3) Yet the conclusions in the report did not come out of analyzing that ledger. The physical experiments run almost entirely in units of 30 trials per condition, and the policy-training experiments build one model from 100 demonstrations. Tie the two numbers into one sentence and you get "we combed through hundreds of thousands of hours and found these defects" — which is not what the report says.

The ledger enters the analysis in exactly one place: counting how common each kind of task is. Measured by collection hours, 18.5% of the work involved mechanisms, sustained contact, or two arms holding one object, and therefore needed force control; another 38.7% would benefit from force control — wiping, pressing, folding, pulling, tight insertion. When a team is deciding whether to change its rigs, "do those tasks make up more than half of what we do?" is a question only the ledger can answer, and "so what happens then?" is the question the 30-trial experiments answered. Keeping those two roles apart is the honest move in this report.

Who wrote it is worth a moment too. The author line opens without a person's name. The organizational name Maniformer Real-World Manipulation Team comes first, followed by 41 individuals. The only affiliation printed at the top is the address teleop.agibot.com, and no formal company name appears in the body or in the acknowledgements. That address is published under the AgiBot teleoperation team's name, so this article calls the authors by that name — but it does not write "AgiBot announced." AgiBot has come up twice in this house: once for annotating failed demonstrations with the cause and whether recovery was possible, and once for a dataset that records the texture of contact. This time it is not a dataset release but a technical report, and it has not been peer reviewed.

The method puts three things in one place. Inverse kinematics computes the workspace on top of 25 published dual-arm robot URDF models; teleoperation experiments run with the control scheme varied; and a fine-tuned π₀.₅ policy goes onto a real robot to be scored. What the abstract ends with is a list of five things to measure before scaling collection: "They motivate evaluating effective workspace, control efficiency, observation completeness, calibration alignment, and motion quality before scaling collection, and addressing system-induced compensations at their source." What is new here is that each of those five now carries a number, and Sections 2 through 6 of this article follow them one at a time.

The five axes named in the abstract, and the value this report attached to each. Every figure in the middle column comes from a controlled experiment of 30 trials per condition or from a calculation on URDF models — none of them from the 207,030-hour ledger.

Having people drive robots by remote to manufacture training data is already a business in Korea. This house covered that scene while asking whether the cumulative data CrowdWorks advertises really belongs to robots. This article skips the part that explains the work and looks instead at what the equipment doing the work leaves behind in the product.

2

Reaching a point is not the same as being able to work there

The raw material for Section 2 is not robots standing in a lab but 25 published URDF models. Verbatim: "Using URDF models of 25 mainstream humanoid and non-humanoid dual-arm robots, we test multiple tool-axis directions at each of 10,075 positions in a sampled volume and use inverse kinematics (IK) to compute the fraction of feasible directions at each position." At each of 10,075 positions inside a sampled volume, the tool axis is swung through several directions and IK decides what fraction of them are feasible. No 25 physical machines were run. The list of 25 models is said to be in an appendix that the retrieved body text does not include, so this article refers to them only by the three categories the report compares: humanoid, lift-wheeled, and tabletop.

What the calculation separates is two different "workspaces." One is reachability — the fraction of positions the fingertip can simply get to. The other is orientation-aware coverage — the fraction where it can also hold the tool at an angle that lets it work. Arm length and reach radius, the numbers that land on a spec sheet, describe the first one. Lay the two side by side on a vertical slice through the shoulder and each body loses a different amount.

Measure one body with two rulers and the answers differ. Orange is shorter in all three cases, and the amount shaved off ranges from 14 to 20 percentage points depending on the body. This slice is cut at one shoulder height, so change the task and the ranking changes too.

Switch to tabletop tasks and the ranking does move. The tabletop body manages 80% pressing downward and 55% reaching forward. The lift-wheeled body is highest reaching forward at 68% and drops to 63% downward. The humanoid gets 52% and 58% respectively. There is no single answer to which body is roomier; the answer only appears once you decide which direction the work will come from.

Widening the workspace has numbers attached as well. Letting the shoulder move inside a sphere of 15 cm radius multiplies the reachable cross-sectional area by 3.4 to 5.5 times over a fixed shoulder. Extending the tool by 10 cm takes the number of reachable positions from 2,761 to 3,851, a gain of 39.5%. There are ways to multiply the workspace without building a longer arm — and that means the column worth reading when choosing a rig is missing from the spec sheet.

How many axes to give the waist carries a number too. Working from two human motion datasets (AMASS and PICO), the report splits torso-relative-to-pelvis rotation into three axes and takes the geometric mean of how much pitch and yaw move relative to roll. All four values exceed 1, falling between 1.21 and 1.88. The roles differ as well. Pitch relocates the shoulder, and yaw changes the direction the arm approaches from, while the job roll does can be covered by moving the torso or the legs. So if the waist cannot carry every degree of freedom, the report says to spend them on pitch and yaw first.

The values in this section attach to hardware, not to data. But the demonstrations that hardware collects become data. At a position the fingertip can reach without holding a usable pose, the operator twists the body, corrects the angle, and gets the job done — and that compensating motion stays inside the successful demonstration. What Section 4 measures is how much of it stays.

3

Same robot, same task, and the control scheme cuts the time in half

Section 3 holds the robot and the task fixed and changes only how the human drives it. Moving the body's parts simultaneously instead of one after another brought the median completion time for picking off the floor and placing on a table behind down from 35.7 s to 16.7 s. The "43-53%" in the abstract is this result, and there is one more condition sitting inside that range. The report measured three together, placing an intermediate condition between the baseline and full parallel control. The intermediate condition saves 25% and 28.5%. Copy only the abstract's number and the middle rung of the ladder disappears.

Task Baseline Intermediate Full parallel
Pick off the floor, place on a table behind 35.7 s 26.7 s (−25%) 16.7 s (−53%)
Wipe two separated areas 27.0 s 19.3 s (−28.5%) 15.3 s (−43%)

Median completion time, 30 trials per condition. Which body parts the intermediate condition couples together is not stated in the retrieved body text, so it appears here under the condition name alone.

The control scheme changes more than the clock. Switching to force control (compliant control) carried all four tasks to 30 successes out of 30; under position control only block stacking hit 100%, with door opening at 83.3%, whiteboard wiping at 80%, and two-arm box carrying at 86.7%. The same swap brought contact forces down with it. Peak contact force on door opening fell from 120.5 N to 34.6 N, a drop of 71.3%, and across the four tasks the reductions run from 55% to 71%. Success rate and contact force are not independent dials; they hang on the same handle.

3.1The lag the operator feels is a different number from compute time

One observation in the latency measurements is unusually practical: "Some filter configurations take less than 0.01 ms yet introduce over 200 ms of phase lag at particular motion frequencies." Running the filter costs under 0.01 milliseconds of compute, yet at particular motion frequencies the phase lag a person feels runs past 200 milliseconds. A log of algorithm execution times will never show that lag. And once latency drops below roughly 100 milliseconds, cutting it further barely shortens completion time.

What the operator is wearing also sets how long the data runs. Mean session length before discomfort is reported is 62.11 minutes on a 2D display and 63.14 minutes on a 3D display, against 18.71 minutes in a VR headset. Nine sessions per device is a small sample, but the direction is unambiguous. Put a headset on and the volume collectable in one sitting falls below a third. The same task then arrives chopped into shorter episodes, and those cuts stay in the data.

3.2The operator changes over 5 to 7 weeks

The least fixed component in a collection setup is the person. "Six operators collecting continuously improved from about 180–400 to 790–1,030 successful items per effective hour over 5–7 weeks." Six people went from 180–400 items per hour to 790–1,030 over 5 to 7 weeks. The report records this as the result of training them to overlap motions, a gain of 2 to 4.5 times, rather than skill accumulating on its own. What exactly "successful items per effective hour" counts, and what gets subtracted from "effective," is never defined in the body, so this article leaves the report's phrasing as it stands.

The same person doing the same task in week 1 and in week 7 produces different data. An inspection that only checks whether labels are correct cannot tell them apart, and the dataset files usually do not record which week the recording came from. Fix the collection procedure to a single variant, on the other hand, and you can isolate what caused what — this house saw that in a tactile glove dataset built from 500 hours on one rig. This section shows the other side: the price of not fixing it. That a rig leaves its mark on the data came up as well from the side selling data off an $8,000 dual-arm workstation.

4

The robot walks the extra distance, and its motion comes out smoother

The experiment in Section 4 is not an autopsy of demonstrations gathered in the field. Trajectories containing detours were deliberately mixed in, their share raised step by step, and four models trained. The design, verbatim: "four models are each trained on 100 demonstrations, of which 0%, 20%, 50%, or 80% are replaced by trajectories containing pauses and detours." Of 100 demonstrations, 0, 20, 50, or 80 percent were swapped for trajectories carrying pauses and detours. How those trajectories were produced is not stated in the retrieved body text. There is no sentence saying operators wandered on purpose, and none saying they were picked out of existing collection either.

Measure the four demonstration batches and the four policies trained on them with the same ruler, and two lines climb almost in parallel. As the demonstrations' mean wrist path grows, so does the policy's. Verbatim: "From 0% to 80% mixture, the demonstrations' mean wrist path grows by 0.215 m and the policy's by 0.194 m, i.e., the policy retains about 90% of the added distance." Of the extra 0.215 m the human covered, 0.194 m shows up in the policy.

Metric 0% detour 20% 50% 80% Retained
Wrist path · demonstration (m) 0.830.880.971.04 —
Wrist path · policy (m) 0.940.971.061.13 About 90%
Joint jerk RMS · demonstration (rad/s³) 3.293.824.735.46 —
Joint jerk RMS · policy (rad/s³) 3.233.283.984.21 About 45%

Four rungs of the detour ladder. The table reports two decimal places, which makes the gap between the two ends look like 0.21 m, but the figure stated in the body is 0.215 m. The right-hand column is the share of the increase between the two ends that remains in the policy.

What is worth something here is that the two lines have different slopes. About 90% of the added path stays, while only 45% of the added jerk does. Jerk measures how roughly a joint moves. It rises when a person hesitates, stops a hand and starts it again, and falls when the motion passes through in one sweep. The policy smooths more than half of that roughness away and keeps only the extra distance of going around. Joint acceleration, one level down, points the same way. The demonstrations' acceleration RMS climbs from 0.26 to 0.48 rad/s², while over the same span the policy climbs from 0.25 to 0.32.

On the left the two lines rise almost in parallel. On the right the orange line lies far flatter. That is one picture of the sentence that the extra distance crosses over while more than half the roughness disappears.

Here is why inspection goes blind. A defect that survives as clumsy motion is something an eye can spot. But what the policy inherited is a long way around, smoothed out, and therefore confident-looking. Reviewing rollout video will not screen that out. The thing to measure is not whether the motion is tidy but path length, completion time, and correction count. In the same experiment, the time from first movement to final release also grew from 25.3 s to 32.6 s, about 29% longer.

One more value can be read straight off the logs. The table above draws two metrics out of a ladder table in the report, and that table carries one row with no demonstration counterpart. It measures how far the velocity jumps at the seam where the policy stitches one chunk of motion to the next — the report's term is handover mismatch. It is defined as the magnitude of the velocity difference at the boundary between consecutive action chunks, divided by the velocity before the boundary. A policy trained on detour-free data sits at 0.36, then 0.58 at 20%, 0.75 at 50%, and 0.78 at 80%. It rises at every rung and more than doubles end to end. Joint jerk measures smoothness inside a single chunk and this measures what happens between chunks, so the two numbers look at different places. A policy trained on detour-contaminated demonstrations travels farther and jolts harder at every seam where motion is joined.

The ladder does not run one direction on every metric, though. The policy's count of brief slowdowns was 37.0 at the 50% rung and drops to 32.0 at 80%. This is not a result anyone can write up as "the more detours mixed in, the proportionally worse it gets." It is also striking that at the 0% rung the policy's path (0.94 m) is longer than the demonstrations' (0.83 m), but the report does not interpret that gap, so this article hangs no argument on it.

4.1Between "it survives" and "almost all of it survives"

That traces of a demonstration survive into a policy is something this house wrote down back in August, in the case where traces of deleted demonstrations stayed in a policy that had recovered its success rate. What that article asked was whether they survive — present or absent — and the answer was an audit verdict. These numbers answer a different question. How much survives, and the answer is in centimetres. Put the two side by side and you get "it survives" and "almost all of it survives."

That collection hardware leaves its mark on data came up in September too, in the article arguing that data gathered on equipment that cannot climb stairs decides where the robot will go. That one was about the paths not taken: the hardware's limits narrowed collection to places a wheel can reach. This one is about the path that was taken. The collected trajectory itself carries the inefficiency, and the inefficiency crosses into the policy.

Whether it can be fixed also splits the two. For demonstrations whose trajectory was fine and only the language instruction was wrong, relabeling lifted the success rate from 73.8% to 90.0%, because the defect lived in the label. A detour lives inside the trajectory, so there is nothing to relabel. The observation that a policy inherits its teacher's habits also turned up on the software-agent side, where the price was a score in an unfamiliar environment; here the price is extra distance and extra time.

5

A demonstration writes down the action, not the reason for it

The experiment in Section 5 shows that a demonstration a person would call flawless can be a blank to the policy. The task runs like this. A block is placed outside the robot's initial field of view, and the robot has to pick it up with whichever hand is closer to it. Which arm to use can only be decided by knowing where the block is. There are three conditions, 30 trials each: the operator watches the block directly while driving (LOS-known); neither operator nor robot sees it at first and the arm is chosen anyway (BLOS-known); and the robot tilts its body to look around before choosing an arm (BLOS-search).

Policies were trained on the demonstrations each method produced, and arm-selection accuracy was measured. Verbatim: "In arm-selection accuracy, BLOS-known (53%) and LOS-known (about 50%), both lacking the initial visual cue, are close to random guessing, whereas BLOS-search, which includes active search, reaches 100%." The policy trained on demonstrations where the operator looked with their own eyes and chose the right arm lands at about 50%, the same as guessing. Nothing is wrong with the demonstrations themselves. What reached the operator's eye simply never reached the robot's camera.

Both series are drawn because drawing one of them would let the picture lie. In the condition where every arm is chosen correctly, the pick-and-place success rate is 60%. Arm selection is the task's first fork, not the task.

The report explains the gap as a difference in what the operator and the model each hold. The operator uses "extra information to make the right decision, while the model lacks the corresponding observation." What BLOS-search captures more of is the process, not the conclusion. The robot tilting its body and shifting its viewpoint to look around enters the trajectory, and so "what did it see before choosing this arm" stays in the data. That is the line the abstract carries: "demonstrations must capture the visual information underlying action choices."

Observation completeness is not a call for more sensors. It asks whether the grounds for a decision were registered in the robot's observation. Every moment when the operator glanced past the monitor, asked the person at the next desk, or reached out on the strength of yesterday's attempt leaves nothing in the data. What remains is the trajectory that resulted from the decision, and the policy has to guess the reason from the result alone.

The phenomenon already has a name. Section 5 of the report cites de Haan et al. (2019) directly: causal confusion, where a policy latches onto some other signal that appeared alongside the real cause of a decision. The same section brings in Mandlekar et al. (2021) on demonstration quality, Belkhale et al. (2023) on data quality in imitation learning, Ross et al. (2011) on DAgger, and Swamy et al. (2022). The problem is an old one, and what this report adds is a number attached to it on a physical dual-arm robot.

6

The data is fine; the data and the robot do not line up

The defect Section 6 measures is not inside the data. The files are fine; the robot that made them and the robot that will move on them are running different joint zeros. Reading the ISO/WD 26264-1 draft in June, this house wrote that unless frames, calibration, and synchronization are recorded, the same motion becomes an entirely different signal to another robot. We can now put one price on leaving that field empty.

The experimental task is tube passing. The left arm picks a tube out of an orange rack and gives it to the right arm, which sets it into a navy rack. Let the two arms' coordinates drift even slightly and the exchange point is where it breaks. Four training sets were built: A, entirely clean; A′, 25 clean demonstrations plus 75 carrying an offset biased in the same direction; B, no clean data at all, only offsets of opposite sign averaging to zero; and B′, again no clean data, only biased offsets. A policy trained on each configuration ran 30 trials.

Training set Contents Success rate
AAll clean, matching the inference robot63.3%
A′25 clean + 75 with an offset biased one way66.7%
BNo clean data; opposite-sign offsets, zero mean20%
B′No clean data; biased offsets26.7%

30 trials per configuration. The difference between A and A′ is 19 trials against 20 — exactly one. That is not a margin for reading the mixed set as better. The line that separates them is whether baseline trajectories matching the inference robot remain in the data.

The conclusion the report draws here is conservative: "Historical data need not be discarded wholesale because of differing zero-calibration batches, but the dataset should contain baseline trajectories aligned with the inference robot." Old data does not have to be thrown out because zero calibration varied from batch to batch, but the dataset has to hold trajectories aligned with the robot that will run inference. The row to watch is B. The configuration that mixed opposite-sign offsets to average them to zero is the lowest of the four. This is not the kind of error that averaging makes disappear.

What collapsed was not the training data. The contrast condition put a joint-zero offset between the robot that collected and the robot that executed: "In contrast, in our test with a joint-zero calibration offset between the collection and inference robots, all 30 trials failed." One number has to appear alongside that to be honest about it. This task reaches 66.7% in its best configuration. It is not that something reliable stopped working; a roughly two-thirds thing went to zero. How large the offset was is not in the body text.

The top four rows and the bottom row are different kinds of change. The four are a problem inside the data; the last is a problem between the data and the robot. Opening the dataset files cannot forecast the last row.

Set this against the case from July where mixing in flawless data made the robot worse and the direction reverses. That one held that even clean data is harmful at the wrong ratio; this one holds that even contaminated data was usable as long as a subset aligned with the inference side remained. The two results do not collide because they look along different axes. One looks at ratios among the data, the other at the alignment between the data and the robot that executes.

6.1Of seven specifications, only one field is empty everywhere

So do today's robot data specifications leave a field where this value could be written? We counted seven of them across four axes. The result was not "nobody records it." The standards themselves — RLDS, LeRobot core, Croissant — do not require the collection setup as a first-class field and leave it to each dataset to record or not. But DROID writes a collector identifier and a collection date into every trajectory, and camera calibration has a field in both DROID and the AgiBot World data card. Exactly one axis is empty in every case: control scheme and latency had no structured field in any of the seven.

What should be recorded Counting seven specifications
Collection robot modelPresent in most, at the level of a string identifier. None require the URDF itself as a first-class field
Control scheme · latencyAbsent in all seven. The one axis where the universal holds
Camera calibrationA field exists in DROID and in the AgiBot World data card. What is empty is joint zero
Operator · collection dateNot required by the standards, but DROID records it per trajectory

A condensed count of RLDS, LeRobot core, Open X-Embodiment, Croissant, ISO/WD 26264-1, DROID, and the AgiBot World data card across four axes. "A field exists" and "the value in the field is right" are separate matters. Several follow-up studies have questioned the accuracy of DROID's camera calibration values.

In July this house named contact force, generation provenance, physical validation, and failure history as the shared gaps across six well-known robot datasets. One line can be added to that list: the collection setup itself — what equipment made the data, what control moved it, and where its zero was.

A move to fill the same gap appeared in another ecosystem the same month. Issue #5329, opened in the LeRobot repository on 11 November 2026, is titled "Datasets should record the calibration and servo gains of the robot that made them," and the accompanying PR #5330 proposes writing joint zero, P/I/D gains, and a robot identifier into every episode. Both are still open. The figures the proposal cites run like this: a policy at 98% on one Franka arm dropped to 18% on a second unit of the same model, and changing the proportional gain by 1.5× took it to 0%. This article could not trace those back to their original source, so they appear here only as figures the proposal cites.

The storage cost the proposal puts next to that is about 120 bytes per episode — roughly 0.001% of the video volume in the same episode. Writing it down cost 0.001% of the video, and not writing it down cost all 30 trials.

7

Why This Matters to Pebblous

From here on this is not what the report states but what Pebblous reads into it, so please keep the two apart.

Not how many hours, but what those hours recorded

When Pebblous looks at robot data, the question is not the volume but what that volume is a recording of. This report hands that question five fields: effective workspace, control efficiency, observation completeness, calibration alignment, and motion quality. None of the five can be measured by opening a dataset file, and once collection is over only their traces remain. If AI-Ready Data means data made ready before it goes into a model, these five are fields that have to be ready before collection starts.

The same success rate is not the same data

An inspection that only checks whether labels are correct catches none of these defects. A demonstration with a detour mixed in is a successful demonstration; a demonstration where the operator looked and chose the right arm is a successful demonstration; and a demonstration collected on a miscalibrated robot was successful on that robot. All three land in the same column marked "success." The number in Section 4 is the harder one. The policy inherits the distance of the detour while smoothing away more than half of the roughness, so the defect survives as smooth, confident motion. That is why watching video is powerless, and what to measure instead is path length, completion time, and correction count. This is the position from which data quality is a record of the process rather than a property of the result.

Four questions for anyone collecting or buying this data

For teams that gather robot training data themselves or buy it from outside, this report converts into contract clauses. All four are values that cannot be reconstructed later if they are not written down at collection time.

  • • Do the collecting robot and the executing robot share the same joint zero and camera calibration, and is that written down next to the data?
  • • Do the demonstrations contain what the operator saw? Were the grounds for the decision registered by the robot's camera as well?
  • • Beyond success or failure, are completion time, trajectory length, and correction count stored too?
  • • Is it recorded when and by whom the data was gathered? Week 1 and week 7 of the same operator are different data.

Where Pebblous stands

The place to stand is not on the side arguing for more robot data, but on the side that makes what the data recorded travel with it. Collection facilities are being built in Korea too. Covering the humanoid data factory going up in Daejeon and the budget for a Physical AI training center, this house noted each time that no one has yet taken responsibility for setting the format. This report has handed that format five candidate fields. Writing them down cost 0.001% of the video, and not writing them down cost all 30 trials. A record cannot be made after the fact.

Every figure and every sentence quoted from the report in Sections 1 through 6 was checked directly against the body, tables, and figures of arXiv:2611.22388 v1. What could not be confirmed is listed here too. The list of the 25 robot models said to be in an appendix, the training conditions of the fine-tuned π₀.₅ policy, the size of the offset used in the test where all 30 trials failed, and how the detour trajectories were produced are all absent from the retrieved body text. No declaration that the data and code will be released appears in the body either, so this article treats the 207,030 hours strictly as an internal collection record and makes no ruling on whether it is the same as the public AgiBot World release. The robot count and task count advertised on the project page appear to be counted in different units from the report's figures, so they are not used in the body. Pebblous has no interest in the report's authors, in AgiBot, or in any organization named in the body.
