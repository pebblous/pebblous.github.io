Executive Summary

The United Kingdom has decided to test, inside its own national screening programme, whether AI can take one of the two radiologist seats that read a breast screening image. The trial is called EDITH, and its first participants enrolled in Cambridge. It will recruit about 700,000 women aged 50 to 71 across 30 screening centres, funded with £10 million from the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR).

The part worth watching is the design, not the model. Each participant is randomised into one of three arms: the current arrangement, where two radiologists each read the image; an arm where a radiologist reads first and AI looks at the same image afterwards; and an arm where AI screens the image first and a radiologist reads it after. In every one of the three, the image passes under a human eye at least once. The primary outcomes are the cancer detection rate and the recall rate, and after screening ends the trial links to cancer registry records for four more years.

Sections 1 through 4 set out what the official announcement and the trial registration say. Section 5, which asks what a company handing one seat of its own work to AI can take from this design, is this article's reading.

1

Today Two Radiologists Each Read the Same Image

In NHS breast screening, a single image goes to two radiologists separately. Neither knows what the other concluded, and when the two disagree the case moves to an arbitration step. Screening covers women aged 50 to 71, who are invited every three years. Reading the same image twice is a safeguard built around how rarely a screen actually turns up a cancer. Miss one, and the next invitation is three years away.

There are not enough people to fill those two seats. Shortages of reading staff are an old story in the UK, which is why the question keeps returning: what if AI took one of the two? EDITH is an attempt to turn that question from an opinion into a comparison.

The first participants enrolled in Cambridgeshire on 23 November 2026. The trial registration number is ISRCTN81384017, the trial is run by the University of Warwick Clinical Trials Unit, and the sponsor is Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust. The chief investigators are Professor Fiona Gilbert of the University of Cambridge and Professor Sian Taylor-Phillips of the University of Warwick. Funding comes from the NIHR Health Technology Assessment programme, at £10 million. Some reports put it at £11 million.

Separately from the trial budget, NHS England is spending about £6 million to build AIR-SP, a research platform for AI in screening. It gathers screening images and reading outcomes into a form research can use. That money buys less for EDITH's own result than for the path the trials after it will travel.

2

Behind "AI Support" Sit Three Arms

Much of the coverage compressed EDITH into a trial of whether AI can replace one of two radiologists. That summary is not wrong, but it erases the heart of the design. According to the description published by the University of Warwick Clinical Trials Unit, each participant is randomised into one of three arms.

  • Standard reading. Unchanged from today. Two radiologists each read the image. This is the baseline against which the other two arms are measured.
  • AI after the radiologist. A radiologist reads first, and AI then looks at the same image. AI takes the second reader's seat.
  • AI before the radiologist. AI screens the image first, and the radiologist reads it knowing that result. AI takes the first reader's seat.

The last two arms both travel under the phrase "AI support", yet the human work inside them differs. When AI comes second, the radiologist finishes a judgement with nothing in hand and only then receives the machine's opinion. When AI comes first, the radiologist is looking at an image that already carries marks. Even with the same AI and the same person, there is no guarantee the two come out the same. Splitting the order into two arms looks like a decision to measure that difference on its own. This is not a sentence from the official announcement; it is this article's interpretation of the design.

A second choice stands out: the AI tool is not fixed to one product. Four systems that have passed accuracy checks in advance take part, and which tool goes to which centre is decided at random. As of 25 November 2026 the names of the four companies appear in no official material. Even without the names, the design says something plain. What is on trial is not one company's model but AI-supported reading as a way of working.

Pick one vendor and measure its performance, and the answer you get is whether that product is good enough. Mix four and randomise them across centres, and the answer changes to whether this way of working holds up anywhere in the country. EDITH is after the second answer.

3

What Earlier Trials Could Not Answer

Trials comparing AI-supported reading with human reading existed before EDITH. Sweden's MASAI trial published its results in The Lancet in January 2026. Across 105,915 participants it compared AI-supported reading with standard reading: sensitivity was 80.5% against 73.8%, with the AI-supported side higher. Specificity was the same at 98.5%, and cancers found late, between screening rounds, ran at 1.55 and 1.76 per 1,000.

On the numbers alone the matter looks settled. Yet a correspondence piece in The Lancet in June 2026 argued it was too early to recommend clinical adoption on that basis. Sensitivity and specificity are intermediate measures; how many of the extra cancers found were ones that never needed treating, which is overdiagnosis, has not been separated out; and mortality data does not exist at all.

The UK has an earlier case of its own. A study using Kheiron's reading tool Mia at NHS Grampian appeared in Nature Cancer in March 2026, reporting a 10.4% rise in the cancer detection rate across 10,889 participants and the potential to cut reading workload by up to 30%. It also pulled the wait for results down from 14 days to 3. The scale, though, was in the tens of thousands. Grampian does not drop out of EDITH either: the Scottish part of the trial is run jointly by the University of Aberdeen, NHS Grampian and the University of Glasgow.

EDITH's 700,000 participants and four years look like figures aimed at those blanks. A difference in cancer detection rate is a difference in rare events, and with a small sample it cannot be told apart from chance. Overdiagnosis and missed cancers show themselves years later, not on the day of the screen. That is why a step linking to cancer registry records for four years after screening ends was built into the design.

The same caution shows in the decision to set two primary outcomes rather than one: the cancer detection rate and the recall rate. Measure detection alone and whoever finds more wins. Lower the reading threshold, send out more recalls, and the detection rate rises with it, while most of the people called back turn out not to have cancer. Those people are left with a few weeks of waiting and further tests. Putting the two measures side by side closes off, in advance, the route of lifting one by wrecking the other. Choosing the measures is itself the act of deciding what will count as success.

The three trials differ in more than scale: the comparison structure and the follow-up period change with them.

Trial Scale Comparison structure Long-term follow-up
MASAI (Sweden) 105,915 AI-supported vs standard double reading Mortality and overdiagnosis unconfirmed
Grampian / Mia (UK) 10,889 Single-tool performance evaluation None
EDITH (UK) ~700,000 Three randomised arms plus four randomised tools 4 years post-screening, cancer registry linkage

4

Why a Human Stays in Every Arm

The trial asks whether AI may stand in for a person, yet for as long as it runs, not one of its three arms lets AI do that fully. Whichever arm a participant lands in, at least one radiologist sees every image. Anyone eligible can say they would rather not take part, and in that case they receive today's standard reading unchanged.

That limit sits against the policy backdrop. The UK National Screening Committee does not currently recommend using AI in the formal screening programme, on the grounds that the evidence is thin. With no recommendation in place, bringing AI inside national screening requires a shape in which failure does not reach the patient. Keeping one human in the loop across a trial of 700,000 people is that condition translated into design.

So the question EDITH actually answers is not whether AI may read alone. It is whether work once done by two people holds its results when one person and AI split it. The two sound like the same question, but responsibility rests in a different place. In the second, a human name is always attached to the final judgement.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Trial

In most companies the decision to hand one seat of the work to AI is made roughly like this. Run a pilot for a few months, hear from the team that it is working well enough, see what looks like a cost saving, and widen the scope. There is no comparison group in that procedure, because nobody measured how the same period went for the people doing the work without AI.

What EDITH spent money and time building is not a model but exactly that comparison group. Translate the design into four questions and it becomes a checklist for a company.

  • Is there something to compare against? If only the AI-assisted side is being measured, there is no baseline for judging whether things got better or worse.
  • Has the lag before results surface been counted in? Just as a cancer missed at screening appears years later, a wrong call in ordinary work usually comes back late.
  • Is someone positioned to catch failures? Keeping a person on every case looks slow, but without that seat there is also no route by which a wrong case gets found.
  • Is the bet riding on a single vendor? A score obtained with one tool is that tool's score, not the score of the whole approach.

All four questions can only be answered if records were kept before the decision was made. Which cases AI handled and which a person handled, which tool and which version was in use at the time, who overturned a result and when. Records like these are not assembled retroactively at the moment a judgement is needed. Like screening images, they have to be set down as they happen for a comparison years later to hold.

Not every organisation can run a trial of 700,000 people. Nor can a decision be called wrong simply because three years of comparison data are missing. What matters is knowing what the decision rested on, and looking at whether the records being accumulated now will serve as that ground later. This is the spot Pebblous points to when it talks about AI-Ready Data.
