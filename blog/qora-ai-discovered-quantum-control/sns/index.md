AI 에이전트 두 대가 사람을 거치지 않고 실험 장비를 돌려, 양자 상태를 더 오래 붙잡아 두는 제어 방법을 찾아냈다. 왜 그것이 듣는지는 설명하지 못했다.

워싱턴대 세인트루이스 물리학과 연구진이 11월 16일 arXiv에 공개한 QORA다. 이론을 맡은 에이전트가 제어 펄스열 후보를 설계하면, 실험을 맡은 에이전트가 계측 제어 소프트웨어를 직접 조작해 다이아몬드 시료에 쏘고 측정값을 되돌려 준다. 그렇게 나온 펄스열은 NV 센터 집합의 스핀 결맞음 시간을 18.6마이크로초까지 끌어올렸다. 사람이 대칭성을 따져 쌓아 만든 기존 최고 수준 펄스열은 같은 장비에서 9.8마이크로초에 머물렀다. 사람은 목표를 정하고 보고서를 읽는 자리에 있었다.

눈길이 가는 쪽은 배율이 아니라 순서다. 18개 펄스로 된 그 블록이 무엇을 상쇄하는지 에이전트는 내놓지 못했고, 사람 연구자들이 나중에 수식으로 분해해 붙였다. 논문은 그 절에 "Human interpretation"이라는 이름을 달아 뒀다.

보정이 어긋났을 때 결과가 얼마나 망가지는지에 관한 수치는 논문에 없다. 측정값이 곧 다음 설계의 입력이 되는 고리에서, 그 측정값의 품질을 보증하는 쪽도 같은 고리 안의 에이전트다. 로봇 시연 데이터도 같은 구조다. 카메라 보정이 조금 틀어진 채로 쌓으면 정책은 틀어진 좌표계 위에서 아주 성실하게 수렴한다.

닫힌 고리 실험에서 데이터 품질은 다 쌓인 데이터를 고르는 문제가 아니라 쌓이는 동안 결정되는 문제다. 페블러스가 수치 옆에 보정 이력을 함께 요구하는 이유도 여기에 있다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Two AI agents ran a lab instrument on their own and came back with a way to hold a quantum state together for nearly twice as long as the best human design. They could not say why it worked.

Physicists at Washington University in St. Louis posted the framework, QORA, to arXiv on November 16. One agent designs candidate control pulse sequences. A second agent drives the measurement software directly, fires those pulses at a diamond sample, and sends the data back. The sequence that emerged held spin coherence in a diamond NV ensemble for 18.6 microseconds, against 9.8 for DROID-C3PO, the strongest sequence human physicists have built. The humans set the goal and read the reports.

What stands out is the order of events. The agents never explained what their 18-pulse block was canceling. Human researchers worked that out afterwards with algebra, and the paper labels that section "Human interpretation."

The paper reports no numbers on what happens when the calibration drifts. In a loop where every measurement becomes the input to the next design, the agent producing the signal is also the one vouching for it. Robot learning has the same shape. Let a camera calibration slip while demonstrations pile up, and the policy converges faithfully onto a frame that is wrong.

In closed-loop science, data quality is not something you select for once the data exists. It is settled while the data is being made. That is why Pebblous asks for the calibration record to sit next to the number.

## Facebook (EN)

There is a subheading in the paper that reads "Human interpretation."

I stayed on that line longer than I expected to.

Physicists at Washington University in St. Louis published the work on November 16. Two AI agents, together called QORA, fired microwave pulses at a diamond sample and found, on their own, an ordering of those pulses that holds a quantum state together longer.

Longer than the best sequence human physicists had built, measured side by side on the same instrument.

What the agents could not do was say why their ordering works. Human researchers sat down afterwards and derived it, and the paper gave that section its own name.

I have started calling this the after-the-fact explanation. The answer arrives first, and understanding walks in behind it.

"When there is no one left to supply the explanation, what do we call the answer the machine found?"

Something else stayed with me. The signal guiding these agents was not a simulation score. It was what the instrument handed back. And the judgment of whether that reading could be trusted came from an agent inside the same loop, checking its own focus and its own frequency drift, correcting itself as it went.

The paper carries no numbers on what happens when that correction slips.

Teaching a robot from human demonstrations has the same shape. Let a camera calibration drift while the recordings accumulate, and the policy converges, very faithfully, onto a frame of reference that is wrong. The loss falls, the validation curve looks fine, and the hand reaches for the wrong place.

When Pebblous looks at data, the questions come before the number. What state was the instrument in. When did the last calibration run. Could another lab walk the same path.

In a closed loop you cannot sort for quality once the data has piled up. It is settled while the pile is forming.

The more answers machines find, the more room we may need to keep for the people who come afterwards to explain them.
