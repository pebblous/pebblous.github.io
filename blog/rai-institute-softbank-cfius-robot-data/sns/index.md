미국 로봇 전문지 더로봇리포트가 9월 18일 복수의 소식통을 인용해 전했다. 매사추세츠주 케임브리지의 RAI 인스티튜트 지분 47.5%가 대상이고, 금액은 양쪽 모두 밝히지 않았다. 거래는 지금 미국 외국인투자심의위원회의 심사를 받고 있다. 같은 시기에 지분 하나가 반대로 움직인다. 소프트뱅크가 들고 있던 보스턴 다이내믹스 잔여 지분은 현대차그룹이 풋옵션을 행사해 사들이는 중이다.

다만 어떤 시연 기록이 어떤 조건으로 함께 가는지는 공개되지 않았다. 심사가 어느 단계에 있는지도, 승인에 조건이 붙을지도 아직 알 수 없다.

규모만 줄이면 같은 구조가 국내 공장에도 있다. 우리 현장의 로봇이 남긴 기록의 보유자가 계약서에 누구로 적혀 있는지, 지금 한 번 펴 볼 만하다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Hyundai Motor Group put more than $400 million into a US robotics lab. The money is gone, and the lab is going to SoftBank.

The Robot Report broke the story on 18 September, citing multiple sources. At issue is a 47.5 percent stake in the RAI Institute in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and neither side has named a price. The deal now sits before the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States. A second stake is moving the other way at the same time: Hyundai is exercising a put option to buy out the Boston Dynamics shares SoftBank still held.

If there is a buyer when no cash is left, the price is not on the balance sheet. It is the researchers, the training code, and the teleoperation records built by people driving a robot by hand. Atlas, the finished machine, stays with Boston Dynamics. What crosses the border is whatever taught that machine to move. This is why a security review looks first at who can reach the data rather than at what the robot can do.

Which of those records travel with the company, and under what terms, has not been disclosed. Neither has the stage the review has reached, or whether any conditions will be attached.

Shrink the scale and the same structure sits in any factory. Whoever holds the records your robots leave behind is either written into the contract or left blank.

## Facebook (EN)

When a factory brings in a robot, the lines read most carefully in the contract are usually price and delivery date.

The line about who owns the records that robot will leave behind tends to go unread.

I kept returning to that unread line this week, reading that RAI, the robotics lab Hyundai Motor Group set up in Cambridge, is passing to SoftBank.

More than $400 million went in at the founding, and it has already been spent.

There is a buyer anyway. Which means the price is not sitting in the account. It is whatever the money left behind on its way through.

What held me was the nature of that remainder.

When a person takes a robot's arm and moves it through a task, the order and extent of every joint movement, and what the camera saw at each moment, accumulate as one bundle. I have started calling this material "the taught record." What a robot can do stands on how thick that record is. A thin foundation will not hold a movement up for long.

Atlas, the finished machine, stays with Boston Dynamics. What is changing hands is the place that taught it to move.

That may be why the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States is looking at this deal. The regulators seem less occupied with the change of ownership itself than with deciding who may reach those records afterward. Nothing gets seized. Access gets divided on paper.

So here too, the fate of the records is settled only as far as the paper settles it.

The slow part of our work at Pebblous, diagnosing data on factory floors, has always been here as well. Somewhere in a robot purchase agreement or a maintenance contract there is a data clause, or there is not. When there is not, a default takes hold, and that default usually hardens in favor of whoever physically holds the records.

"When the contract ends, which side keeps what our robots recorded?"

A lab in Cambridge changing owners and a single robot arm on a factory floor leaving traces seem to differ in scale more than in kind.
