전 세계에 연결된 장비 160만 대와 정형 데이터 16페타바이트를 가진 회사가, 피지컬 AI에서 어려운 것은 모델이 아니라고 말했다.

캐터필러 최고기술책임자 하이메 마이니어트는 8월 라스베이거스 Ai4 대담에서, 어려운 부분은 그 기술을 고객의 작업 현장과 업무 흐름 안에 집어넣는 일이라고 했다. 이 회사는 인력난과 위험한 작업 조건 때문에 광산을 먼저 무인화했고, 지금은 그 경험을 지형이 수시로 바뀌고 여러 회사의 인력과 장비가 섞이는 건설 현장으로 옮기는 중이다.

쌓인 데이터가 곧 답변이 되지는 않는다. 장비가 올려 보내는 값은 압력과 오류 코드지만, 기계 옆에 선 기술자가 필요한 답은 이 증상이 어느 지침서 몇 절에 해당하고 어떤 부품이 함께 필요한지다.

회사가 앞으로 5년간 1억 달러를 임직원 11만 8천 명 교육에 쓰기로 한 배경이 여기에 있다. 1인당으로 나누면 크지 않은 액수지만, 대상이 자율화 조직이나 데이터 팀이 아니라 전사다.

장비도 데이터도 갖춘 회사가 첫 예산을 사람에게 돌렸다. 피지컬 AI의 병목이 어디에 있는지에 대한 이 회사의 답으로 읽힌다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Caterpillar has about 1.6 million connected machines in the field and more than 16 petabytes of structured data behind them. Its chief technology officer says the hard part of physical AI is neither of those.

At the Ai4 conference in Las Vegas in August, CTO Jaime Mineart said the difficult part of autonomy and physical AI is incorporating the technology into the customer jobsite and into the workflows. The company automated mines first, where labor was scarce and conditions dangerous, and is now carrying that work into quarries and construction sites, environments Mineart called far more dynamic.

The gap shows up in the data itself. Machines report pressure, run hours, and fault codes, while a technician standing beside one needs to know which section of which service manual applies and what parts the job will take. Connecting the two still runs through people who have spent decades around the equipment.

That is the context for the money. Caterpillar plans to spend $100 million over five years training its 118,000 employees in AI, autonomy, and robotics. Divided per head the sum is modest, but the target is the whole workforce rather than an autonomy group or a data team.

A company that already owns the machines and the records put its first budget into people. Read that as its answer to where the bottleneck sits.

## Facebook (EN)

If you have ever stood next to a machine, flipping through a thick service manual with the job on hold, you know how long those few minutes feel.

The tool Caterpillar introduced earlier this year aims at exactly those minutes. A technician can ask out loud without putting the work down, and the assistant finds the right section among thousands of manuals, walks through the repair step by step, and names the parts the job will take.

But what the company's chief technology officer said in August was not a product boast.

The hard part, he said, is incorporating the technology into the customer jobsite and into the workflows.

About 1.6 million connected machines. More than 16 petabytes of structured data. A company with no visible gap in its assets pointed at the jobsite as the difficult part.

I keep thinking of that stretch as the space between a record and an answer. Sensors leave behind pressure, temperature, fault codes. A technician needs to know which section of which manual this symptom belongs to. The labels and context that bridge the two still live in the head of someone who knows which sound means which part.

"After decades of judgment accumulating on the jobsite, what form are we keeping it in?"

The line that stayed with me was the budget: $100 million over five years to train 118,000 employees. Per person it is a small sum. What lingers is that the target is the entire workforce rather than a data team. Moving field judgment into records, and then putting the resulting tool back into the field, does not finish inside one department.

The questions Pebblous returns to whenever physical AI comes up sit in the same place. Is the record coming off the field in a shape you can learn from? Does an expert's judgment survive as a label?

A company that already holds the machines and the data put its first budget into people. Whether that order holds for the rest of us is something I find myself asking again.
