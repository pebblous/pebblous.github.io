사진 장수는 30장으로 묶어 둔 채 나눠 찍은 방식만 바꿨더니, 소행성 자전주기를 되살린 횟수가 0번에서 10번으로 갈렸다.

브라질 상파울루주립대의 발레리오 카루바 등 10명이 9월 24일 arXiv에 올린 논문이다. 칠레 베라 루빈 천문대가 시험 가동 중에 남긴 관측은 천체 대부분이 하루 이틀에 몰아서 몇십 번 찍힌 뒤 한참 잊힌 자료다. 연구진은 관측이 더 쌓이기를 기다리는 대신, 가장 잘 찍힌 소행성 2026 DO14의 기록을 일부러 깎아 냈다. 전체 관측 기간에 시간 덩어리를 두 개·세 개·네 개로 놓고 그 안에서 30개만 남긴 뒤, 원래 자전주기가 되살아나는지를 열 번씩 확인했다.

덩어리가 둘이고 창이 가장 좁은 조건에서 고차 푸리에 방식은 열 번 모두 빗나갔다. 넷으로 늘리자 모두 맞혔다. 사진 장수는 내내 같았으니, 결과를 가른 것은 몇 장을 찍었느냐가 아니라 며칠에 걸쳐 몇 번으로 나뉘었느냐다. 덩어리를 늘리는 일은 관측 횟수를 늘리는 일이 아니라 자전의 여러 구간을 골고루 훑는 일이다.

그 기준을 실제 관측에 걸자 초기 후보 140개 가운데 36개만 남았다. 통과한 것들의 평균 관측 횟수는 앞선 퍼스트룩 자료에서 주기를 얻어 낸 천체들과 사실상 같은 값이었다. 방법이 요구량을 낮춰 준 것이 아니라 요구량을 드러내 준 셈이다.

논문은 어긋난 자리도 같은 지면에 적어 두었다. 네 가지 색 필터의 밝기 차이로 유형을 붙인 천체 가운데 기존 문헌과 직접 견줄 수 있던 것은 둘뿐이었고, 둘 다 달랐다. 그래서 이번 분류를 확정이 아니라 후보로 부른다.

성기게 찍어도 된다는 이야기가 아니라, 어디부터가 성긴 것인지에 숫자로 금을 그은 이야기다. 데이터가 부족하다는 판단이 느낌에 머무는지 진단이 되는지는 그 금이 그어져 있느냐에 달려 있다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Hold the photograph count fixed at 30, change only how those photographs are spread across days, and an asteroid's rotation period goes from irrecoverable to recovered on every try.

That is the result in a paper posted to arXiv on September 24 by Valerio Carruba of São Paulo State University in Brazil and nine co-authors, accepted at Planetary and Space Science. The observations the Vera C. Rubin Observatory left behind during test operations catch most objects a few dozen times over a day or two and then leave them alone for weeks. Rather than wait for the record to thicken, the team took the best-observed asteroid in the set, 2026 DO14, and threw most of its data away on purpose. They placed two, three, or four clusters of time across the run, kept 30 points inside them, and checked ten times over whether the original period came back.

With two clusters and the narrowest window, the higher-order Fourier method missed every one of its ten tries. With four clusters it hit every time. The photograph count never moved, so what separated the two was not how many images existed but how many separate sittings they were drawn from. Adding clusters does not add observations; it spreads the sampling across the rotation instead of revisiting the same stretch of it.

Applied to the real data, the standard left 36 of 140 initial candidates standing. Those that passed had been observed about as often as the objects that yielded periods in the earlier, denser First Look sample. The method did not lower the requirement. It made the requirement visible.

The paper prints its disagreements in the same place as its results. Of the objects classified from their colors across four filters, only two carried a published taxonomy that could be compared directly, and both conflicted. The authors call this round of classifications candidates rather than conclusions.

None of this says sparse sampling is good enough. It says, in numbers, where sparse begins. Whether "we don't have enough data" is a feeling or a diagnosis depends on whether that line has been drawn.

I have written "we don't have enough data" into a deck before.

I did not know, as I typed it, how much would have been enough. Nor did I write down where the judgment had come from.

A paper on asteroids, posted a few days ago by ten researchers in Brazil, brought that sentence back.

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile has not begun its main survey yet. What is accumulating now comes from test runs, so an asteroid gets caught a few dozen times over a day or two and is then left alone for weeks. Whether a rotation period can be pulled out of a record like that was the question.

The first thing the team did was not to gather more observations.

They took the best-observed asteroid in the set and threw most of its record away on purpose.

The photograph count was pinned at 30. The only thing they varied was how those 30 were divided across days and sittings.

With two clusters, and those windows opened as narrowly as they went, one method failed to recover the true period on all ten tries. With four clusters it recovered it every time. The photographs never changed in number.

There is no settled name for this move, measuring a limit without collecting a single new record. In my notes I have started writing it down as "cutting it back."

Missing data and lopsided data are different problems, and the first one's name tends to get borrowed for the second one's situation.

Held up against the real observations, the standard left 36 of 140 candidates standing. Those 36 had been observed about as often as the objects that yielded periods in the older, denser sample. The method had not lowered the requirement. It had made the requirement visible.

"The sentence in our own documents that says the data is insufficient: which experiment produced that judgment?"

This is why, when we run a data diagnosis at Pebblous, the first question is not whether a label is correct. With the guideline and the hands behind it on record, a wrong label can be fixed and a correct one has something to stand on.

What stayed with me was not the 36. It was that for the 104 objects that fell short, no value was written down at all.

To choose writing nothing, you have to decide first what would have passed.
