## LinkedIn (EN)

Meta confirmed on October 23 that it has acquired Sundial, an analytics company founded in 2021. Once the deal closes, Sundial takes no outside customers, OpenAI among them.

The product cleaned up data, settled what a metric meant and wrote the query that went to the database, so that any team could ask in plain language without going through a data team. Co-founders Julie Zhuo and Chandra Narayanan, both former Facebook employees, join Meta's newly formed DATA org with the company they built outside it. Neither side named a price.

The two figures are worth placing side by side. Sundial had raised $23 million in total, and across from that sits the $130 billion to $145 billion Meta has set aside for capital expenditure this year. What a company spending on that scale picked up was not another model but a tool for asking what the money did.

Of the four obstacles Zhuo said the product had automated away, the first two fail quietly. A broken query throws an error and a thin explanation makes a reader pause, but a wrong cleaning rule and a drifted metric definition both return a clean, rounded number. The person who asked cannot see which rules the answer stands on.

## Facebook (EN)

A number lands in the middle of a chart, rounded to one decimal place, and I almost never stop to doubt it.

Meta confirmed its acquisition of Sundial on October 23.

It is the analytics tool that let any team ask a question in plain language and get an answer back without going through a data team. OpenAI was a customer. Once the deal closes, those customers lose it.

In the announcement post, co-founder Julie Zhuo listed four obstacles the product had automated away. Cleaning the data. Settling what a metric means. Writing the query. Telling the story of the result.

The last two tend to get caught. A broken query throws an error. A thin explanation makes a reader pause.

The first two are different. A wrong cleaning rule still returns a number. A drifted metric definition still returns a number. Rounded, centered in the chart, with no warning attached.

There should be a name for these. A clean wrong answer.

"Can the person who receives this number see which rules it stands on?"

When questions went through a data team, there was a person sitting in that spot. Whoever wrote the query looked at the data too, and odd values usually got caught by that hand. Shortening that path is what self-serve analytics is worth. The gap opens in that same spot.
