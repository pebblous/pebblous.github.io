## LinkedIn (EN)

ChatGPT took 88 percent of what US House offices spent on AI tools over the past year.

CNBC reached the figure by pulling every charge with an AI vendor named on the line from the House's own disbursement records. By transaction count the gap is wider still: 798 charges for ChatGPT against 37 for Claude. The lead traces back to 2023, when the House Digital Service handed out free ChatGPT Plus licenses to a bipartisan group of staffers. The tool they learned on became the tool their offices later paid for.

What the spending data cannot show matters more. Staffers use these tools to summarize bills, draft hearing questions, and answer constituent mail, work that ends in a vote or in an official reply to a citizen. Nothing in any system records what went into the window or how much of the output survived into the final text. The Department of Homeland Security has already established that AI output is a federal record subject to FOIA, yet no AI-specific retention schedule from the National Archives has been published so far.

When procurement figures stand in as evidence of use, that is itself the finding: there is no record of use. Pebblous treats data quality and the record of which data informed which decision as the same problem.

## Facebook (EN)

Write to your representative's office and a reply arrives a few weeks later.

There is, at the moment, no way to ask where the first paragraph of that reply came from.

A tally published this week found that ChatGPT took 88 percent of what US House offices spent on AI tools over the past year. Reading it, what held me was not the vendor ranking but the caveat beside it: the count cannot see usage that runs through free accounts.

No charge means no accounting entry, and no accounting entry means the use leaves nothing behind. I have started thinking of it as "use that survives only as a receipt."

Bill summaries, hearing questions, replies to constituent mail. Text close to public judgment passes through that route, and what remains afterward is a subscription charge.

The legal question is not unsettled. The Department of Homeland Security established that AI output counts as a federal record and falls under FOIA. But a ruling only works when the record is actually there, and as far as published material shows, the National Archives has yet to issue a retention schedule written for AI output.

"Two years from now, can this decision be traced back?"

That is the question Pebblous keeps alongside data quality when it talks about AI-Ready Data: keeping a record of which data informed which decision. The House figures show, from the procurement side, what is left in your hand when that record is missing.

Adoption gets counted every quarter. The retention schedule has not arrived. How the documents piling up in between get classified is being decided right now.
