Executive Summary

A company that films people doing the laundry and the dishes at home and sells that footage to robot developers has raised a large round. Humyn Labs, based in Bengaluru, said on November 18 that it had raised $47 million in a Series A led by Accel. It builds its own two-camera rigs, films people in more than 20 countries across Latin America and Asia going about their ordinary work, and tags hand motion and object contact in the footage before handing it to frontier AI labs and robotics companies.

The three main collection sites the company named are Brazil, India and Korea. About 90 percent of its revenue comes from the United States, which means the places being filmed and the places buying the footage are not the same. What nobody has said is which Korean households are being filmed, how they were found, what is being recorded, what the people on camera agreed to, or how much they are paid. None of that appears in the original report or in Korean coverage, and that blank is what this article cares about most.

Sections 1 through 4 set out what has been reported. Section 5, which asks where Korean privacy law puts this kind of filming, and section 6, which treats the same matter as a data quality problem, are this article's own reading.

1

The Company Films Housework and Sells the Footage to Robot Companies

What Humyn Labs sells is labeled video. The company builds its own rigs carrying two cameras, hands them to people or straps them on, and records while those people do what they would have been doing anyway. Unstaged work rather than performed motion is the point the company stresses. Its software then processes the footage, marks what is happening in each scene, and separately tracks the path the hands took and how each object was touched.

Why robots need this becomes clear from the robot side. For an arm to fold a towel or set a cup in the sink, it has to watch a great many examples of a human hand drawing that trajectory and applying that much force. Material like that is not lying around on the internet. Text and pictures can be scraped from the web, but what someone's hands did in someone's kitchen comes into existence only once a camera has been brought into that kitchen. That is the business, and the 12 paying customers include frontier AI labs, robotics companies and firms building world models.

The company was founded this February by Manish Agarwal, former chief executive of the gaming company Nazara Technologies, and Ishank Gupta, formerly of AB InBev. The two had earlier co-founded a company called KGeN, and KGeN's contributor network across emerging markets now works as the channel for finding households to film. Local partners pick the locations and manage the shoots. Of the 51 full-time employees, most sit in Bengaluru.

2

Nearly Half of the Round Is Borrowed Money

The $47 million is two different things. The $26 million of equity was led by Accel, with Prosus Ventures, BeeGlobal, Bajaj Finance's Finserv Intelligence, the Bajaj Alts AI Opportunities Fund, Dallas Venture Capital, Lighthouse Canton and Jump Trading taking part. The remaining $21 million is venture debt, led by Lighthouse Canton with Trifecta Capital joining.

Mixing in debt says something about the shape of this business. The company pays the people doing the filming and the local partners on a daily or weekly cycle. Getting paid by its AI lab customers takes 30 to 60 days. Money leaves before it arrives, so cash has to sit in that gap at all times. The founders said that borrowing to cover the gap dilutes them less than selling more equity would.

The scale came out with the round. Revenue from April through October was $10 million, signed contracts come to another $20 million, and a further $20 million of deals has not closed yet. About 90 percent of that revenue is money paid by customers in the United States. The video leaves homes in Latin America and Asia while the money arrives from America, and the numbers now show that shape.

Korea Is a Main Site, and That Is Where the Record Stops

The original report named Brazil, India and Korea as the main collection sites. On how many Korean households are being filmed, or which partner handles the work, it added nothing. Korean coverage says no more. A Korean robotics trade outlet reported that the money will go toward developing robots that move through real environments in Korea, India and Brazil, and noted that the company has collected Korean voice data before. What was filmed in whose home does not appear there either.

Nor does any report explain why Korea. Brazil and India are emerging markets that KGeN, the founders' earlier company, already reaches, and Humyn Labs has said it uses that network to find homes to film. Korea does not sit naturally under that explanation. Incomes differ, and so does what a single shoot would cost, and yet no report says why Korea is one of the three main sites.

Hold the company's stated method against Korea and the picture sharpens. Filming unstaged daily life means that while the camera runs, the household goes on living as it normally does. A home holds more than the person who was handed the rig. A family member walks through, a child sits in the frame, and whatever hangs on the wall is recorded too. Agarwal, speaking about the people doing the filming, put it this way: "The worker is a worker who is incentivized, but he needs to be monitored and supervised for the right input." The person that sentence points to is the one holding the rig.

The original report does note that privacy and consent worries follow in-home filming across the industry. It does not describe what consent process Humyn Labs runs. There is at present no public document showing what a Korean consent form looks like, what the compensation is, or whose asset the footage becomes. No Korean regulator has announced that it is looking at this either.

India's Government Has Already Asked the Same Questions

What has not happened in Korea has already happened in India. According to a report in Entrackr, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology called in home services platforms in mid-November and examined the practice of collecting first-person video and sensor data from gig workers for physical AI training. Of the three companies at the table, only Pronto had actually begun filming inside homes. Snabbit and Urban Company said they would not do so.

The ministry raised three issues: whether worker consent is properly obtained, how the privacy of third parties such as family members caught in the frame is protected, and whether data gathered in India is being sold or transferred to AI developers abroad. The legal footing cited was the Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023. Experts quoted in the report said video, facial images, location records and sensor readings can all qualify as personal data. The ministry asked Pronto for a detailed note on its collection practices and standard operating procedures, and asked the companies present to disclose their data sharing agreements and retention policies. No formal order or enforcement notice has been issued.

Another company in the same line of work shows what an answer to those three questions can look like. Human Archive, based in Silicon Valley, said in May that it had partnered with Indian home services, hotel and restaurant businesses to put camera-equipped caps on gig workers and gather first-person video, with "more than 1,000 active headsets deployed" in the field. The company says all the material is anonymized and faces are blurred, and that its commercial contracts comply with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act. Households receiving the service get a choice: agree to be filmed and pay a discounted rate, or pay full price for a visit with no filming.

The same article records two other things. Workers are paid a base of "$1 per hour for participating in egocentric data collection," and it is unclear what they are told about where that video goes and what it becomes. Even at a company that has disclosed more of its consent design than most, the choice on the consumer side carries a price tag while the notice on the filming side cannot be checked from outside. How much has been disclosed is not the same as how carefully it was built.

Humyn Labs was not among the companies the ministry called in. There is no basis for carrying an inquiry aimed at other companies over to this one. But the three questions the ministry raised do not single out a company. Any arrangement that gathers video inside homes and sells it to AI developers abroad attracts the same questions wherever it operates. That nobody in Korea has yet asked them officially does not mean they fail to apply.

5

Where Does Korean Law Put a Camera Inside a Home?

A device worn on the body or held in the hand is what the Personal Information Protection Act calls a mobile visual data processing device. Article 25-2, which governs operating such a device for business purposes, is built around open places. On a street or in a shop it allows filming where the fact of recording is indicated and the person present does not refuse. Someone else's living room does not meet that premise. To film a person inside a home and use the result as data, this article reads the law as falling back on the principle that the data subject's consent is required.

The difficulty lies in how many people have consented. The person who received the rig and signed a contract clearly knows and takes part. The family member who walks behind them while they do the dishes is not a party to that contract. The second of the three questions India's ministry raised sat at exactly this spot.

The AI training exception that takes effect on March 9, 2027 might be read as the answer here, but the text puts it somewhere else. The amended Personal Information Protection Act, passed by the National Assembly on August 20 and promulgated on September 8, adds Articles 28-12 through 28-15, opening a path to use lawfully collected personal information for a purpose outside the one it was collected for, namely developing AI. Three conditions have to be met together, and the Personal Information Protection Commission has to deliberate and approve. First, the nature of the information, video or voice for instance, has to be such that anonymizing or pseudonymizing it would make the development impractical. Second, the processing has to take place in an environment with safeguards meeting standards set by Presidential Decree. Third, the risk of unfairly harming the interests of the data subject or of third parties has to be markedly low.

If a breach of those conditions surfaces later, it does not end with a fine. The approval is revoked and the ground the processing stood on disappears with it. This is not a regime that removed consent. It is one that put commission review where consent used to be.

Collection of the Humyn Labs kind is shot for AI training from the start. The heading on the new block of articles reads, in translation, "use of personal information beyond the purpose of collection for the development of artificial intelligence technology," and what the provisions address is personal information that has already been lawfully gathered. Since nothing here is being turned to a purpose outside the original one, the exception does not cover the case, and the question stays where it was: consent at the moment of collection. The exception is also no basis for any processing until March 8, 2027. Even after that, its outline depends on the Presidential Decree and the notices still to come. If a camera is running in a Korean home today, the standard that judges it is the current consent rule.

6

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Supply Chain

The shortage of robot training data has been discussed for years. What is new is that filling it has become an industry. A filming network spans more than 20 countries, money goes out on a daily cycle, and labeled footage crosses to the United States. Better material comes from more private spaces, and that is the condition this industry runs on. The scenes you can get only in a kitchen or a laundry room are the expensive ones.

Looked at from where data quality is handled, one thing is missing. The side responsible for quality and the side holding the rights are not the same side. Quality is made by the person filming: using their hands properly, recording to the end, holding steady enough for the footage to be usable. That is why the founder talked about monitoring and supervision. Yet nothing published anywhere records whose asset that material becomes, or what returns to the person filmed when a model trained on it is sold.

So a few things belong next to the figures when a supply chain like this one is read.

  • The scope of consent. Whether only the person holding the rig agreed, or whether the others living in that home were asked separately, has to be recorded with each piece of material. Without it there is no way later to tell which footage can be used.
  • The chain of purpose. How far the original consent reached, and whether it travels with the video when the video is resold or passed into training for another model, has to be written down.
  • The end point of compensation. Beyond what was paid for a single shoot, the contract has to address what the person filming gets when a model built on that material earns money.

These three lines are conditions on an asset rather than ethical advice. A model trained on video whose consent scope is unclear can later face a demand that the video be removed, and lifting one household's scenes back out of a model is effectively impossible. Because what is not recorded at the point of collection cannot be reconstructed afterwards, this is the same kind of problem as quality.
