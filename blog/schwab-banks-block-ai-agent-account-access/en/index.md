# Charles Schwab Blocks the AI That Logs Into Your Account

_Schwab says it turns away outside AI assistants in the tens and twenties every week, and four more U.S. banks followed within four days_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> On October 15, on the call for Charles Schwab's third-quarter results, chief executive Rick Wurster said outside AI assistants will not be allowed to log into client accounts. He meant the route where a client hands over an ID and a password and a program works the browser on their behalf. The same week, JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi took much the same position, and Wells Fargo changed its terms instead of blocking anything. This article looks at what each of those four days closed and what each of them left open.

> The reason the banks gave is liability: who pays when something goes wrong. A different number sits in the same reporting. JPMorgan, Bank of America and Wells Fargo hold $1.6 trillion in deposits that pay no interest, and if an AI assistant walked that money into accounts that do pay, the three banks would owe an estimated $47 billion more a year. Schwab did not bolt the door shut either. It is preparing a separate channel that opens balances and research while keeping money movement closed.

> Sections 1 through 4 follow what sits in the results release and the reporting. Section 5, which reads the decision as a question about data access rights, is this article's interpretation.

## What Schwab Blocks Is the Way In

Charles Schwab is a large U.S. brokerage. It holds tens of millions of accounts in which ordinary people buy and sell stocks and keep retirement money, and at the end of September the cash sitting in those accounts waiting to be invested came to $492.8 billion. On October 15, on the call for its third-quarter results, chief executive Rick Wurster set out one rule about those accounts.

![Charles Schwab logo signage at the entrance of its Manhattan office](./image/img-01-schwab-office.jpg)
*▲ Charles Schwab's New York office entrance | Source: [Wikimedia Commons (Ajay Suresh, CC BY 4.0)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charles_Schwab_Corporation_(55283756063).jpg)*

His sentence carried a clear condition. Schwab wants to deliver AI to clients in a safe and secure way, he said, and there is one way he does not think is safe: "just an agent coming to our site and trying to log in on behalf of a client. We are not going to allow that." That is where the line falls: on the way in, not on AI itself. The route Schwab closes is the one where a client hands an ID and a password to an outside program and that program works the screen as though it were a person.

The next day, in a Bloomberg Television interview, Wurster added the size of it. He gets a weekly report on the blocks, and the big agents are stopped in the tens and twenties each week. Not hundreds, not thousands. He read the number this way: people like AI, but they are not yet ready to hand over a financial account whole.

The case for blocking is all in one example Wurster reached for: Muse, Meta's personal AI assistant. He described a client giving Muse a password and letting it act on their behalf, and then asked what follows: "We can't control it, the client can't control it, something could go wrong and who's liable?" Schwab will not allow it, he said, to protect the client and to protect Schwab.

> [!callout]
> The block here lands on identity, not on permission. A program that borrows a client's credentials is written into the record as the client. Once something has gone wrong, the record alone cannot say whether that session was a person or a program, and if it cannot say, the responsibility cannot be divided either.

## Five Banks Drew Their Lines in Four Days

The decision was not Schwab's alone. Over the four days from October 13 to 16, when the third-quarter results of the large U.S. financial firms were stacked together, five of them said in turn how they would treat outside AI agents. They answered the same question, and the answers did not come out the same shape.

| When | Who, and where | What was decided |
| --- | --- | --- |
| October 13 | Jamie Dimon, JPMorgan, after quarterly results | Said he will not let outside agents into the bank's own systems: "you don't want rogue agents and there could be thousands of them." Third-party tools can already read account data; moving money and paying bills stay closed |
| October 14 | Brian Moynihan, Bank of America, CNBC interview on results day | "We strongly believe there should be a human in the loop to authorise money moving out of your account." Closer to a condition on approval than to a block |
| October 15 | Rick Wurster, Charles Schwab, on the earnings call | An agent may not log in on a client's behalf. The next day, on Bloomberg Television, he gave the weekly count and the plan for a separate channel |
| October 16 | Citigroup, in a statement to the Financial Times | Does not permit third-party AI agents or assistants to "log into a customer's account or transact directly" within its digital banking platforms |
| Effective November 24 | Wells Fargo, revised online access agreement | Did not announce a block. Its terms say a request arriving through a third-party AI tool may be handled as though the customer sent it directly, and place the duty of managing that tool on the customer. The bank also kept the right to limit or cut off a given tool |

Sources: each firm's results and the reporting that carried them. The Wells Fargo row is the bank's announced revision to its online access agreement, and the date is when it takes effect.

Read the table down the page and the differences in temperature show. Citi closes both logging in and transacting. JPMorgan says it will not take outside agents into its own systems while account data is already open to third-party tools. Bank of America sets a condition, a person in the loop, rather than a block. Wells Fargo left the door alone and changed the sentences around it. One Bogleheads forum member compared the two versions and counted the word "AI" 44 times in the agreement that takes effect on November 24. The May 21 version had it once.

There is an earlier event behind the cluster. Meta released Muse, its personal AI assistant, on September 8. Tell it what you want in conversation and it sorts mail, books appointments and orders things; within a week of launch it was the top free app in the U.S. App Store, and on the night of September 20 Amazon blocked it from buying, citing a breach of its conditions of use. Pebblous covered that block once already, in [an article that read it as a question of contract](/blog/amazon-blocks-meta-agent-access-rights/en/). This is the second front to branch from the same trigger, and the weight is different, because what sits behind the door is not an order button but an account with money in it.

## The Reason Given and the Money at Stake

The reason the five gave in public is broadly the same: security and liability. If an outside program comes in on a client's credentials and moves money, and the instruction turns out not to be what the client meant, who covers the loss? U.S. law as it stands gives no clear answer. According to the reporting, people at the Federal Reserve privately support what the banks have done while holding that the law needs to settle how a customer authorizes an autonomous system and where liability falls when something goes wrong. That legislation does not exist yet.

A different number runs alongside the same reporting. A bank that takes in outside agents stands to lose more than legal costs. JPMorgan, Bank of America and Wells Fargo hold $1.6 trillion in deposits that pay no interest, which is 16% of their combined liabilities. It is money customers leave in checking accounts earning next to nothing, and for the banks it is the cheapest funding they have.

That money earns close to nothing right now. The average U.S. checking account pays 0.1% a year. Move it into an account that pays interest and it earns 3.3% to 5.0%. Those are the two numbers the reporting sets side by side. The gap is not new, and customers were not unaware of it. Moving simply meant a person opening another account and setting up a transfer.

It is not hard to picture what an AI assistant could do with that money. Reading a balance and walking it over to a better rate is the kind of errand an agent is good at, and the kind a person keeps putting off. Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo, assumed 3% a year on that $1.6 trillion and put the extra annual cost to the three banks at $47 billion.
