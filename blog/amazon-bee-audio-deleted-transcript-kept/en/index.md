# Amazon

_A free account keeps 14 days of memory, and $11.99 a month lifts the limit. That written record also travels to Gmail, Outlook and Alexa+._

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> This article reads the two AI devices Amazon showed on 15 October through one question, which is where the data ends up. The Bee Frontier, worn on the wrist or clipped to clothing, is $74.99. The Bee Eclipse, a credit-card sized unit that attaches magnetically to the back of a phone, starts at $99.99. They are the first products to come out since Amazon bought the startup Bee in 2025.

> The promise both devices lead with is that no raw audio is kept. What the microphones pick up is turned into insights in real time and then deleted on the spot, and nothing is stored separately, as Amazon puts it. Something else sits outside that deletion. The transcripts of those conversations, and the summaries built from them. Of the two it is the transcript that Amazon says stays encrypted, under keys the device alone creates and controls. Free users keep 14 days of memory, and Bee Pro at $11.99 a month lifts that limit.

> Everything to this point is written in Amazon's announcement and in TechCrunch's report. The later part of Section 4 and Section 5, where the price list and the integration list are read as a signal that the priced asset is the text, is this article's own interpretation.

## Bee Frontier on the Wrist, Bee Eclipse on the Phone

Amazon acquired Bee, an AI wearable startup, in 2025. The Bee Frontier and the Bee Eclipse unveiled on 15 October are the first products since that deal. Both do the same job. They listen to the talk going on around you through the day and hand it back as summaries and to-do lists. What differs is where they sit on the body.

![Official Amazon product photo showing the Bee Frontier wristband in three colors (Lavender, Sage, Onyx) alongside the Bee Eclipse phone attachment in four colors (Pink, Gold, Silver, Black)](./image/img-01-bee-devices.jpg)
*▲ Bee Frontier (wristband, Lavender/Sage/Onyx) and Bee Eclipse (phone attachment, Pink/Gold/Silver/Black) | Source: [Amazon official announcement](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/devices/amazon-personal-ai-bee-frontier-bee-eclipse)*

The Bee Frontier is a $74.99 device you wear on the wrist or clip to your clothes. It comes in Onyx, Lavender and Sage, and carries an IP67 water resistance rating. The battery runs up to 7 days and charges over USB-C. Two microphones filter out noise, and the only things on the outside are a single button and an LED that tells you whether the device is on. It is on sale now.

The Bee Eclipse is a slim credit-card sized attachment that snaps magnetically onto the back of a phone, starting at $99.99. Silver, Midnight, Honey and Blush are the colors. The battery running 21 days or more is the sharpest difference from the Frontier. A release date has not been announced.

Put price, placement and battery side by side and the two devices split into different uses. One goes on the wrist and gets charged every week, and the other goes on the back of a phone and can be forgotten for three. The table below pulls out only the published specifications that bear on that choice.

| Item | Bee Frontier | Bee Eclipse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price | $74.99 | From $99.99 |
| Worn | On the wrist or clipped to clothing | Magnetically on the back of a phone |
| Battery | Up to 7 days, USB-C charging | 21 days or more |

The price shows which slot Amazon went after. The products TechCrunch set beside it are the Pocket AI at $129 and the Plaud Note at $159, with the Note Pro at $189. Amazon cut in underneath. For a device meant to stay on all day, the first thing the company lowered is the price that makes a buyer hesitate.

## What Gets Deleted When Amazon Says Deleted

Selling a device that is always listening means explaining first what happens after it listens. The name Amazon put at the front of this launch is Bee Private Compute. Conversations are handled inside a secure, isolated processing environment, and no one, Amazon included, can reach a user's data. That is the company's description.

Within that structure, what happens to the raw audio is stated plainly. Sound picked up by the microphones is turned into insights in real time and deleted immediately afterwards. The raw audio is not stored, Amazon wrote. Start recording a phone call and a prompt appears telling you to ask the other party for consent, and the device itself carries one power button and an LED showing whether it is running. The design lets anyone outside the wearer see whether it is on.

Read that far and the privacy question looks settled. If no recording survives, there is no audio to leak. That sentence is doing promotional work in this launch, and it leads whenever the product is set apart from its competitors. The subject of the promise, though, is raw audio all the way through. It says nothing about anything that is not audio.

## What Stays Is the Written Record

The same announcement also lists what is kept. Transcripts of the conversations, and summaries. The sentence about encryption, though, attaches to only one of those two. Transcripts are encrypted with keys that only the device creates and controls, Amazon wrote, which means nobody else, Amazon's own employees included, can open those files unless the user deliberately shares them. No such sentence was written for the summaries. Deletion is left open on both. Users can delete their transcripts and summaries at any time, the notice says.

A notice offering deletion at any time tells you something in reverse. It tells you there is something to delete. No such notice attaches to the audio, because the audio is already gone. The boundary between an original and a derivative is drawn right between the data that needs a delete button and the data that does not.

Break the handling of a single spoken sentence into steps and two paths appear. One ends at deletion, and the other begins there.
