## LinkedIn (EN)

The same H100, for the same hour, costs about 2.7 times as much from a hyperscaler as from a neocloud. That is where the two indexes stood on September 17.

Liquid Compute came out of stealth in New York on September 15 with a $15 million seed co-led by FirstMark and Chemistry. The company wants to build a market where compute capacity actually changes hands and then place a financial market above it, and rather than renting someone else's venue it has applications pending at the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission for both exchange and clearinghouse status. Two compute futures tracking Silicon Data's rental indexes are due to list on NYMEX on October 5.

The founders compare compute to a power grid rather than to oil for a plain reason. A GPU hour nobody uses today cannot be stored and sold tomorrow, it simply disappears, so trading it means writing the specification down first: which chip, in which place, and for how long you commit.

The venue is arriving ahead of the specification. The October contracts settle against a single axis, rental price, and the benchmark that measures what a cluster actually delivers is still being built. Two clusters of identical chips can produce meaningfully different output depending on how they are wired, as the index provider itself acknowledges.

Pricing compute is measuring compute, and measuring starts with writing down what to measure. That is the same problem Pebblous has been working on in data quality grading.

## Facebook (EN)

"Did we get a good price?"

If that question has ever crossed your mind while looking at a GPU invoice, what should feel strange is that there has been no way to answer it.

Two companies buying the exact same capacity could pay wildly different prices with no way to know who got the better deal. That sentence comes from the chief executive of the company that builds the price index.

On September 15, Liquid Compute opened its doors in New York saying it intends to turn that question into a market problem. Build the place where capacity actually changes hands first, then put a financial market above it.

The founders compare compute to a power grid rather than to oil, and the reason is plain.

A GPU hour nobody uses today is not something you can store and sell tomorrow. It disappears.

So the work of this market is not holding inventory. It is matching scattered hours to scattered places.

And matching means writing things down first. Which chip, in which place, available until when.

Only when that line, the one worth calling a grade sheet, is filled in can you say whether two offers are the same good.

But the price arrives before the specification.

The two contracts due to list on NYMEX on October 5 settle against one axis, the rental price. The benchmark that measures what a cluster actually delivers is still being built.

"Why do two clusters of identical chips deliver different output?" The layer that answers that question is being built after the venue opens.

From where we sit, having worked on data for years, the order is familiar. Pebblous has been holding the same shape of problem in data quality. Completeness, accuracy, whatever the axis, unless you set it first and write down how it gets measured, you cannot say which of two datasets is better.

The price always moves first. The specification follows late.

What to write down in the time between is something nobody has settled yet.
