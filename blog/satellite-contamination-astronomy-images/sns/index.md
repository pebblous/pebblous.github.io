## LinkedIn (KO)

한 변 10도짜리 하늘을 10초만 열어 두면, 그 사진 한 장에 인공 물체가 들어올 확률이 15%다.

IAU CPS와 체코과학원 연구진이 이달 초 arXiv에 올린 값이다. 러시아 MMT9 로봇 관측소가 2025년 한 해 기록한 밝기 측정 3,200만 건에서 하늘에 떠 있는 물체의 밀도를 구한 뒤, 여기에 시야 크기와 노출 시간을 얹어 계산했다. 넓게 찍는 서베이 망원경이 오래 찍는 망원경보다 이 문제에 더 취약하다는 것도 같은 계산에서 나온다.

분포를 가른 해는 2020년이다. 그 이전에 발사된 물체들 사이에서 위성 대 로켓 몸체의 개체수 비는 약 2대 1이었는데, 이후 발사분에서는 50대 1이 됐다. 로켓 하나에 위성 수십 기를 싣는 방식이 굳었기 때문이다.

개체수만 옮겨 간 것이 아니다. 위성은 로켓 몸체보다 밝아서 밝기 분포의 봉우리 자체가 두 등급 밝은 쪽으로 이동했다. 밤하늘을 어지럽히는 주범을 우주 쓰레기라 부르는 관용이 있지만, 이 데이터에서 잔해가 만드는 밝은 관측은 미미했다.

15%는 밤 시간 전체를 평균한 값이라 지구 그림자가 천정을 덮는 자정 무렵에는 낮고 박명 무렵에는 높다. 저자들은 사업자 계획대로 위성이 100배로 늘어나는 경우도 따로 계산했다. 미래 위성군의 밝기와 고도가 지금과 비슷하다는 조건 아래에서, 짧은 노출로 찍는 사진은 밤의 상당 시간 동안 거의 전부가 궤적을 품게 된다. FCC와 ITU에 접수된 라이선스 신청서에 적힌 위성 수의 합은 이미 233만 기다.

결측이나 라벨 오류는 수집이 끝난 뒤에 생기니 파이프라인 안에서 고칠 수 있다. 노출이 열려 있는 동안 들어온 빛은 그렇지 않다. 데이터 품질의 상한을 정하는 것이 정제 기술이 아니라 수집 조건일 때가 있다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Hold a patch of sky 10 degrees on a side open for 10 seconds, and the odds an artificial object lands on that one frame are 15%.

Researchers at the IAU Centre for the Protection of the Dark and Quiet Sky and the Czech Academy of Sciences posted the figure to arXiv this month. They took the density of objects overhead from 32 million brightness measurements logged by the MMT9 robotic observatory across 2025, then combined that density with field size and exposure time. The same arithmetic shows that wide survey telescopes are more exposed to this than telescopes that stare for longer.

The dividing year is 2020. Among objects launched before it, satellites outnumbered rocket bodies roughly two to one. Among objects launched since, the ratio is fifty to one. Stacking dozens of satellites onto a single rocket did that.

The counts are not the only thing that moved. Satellites are brighter than rocket bodies, and the peak of the brightness distribution has shifted two magnitudes brighter along with them. It is common to blame space junk for a cluttered sky, but in this dataset debris accounted for very little of the bright end.

The 15% is an average across the whole night, lower near midnight when the Earth's shadow sits overhead and higher at twilight. The authors also ran the hundredfold increase operators have filed for. If future satellites resemble today's in brightness and altitude, short exposures would carry a trail almost every time through much of the night. Licence applications already on file with the FCC and the ITU add up to 2.33 million satellites.

Missing values and bad labels appear after collection, which is why a pipeline can repair them. Photons that arrive while the shutter is open are a different matter. Sometimes the ceiling on data quality is set by collection conditions rather than by cleaning technique.

## Facebook (EN)

I had never thought about what happens in the sky during the ten seconds a shutter stays open.

That is how astrophotographers work. Ten seconds. The sky looks perfectly still for all of it.

A paper posted to arXiv this month put a number on those ten seconds. For a patch of sky 10 degrees on a side, the odds that an artificial object crosses the frame are 15%. IAU CPS researchers arrived at it by sorting 32 million brightness measurements logged by a single robotic observatory in Russia across 2025.

What stayed with me afterwards was not the 15%. It was where the 15% comes from.

Most of what we call a data quality problem happens after collection. A value goes missing, a label is wrong, a row lands twice. It happens inside the pipeline, so we fix it inside the pipeline.

A satellite trail happens outside.

For those ten seconds the shutter is open, those pixels really do receive photons. Picking the streak out later discards the contaminated part; it does not restore the signal that was covered. I have started calling this kind of thing "contamination upstream of collection." The phrase is not in the paper. It is a name I reached for while writing.

A brightness ceiling of magnitude 7, set by the International Astronomical Union in 2024. Orbital capacity assessments and a fairer allocation of finite orbits, recommended by the G7 science academies in Paris this May. Every rule that bears on this problem operates before the data exists, because astronomy does not own the conditions under which it observes.

The question Pebblous hears most often while diagnosing data quality is how this data can be fixed. Sorting what can be fixed from what cannot changes the conversation that follows.

"Which contamination in our data cannot be undone once collection ends? And who sets the conditions that produce it?"

A data team can answer the first question. The answer to the second usually sits somewhere outside the data team.
