# Does Scale AI

_Privacy group noyb has filed five GDPR complaints over Outlier, where 900,000 freelancers build AI training data under eighteen third-party trackers_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> The workplace where AI training data gets made has become the subject of privacy complaints in Europe. noyb, the privacy group headquartered in Vienna, has filed five GDPR complaints against Scale AI with the regulators of France, Italy, the Netherlands and Austria. The setting is Outlier, the platform the company runs. More than 900,000 freelancers there build the material that models learn from, and what they produce flows on to Meta, OpenAI, Google, xAI and Anthropic.

> The filings make three claims. The French complaint says eighteen third-party trackers run on the Outlier work screen, and that one of them, a session-replay tool, captures mouse movement, clicks and keystrokes and ships them outward every few seconds. Identity checks are handled by an outside company called Persona, which takes a video of the worker's face along with an ID document. And four requests to see one's own data went unanswered. Scale AI replies that the claims are unfounded and inaccurate, that every practice is disclosed, and that contributors consent to it.

> Sections 1 through 5 are facts confirmed by noyb's release and by reporting that worked through the complaints. Section 6 is this article's reading of what the case means for the side that buys the data. A complaint is not yet a regulator's finding, and that stays in view throughout.

## Five Complaints Land With Four National Regulators

noyb is a privacy organization based in Vienna, Austria. The lawyer Max Schrems founded it in 2017, and it has since brought complaints against big technology companies before regulators across Europe. On October 27, 2026, the group filed five complaints against Scale AI. Three organizations joined it: Intérêt à Agir in France, DiPLab, which researches platform work, and Reversing.Works, which has reverse-engineered working conditions out of the technology itself.

The filings were split across the regulators of France, Italy, the Netherlands and Austria, with two of the five going to Austria. Each complaint is brought on behalf of a single worker on Outlier, so five people in all. Article 80(1) of the GDPR opens the route for an organization to lodge a complaint on a person's behalf.

Outlier, where all of this takes place, is the freelance work platform Scale AI runs. More than 900,000 people are registered on it. Their job is to score the answers a model produces, write better answers themselves, and attach labels to images and text. Scale AI supplies that output to Meta, OpenAI, Google, xAI, Anthropic and U.S. government agencies. Meta bought 49 percent of Scale AI in June 2025, becoming both customer and largest shareholder.

What noyb asks the regulators for is a full investigation into whether the law was broken and, if it was, a fine. A third request goes with those two. According to The Record, the group also wants them to "study the way tasks are allocated by Scale." Trackers and biometric records raise the question of what gets collected; task allocation raises the question of who gets which job. The articles the complaints have in common are Article 5(1)(c) on data minimization, Article 12(3) on the deadline for answering an access request, Article 15 on the right of access itself, Article 25(1) on protection built in by design, and Article 28(1) on the guarantees required of processors. Added to those is each country's national implementation of Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, the clause that requires consent before a tracker is placed.

## The French Complaint Names Eighteen Third Parties

A tracker is a small piece of code loaded into a web page that reports what happens on that page to an outside company. The French complaint says code of that kind on the Outlier work screen reports outward to eighteen destinations. On the advertising and social side there are Facebook, LinkedIn, TikTok, Snapchat, Quora and Twitter Analytics. On the usage-measurement side, Google Analytics, Amplitude, Posthog and Segment. Then the survey tool Sprig, the error tracker Sentry, the customer support tool Forethought and the recruiting service Recruitics. Persona, the identity-verification company, is on the list as well.

The list is not the same in every country. The Dutch complaint rests instead on a log one worker made of their own device's traffic on May 22, 2026, and seven were caught there. Mickey Manakas, a noyb spokesperson who spoke to The Record, said there are definitely more, and that "the number of trackers identified varies by country and is not comprehensive since noyb focused primarily on social media trackers and analytics." Eighteen, then, is not a settled count for the platform. It is what one filing in France counted.

noyb's release picks out five trackers as representative, and one of them is missing from the French list: "the data analysis company Datadog." PPC Land describes it as a name "which appears in the second Austrian complaint." That is why Datadog does not appear among the eighteen laid out below.
