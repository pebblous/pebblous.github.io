## LinkedIn (EN)

Korean businesses were signing in to government websites with their customers' passwords to pull records, even where a safe official channel already existed.

That is the finding of a survey South Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission (PIPC) released on November 26. It counted 3,164 scraping setups run by 754 businesses against public websites. On Gov24, the main government services portal, more than seven in ten of the data types being scraped could already be sent through a secure channel. The regulator called it habit.

In scraping, the business rather than the citizen decides what data moves, when and where. When something goes wrong, responsibility is unclear. The PIPC will not ban the practice outright. Agencies will move to APIs and other formal channels in 2027 and 2028, though which agency opens which API, and when, has not been announced.

Handing over your login so someone else can enter as you and collect records is also how many AI agents work today. Seoul has now put a number on it for the first time. Anyone building on such data should be able to show whose consent it came with and which door it came through.

## Facebook (EN)

Have you ever pulled your documents into a loan app with a single digital certificate?

A few seconds later, your income statement and resident registration record fill the screen, one after another. It is convenient, so you rarely ask what happened in those few seconds.

In those seconds, the one logging in to the government portal with your certificate was not you.

This week, Korea's privacy regulator counted those seconds for the first time. Businesses were running 3,164 scraping setups that sign in to public websites on users' behalf. On Gov24, the main government portal, more than seven in ten of the data types being scraped already had a safe official channel. They were scraped anyway, out of habit.

One line from the survey stays with me. The business, not the citizen, decides what information is sent, when, where and why.

This kind of "proxy login" is not a strange story.

A person hands over an account, and whoever receives it walks in as that person and gathers records. It is much the same thing we now ask AI agents to do.

The government says agencies will switch to formal channels such as APIs starting in 2027. Even after the channels change, a question remains.

"Which door did my data come through? Can that door vouch for where it came from and who agreed to it?"

Working on data that goes into models, I keep arriving at the same thought: the shape of the door is part of the data's quality.
