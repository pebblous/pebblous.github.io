Executive Summary

Apple has opened a study in the United States that takes people's health questions. Announced on November 15, 2026, it is called the Apple Health AI Usage Study. Participants install a research app on an iPhone and ask about a symptom or a condition, and OpenEvidence, a clinical AI company, answers using literature such as The New England Journal of Medicine, the Journal of the American Medical Association, and Nature. Participants can also pick which HealthKit records to send along, among them resting heart rate, activity, sleep, and lab results, and enrollment is capped at 200,000 people age 21 and older.

One condition is attached in the press release. The study data will not be used to train AI models. Records that pair a health question with the body of the person who asked it are among the hardest to obtain on the open market. Apple is collecting that pairing at a scale of 200,000 people and has closed the training door first. What it wrote down to look at instead is not whether the answer is correct, but how far people trust it, how much of it they understand, and what they do afterward.

Sections 1 through 4 are the facts left by Apple's announcement, by press coverage, and by a June paper in Nature Medicine. Section 5 is the Pebblous reading.

1

A Study That Takes Health Questions From 200,000 People

What Apple opened is a study, not a shipping product. Install the Apple Health AI Usage Study app from the App Store, clear the consent steps, and you are a participant; from then on the work is asking whatever you have been wondering about. Whether it is all right to be sleeping this badly, what a lab number means, whether a symptom is worth a clinic visit. OpenEvidence writes the answer, and citations to the peer-reviewed literature behind it appear on screen alongside it. The app is not only a question box. Press reports list planning workouts and meals among its uses.

The conditions are US residence, age 21 or older, an Apple Intelligence-enabled iPhone (iPhone 15 Pro or later), and iOS 26 or later. Enrollment is capped at 200,000, and press reports say the study runs longer than 12 months with participants answering a monthly survey. Asking a question is not where it ends, in other words. Apple says the app carries safety guardrails meant to detect certain concerning questions, such as self-harm, and to suggest following up with a provider.

Several lines are drawn around the handling of the data as well. According to Apple, questions are stripped of identifying information before they travel to a secure third-party cloud, and OpenEvidence deletes queries and responses within 24 hours and does not train its own models on those queries. Whether to send HealthKit records, and whether to answer the survey, is the participant's call. Sitting above all of that is one sentence from the body of the press release.

"The study is designed from the ground up with privacy protections, transparency, and clinical guardrails, and study data will not be used to train AI models."

The last clause is the one that matters here. Apple's press release nails it down in the body of the announcement, under privacy.

One thing is worth keeping separate. Everything up to here is the design Apple describes, and no outside audit has confirmed any of it yet. The content of a promise and the evidence that the promise was kept are two different records, and the second has never been published. The name of the third-party cloud that holds the questions has not come out either.

2

A Tool Built for Doctors Opens to the Public

OpenEvidence, the partner Apple picked, starts from somewhere other than the companies that grew out of consumer chatbots. Apple's press release introduces it as "a leading clinical AI platform widely used by healthcare professionals." It is the tool a physician opens mid-visit to check the evidence quickly, and the structure of attaching a source document to every answer was built for that use.

Pebblous gave this company a piece of its own back in June. The story there was that instead of growing a bigger model, it licensed literature such as The New England Journal of Medicine, the Journal of the American Medical Association, and Cochrane and built a moat out of it (The Moat of the Medical AI 40% of US Doctors Use Is Licensed Journals). The subject of that piece was the doctor. In this study, the person sitting in front of the screen is a layperson.

That difference becomes the raw material of the research. What happens when an answer tuned for professionals lands in front of someone who is not one? Does the title of a cited paper raise trust, or does it go past unread? Between an accurate answer written in hard words and a less accurate one written in plain words, which does a person follow? What Apple says it wants to ask sits in that spot.

3

The Four Things Apple Says It Will Measure

The press release names four aims for the study: what builds a user's trust, what the quality of the responses looks like, how much of an answer a participant comprehends, and what everyday health actions follow from it. Sumbul Desai, M.D., Apple's vice president of Health and Fitness, said at the announcement, "At Apple, we believe responsible innovation starts with scientific rigor and clinical grounding." Desai added that "Health is deeply personal, and conducting a dedicated research study is the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend, and evaluate health AI in everyday life."

Not one of the four asks directly whether the answer is medically correct. Even the quality rating has participants themselves scoring accuracy, specificity, and relevance, which puts a layperson in the grader's seat. The axis of measurement stands on the side of the person rather than the side of the model.

That is where the decision to leave HealthKit sharing optional comes from too. Apple describes it as a way to see how the experience changes once personal data is added. The same person receives answers that know their sleep and heart rate and answers that do not, which makes this a design for checking, across 200,000 people, whether an answer that knows your records lands harder.

The press release adds one more line outside those four. It says the study will also explore how health AI can potentially help participants better prepare for clinical visits with their own doctors. Working out what to ask and what to bring before seeing your own physician puts the thing being measured past the screen and up to the exam room door. The app also guides users toward specific providers on questions that look concerning, as reported.

4

The Accuracy of That Tool Is Still in Dispute

Once a study measures trust, the record of the tool it runs on becomes a fair question. A brief communication from NYU Langone researchers, published in Nature Medicine on June 12 of this year, ran exactly that comparison. It put two specialized clinical tools (OpenEvidence and UpToDate Expert AI) and three general-purpose models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6) through three tests. Google's AI Overviews joined the last test, which brought the field there to six systems. The paper's title came down on the side of general-purpose models over specialized clinical AI.

On 500 medical licensing exam questions, all five systems gathered above 88%. Gemini scored 97.4%, GPT 94.2%, and Claude 90.2%, while OpenEvidence took 89.6% and UpToDate 88.4%. Where the test asks for knowledge, the specialized tools stay close behind.

The gap opened in the other two tests. On 500 HealthBench items, which score agreement with expert answers, GPT took 88.0 points, Gemini 79.3, and Claude 77.0, while OpenEvidence stopped at 62.6 and UpToDate at 61.3. The 100 real physician queries from clinical practice, graded blind by 12 clinicians, came out much the same: on a 4-point scale, Gemini 3.62, GPT 3.54, and Claude 3.52, against 3.24 for OpenEvidence and 3.17 for UpToDate. The paper separately flagged that Google's AI Overviews, at 3.27, matched or beat both specialized tools.

One more number stands out among the individual criteria. OpenEvidence averaged 2.84 on clarity, the lowest of the six systems. The authors read that result as a problem of delivery rather than a problem of knowledge. The same table also holds UpToDate declining to answer 19% of the queries, against 1% to 3% for the general-purpose models.

The argument did not stop there. Retraction demands and methodological rebuttals followed within weeks of publication, and OpenEvidence pushed back publicly that the design was biased. The Real-POCQi preprint that came next used OpenEvidence's own real queries and specialty-matched graders and reached the opposite conclusion. The possibility that standard benchmarks leaked into training is a limitation the authors themselves acknowledged. All that is certain right now is that which side is more accurate remains unsettled.

Apple's study turns independently of that argument, because what participants rate is how they themselves received the answer. The part worth holding onto is that 200,000 laypeople will be sitting in front of the tool that scored lowest on clarity.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Study

Seen from the data side, this announcement cuts against the default. Collection on this scale usually exists to make a model better, and all the more so for records that pair a question with the state of the body behind it. Data where the intent and the condition run together inside the same person is hard to buy and impossible to manufacture without consent. Apple is gathering exactly that, and it erased training as a use before anything else.

Narrowing the use widens the participation. What makes people hold back a health question is less the question itself than not knowing how far it travels. A pledge not to train on the data, along with deletion inside 24 hours, is the trade offered to shrink that feeling and seat more people. The number 200,000 only fills if the trade holds.

The sharper thing to notice is where the axis of measurement moved. Discussion around AI-Ready Data mostly asks how much better data makes a model. This study asks how much more data makes a person believe. The two answers need not agree. If an answer that knows my sleep record feels more trustworthy than one that does not, what raised that trust is familiarity, not accuracy.

So the findings can run in two directions. Learning what people lean on when they accept health advice can go into designing explanations that are easier to understand, and it can go into designing packaging that makes a wrong answer easier to believe. The same discovery serves both ends. That is also why measuring trust first, while the argument in Section 4 sits unresolved, catches in the throat.

What is my reason for believing health advice from an AI? Is it what the answer says, or is it the feeling that the answer knows my sleep and my heart rate? The answer to that question is what Apple wants from 200,000 people, and nobody loses by working out their own answer first.
