## LinkedIn (KO)

애플이 11월 15일 미국에서 연 건강 AI 연구는 참가자의 질문과 그 사람의 몸 기록을 함께 받는다. 아이폰 앱으로 증상이나 검사 수치를 묻고, 안정시 심박수와 수면 같은 헬스킷 기록은 내고 싶은 만큼만 골라 붙인다. 모집 상한은 21세 이상 20만 명이다. 그리고 보도자료에는 이 자료로 AI 모델을 학습시키지 않겠다는 문장이 붙어 있다.

질문에 답하는 쪽은 임상 AI 오픈에비던스다. 의사가 진료 중에 근거를 확인하려고 열던 도구이고, 답마다 『뉴잉글랜드 의학저널』이나 『미국의사협회지』 같은 출처를 달아 준다. 전문가용으로 다듬어진 답 앞에 이번에는 일반인이 앉는다. 다만 여기까지는 애플이 밝힌 설계이고, 바깥에서 확인해 준 기록은 아직 없다.

보도자료가 연구 목적으로 꼽은 항목은 넷인데, 어느 것도 답이 의학적으로 맞는지를 직접 묻지 않는다. 신뢰가 무엇에서 생기는지, 답변 품질은 어떤지, 참가자가 얼마나 이해하는지, 그래서 일상에서 무엇을 하는지다. 품질 평가조차 참가자 본인이 매긴다. 채점자가 일반인이라는 뜻이고, 재는 자리가 모델이 아니라 답을 받은 사람 쪽이라는 뜻이다.

그 도구의 성적은 아직 다투는 중이다. 6월 『네이처 메디슨』 비교에서 오픈에비던스는 전문가 답변과의 일치를 보는 헬스벤치에서 62.6점으로 GPT의 88.0점에 뒤졌고, 명료성 평균은 2.84로 여섯 시스템 가운데 가장 낮았다. 논문 저자들은 이를 지식의 문제가 아니라 전달의 문제로 읽었다. 이후 철회 요구와 방법론 반박이 이어졌고, 반대 결론에 닿은 프리프린트도 나왔다.

페블러스가 DataClinic으로 재는 것은 데이터가 모델을 얼마나 좋게 만드느냐다. 이번 연구가 재려는 것은 데이터가 사람을 얼마나 믿게 만드느냐이고, 두 물음의 답은 같지 않을 수 있다.

같은 발견은 양쪽으로 쓰인다. 더 이해하기 쉬운 설명을 설계하는 데도 쓰이고, 틀린 답을 더 잘 믿게 만드는 포장을 설계하는 데도 쓰인다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

The health AI study Apple opened in the United States on November 15 takes a participant's question together with the record of their body. Users ask about a symptom or a lab value through an iPhone app and attach as much or as little of their HealthKit data as they care to, from resting heart rate to sleep. The cap is 200,000 participants, all of them 21 or older. Sitting in the announcement is the line "study data will not be used to train AI models."

The answers come from OpenEvidence, the clinical AI a doctor reaches for mid-consultation to confirm a point, which hangs a source on every response, from The New England Journal of Medicine to JAMA. This time it is a layperson sitting in front of an answer built for professionals. All of that is the design as Apple describes it, and nothing outside the company has confirmed it.

The press release names four aims, and not one of them puts the question of medical correctness directly. They are what builds trust, how good the responses look, how much of an answer gets through, and which everyday health actions come of it. Even the quality rating is left to participants themselves. The grader is a layperson, and the thing being watched sits on the side of the person who received the answer rather than the model that wrote it.

The record of that tool is still contested. In a June comparison in Nature Medicine, OpenEvidence scored 62.6 on HealthBench, which measures agreement with expert answers, against 88.0 for GPT, and averaged 2.84 on clarity, the lowest score among the six systems tested. The paper reads that as a problem of delivery rather than of knowledge. Calls for retraction and challenges to the method came within weeks, and a later preprint arrived at the opposite verdict.

What Pebblous measures with DataClinic is how much better data makes a model. What this study wants is how much more it makes a person believe, and the two answers need not agree.

A finding of that kind cuts both ways. It can go into explanations that are easier to follow, and into packaging that gets a wrong answer believed.

## Facebook (EN)

There was a night I woke at three and went looking up a symptom on a phone.

Whatever I read, the deciding was still mine to do.

On November 15 Apple turned that scene into a study in the United States. You install a research app on an iPhone, ask about a symptom or a lab number, and OpenEvidence, a clinical AI, answers out of the medical literature. Resting heart rate, sleep, and the rest of the HealthKit record go along only as far as you choose to send them. Enrollment stops at 200,000 people age 21 and older.

What keeps a health question from being asked is rarely the question.

It is not knowing how far it travels.

The line about not training on the data sits in exactly that place. It is also the design as Apple describes it, with nothing from outside the company confirming it yet. A promise written by the company and a record confirming it from elsewhere are not yet in the same place.

What Apple wrote down to measure, though, was not whether the answer is right.

It is how much of it they believe, how much of it gets through, and what they do next.

That is likely why sharing the HealthKit record was left optional. The same person ends up receiving answers that have seen their sleep and heart rate, and answers that have not.

If the one that knows my record feels more reliable, what lifted that feeling may be familiarity rather than accuracy.

The number DataClinic returns for Pebblous says how much better a set of AI training data can make a model. There is no column in it for how much more that data makes a person believe.

"At the moment I decided to believe that answer, what had persuaded me?"

Apple is looking for that answer across 200,000 people. I would rather have my own first.
