## LinkedIn (EN)

A federal agency has offered to rewrite documents the executive order had specifically set aside as ones that need not be rewritten.

Executive Order 14434, signed on September 29, directs agencies to replace "Artificial Intelligence" with "Super Intelligence" in correspondence, websites, reports, and other non-statutory material. Section 2 then sets aside previously issued regulations, Presidential actions, contracts, grants, and other historical documents. At an October 7 webinar held by its Directorate for Mathematical and Physical Sciences, the National Science Foundation said a program director may work with a researcher to change "AI" to "SI" in grant abstracts. That is an offer, not an instruction.

NIST went the other way. It stamped a notice at the head of every page it renamed, naming the clause it relied on and saying that previously completed work and historical documents were left alone. Same order, same carve-out, two agencies drawing the line in different places.

Neither agency broke a rule, and the legal meaning is unchanged either way. The difficulty shows up in the counting. Answering "how much AI funding went out last year" comes down to counting words in abstracts. The Federation of American Scientists did exactly that, entering terms like AI and Machine Learning into the agency's award search and pulling grants from a single directorate. If an abstract read "AI" last year and reads "SI" now, and the database shows only its current state, anyone recounting last year sees the past through the name it was given afterward.

The change is neither complete nor a regular substitution, so rewritten and untouched records land in the same table. The remedy is not hard: leave the award identifier alone, bind the old name and the new one as aliases, and log what changed and when. The line NIST attached to each page is the minimal form of it.

## Facebook (EN)

"Would you like your program director to change the AI in your abstract to SI?"

Stand where the researcher is standing and the answer is nearly settled before the question finishes.

One word changes. The award amount does not move. The research does not move. It is harder to find a reason to say no than to say yes.

That is roughly the shape of what came out of a National Science Foundation webinar on the 7th. Junping Wang, a deputy division director for mathematical sciences, said a program director may work with a researcher to change "AI" to "SI" in grant abstracts and related documents.

The executive order signed at the end of September tells federal agencies to write "Super Intelligence" where they used to write "Artificial Intelligence." It then sets aside previously issued regulations, Presidential actions, contracts, grants, and other historical documents. The place the agency offered to rewrite sits on that list.

NIST handled it differently. It put a line at the head of every page it renamed, naming the clause it relied on and saying that previously completed work and historical documents were left alone. Two names now live on one page.

Neither agency broke a rule. The order does not require that historical documents be altered, and it does not forbid it. The legal meaning is unchanged.

The question about rules closes that quickly. The one that does not close comes after it.

"When rewritten and untouched records sit in the same table, which name does last year keep?"

A full sweep would settle this with one substitution rule. What is happening instead is closer to "selective renaming." The abstracts of researchers who ask get rewritten, the ones who do not ask stay as they were, and other agencies are not touching their records at all. Count only "SI" and the number comes out low. Count only "AI" and the rewritten ones drop out.

When Pebblous talks about AI-Ready Data, one condition always comes attached: the lineage has to survive even when the same thing goes by different names in different sources. This episode shows, at national scale, what blurs when that condition does not hold.

The remedy is not hard. Leave the identifier alone, bind the old name and the new one as aliases, and log what changed and when. The line NIST attached to each page is the minimal form of it.

A name is a description, but it is also an identifier. It is easy to change and hard to restore.
