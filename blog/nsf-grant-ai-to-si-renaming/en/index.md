# The National Science Foundation Offers to Change 

_Executive Order 14434, signed on September 29, left grants already issued alone, and at an October 7 webinar the agency said a program director can rewrite the wording with the researcher_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> This article reads one sentence spoken at an informational webinar held by the National Science Foundation (NSF). Junping Wang, a deputy division director for mathematical sciences, told researchers that "your program director may work with you by changing AI to SI" in grant abstracts and related documents. The occasion is Executive Order 14434, signed on September 29, 2026, which directs federal agencies to write "Super Intelligence" where they had been writing "artificial intelligence."

> The order limits where that applies. Section 2(a) names official correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, and policy documents, all of them non-statutory. Section 2(b) then sets aside regulations, Presidential actions, contracts, grants, and other historical documents already issued, none of which has to be altered. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) carried that second sentence onto every page it renamed, almost word for word. The offer made at the webinar points at grants, an item on that set-aside list. FYI, the science policy newsletter of the American Institute of Physics, wrote that "Renaming existing grant materials would go beyond requirements in the September executive order."

> That much is what the order text and the reporting establish. Section 4 of this article reads the same facts a second way. When renamed records sit beside records nobody touched, counting how much "AI funding" a given year carried gets harder. That reading belongs to this article rather than to any agency.

### Key Numbers

Three numbers: the deadline the order fixed for legislative language defining Super Intelligence, how many awards a keyword search for "AI funding" turns up in a year, and how many kinds of document the order says need not change.

## 'May Work With You' Is Not a Directive

The webinar itself was ordinary. The Directorate for Mathematical and Physical Sciences at the National Science Foundation held an online session to walk applicants through a funding opportunity in the mathematical sciences. The agency's own event listing puts it on Wednesday, October 7, at two in the afternoon, an informational webinar for a solicitation that bundles more than a dozen programs. FYI, the newsletter that reported the remark, gave no date and said only "Wednesday."

The remark FYI carried came from Junping Wang, a deputy division director for mathematical sciences. On the "AI" written into grant abstracts and related documents, he said, "your program director may work with you by changing AI to SI." In the same session he also said that AI tools inside the agency may be used to summarize parts of a submission "but not for making recommendations."

The sentence turns on its modal verb. This is an offer, not an instruction. Some abstracts will stay as they are because nobody asks, and program directors may judge the request differently from one another. The agency did not announce that it will rewrite its whole portfolio. It also means that rewritten documents and untouched ones will accumulate side by side in the same database.

Renaming is happening on more than one track. The same newsletter reported that the agency plans to rename eight proposal review panels to match new office names. That change comes from a reorganization rather than from the executive order. Behind both sits a year of criticism: science agencies issued far fewer awards in fiscal year 2026 than usual, and the agency closed the year with more than a billion dollars unspent.

## How Far Does Executive Order 14434 Reach?

Executive Order 14434 was signed on September 29 and published in the Federal Register on October 2 under the title "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence." Section 1 gives the reason for the new name: the systems now in use have outgrown what the words "artificial intelligence" were first meant to cover. The instruction in Section 2(a) is one sentence long. "To the maximum extent permitted by law, executive departments and agencies (agencies) shall use 'Super Intelligence' and 'SI' in place of 'Artificial Intelligence' and 'AI' in official correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents, and other non-statutory documents within the executive branch."

Section 2(b) draws the line in the very next sentence. "Nothing in this section requires the alteration of previously issued regulations, Presidential actions, contracts, grants, or other historical documents." A decision not to reach backward is written into the order itself.

Section 3 fixes the meaning as well. "Super Intelligence" and "SI" refer to the same technology that 15 U.S.C. 9401(3) defines as "artificial intelligence." The name changes and the legal scope does not. That arrangement is not meant to last, though. The same section gives the president's assistant for science and technology 60 days, which runs to November 28, to deliver legislative language establishing a federal definition of Super Intelligence. That language is to cover modifying or expanding the existing statutory definition along with the conforming amendments that would follow. Once the definition widens or shifts, the matter stops being only about a name.

The order's reach is narrow too. It covers the executive branch, and terms written into statute stay as they are. What anyone outside the executive branch calls the same thing is not something this order settles.
