## LinkedIn (EN)

The largest open weight ever released goes public today. Moonshot AI's 2.8-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 becomes something anyone can download.

The headline is not the price tag. Call the model through the API and your prompts and internal documents pass through servers under Chinese jurisdiction; hold the weights and run them yourself, and that traffic ends inside your own borders. The real value of an open weight is not the cost you save but where your data travels.

Then the physics arrives. Even quantized to 4-bit, the weights come to 1.4 terabytes, and keeping that mass resident in memory takes at least eighteen 80GB-class accelerators. Moonshot itself paused its subscription service when its own GPU supply ran short.

Hence the paradox of openness: anyone can download it, but not everyone can run it. Data sovereignty turns out to depend on whether you can carry the infrastructure it sits on.

## Facebook (EN)

When I hear "open weight," the first thing that used to come to mind was simply: now I can use it for free.

Watching today's Kimi K3 release, I found myself thinking about that word again.

2.8 trillion parameters, the largest open weight in history. The file is there for anyone to download.

And yet what stayed with me was not the price.

Call this model through the API, and the sentences I write and the documents my company holds make one pass through a server under Chinese jurisdiction. Hold the weights and run them inside our own walls, and that traffic never crosses the border.

"Maybe the real worth of an open weight isn't what it costs, but where my data goes."

That part is the good news. The trouble was the weight itself.

Even trimmed to 4-bit, the model comes to 1.4 terabytes. To keep that resident in memory you have to lash together dozens of high-end accelerators. Even the company that built it had to pause its own service when the GPUs ran short.

So I've started calling this the paradox of openness. Anyone can download it; not everyone can run it. The decision to keep your data inside your own border quietly stalls at a plainer question: can you carry the infrastructure that decision rests on?

The door is open. But opening a door and making a home behind it may be two different things. The ability to say where your data is right now and whose hands it has passed through — that is the place Pebblous has spent a long time looking at.
