소재마다 학습 데이터를 새로 만들지 않고도, 광학 현미경으로 찍은 단층 이황화몰리브덴에서 입자와 배경을 가르는 정확도가 99.7%에 닿았다.

듀크대학 연구진이 2025년 내놓은 자율 특성화 체계 ATOMIC의 수치다. 전자현미경 쪽을 훑은 리뷰도 같은 방향을 가리킨다. 에브로피 툴케리두를 비롯한 네 사람이 7월 11일 프리프린트로 공개한 리뷰는 현미경이 서술적인 영상 기법에서 구조 해석과 동적 분석, 과학적 추론을 위한 데이터 기반 플랫폼으로 넓어졌다고 적었다.

앞의 둘과 마지막 하나 사이에 간격이 있다. 사진 안에서 닫히는 물음은 이미 기계의 몫이다. 입자가 몇 개인지, 지름이 얼마인지, 결함이 어느 자리에 있는지는 픽셀을 다 읽으면 결정되고, 맞았는지는 같은 사진에 전문가가 그어 둔 경계선과 견주어 가린다. 왜 이 구조가 나왔는지, 이 구조가 무엇을 할 수 있는지는 다르다. 전구체와 온도와 시간, 그리고 성능 측정값이 있어야 답이 나오는데 그 값들은 픽셀 어디에도 없다. 분할 정확도를 99.9%로 끌어올려도 이 선은 넘지 못한다.

그 값을 자동으로 남겨 줄 자리로 꼽히는 것이 자율 실험실이다. 다만 2025년 네이처 커뮤니케이션스에 실린 논문은 지금의 하드웨어가 실험 메타데이터 대부분을 담지 못한다고 적었다. 온도나 상대습도처럼 나중에 같은 결과를 다시 만들어 보려면 꼭 있어야 하는 값이 상용 장비에서 흔히 빠진다.

미국 에너지부가 과학 AI에 건 첫 3억 2천만 달러에서도 51개 과제 중 14개가 로봇과 자동화 실험실, 대형 실험의 자율 제어로 갔다. 더 큰 모델은 사진을 더 잘 읽게 한다. 사진 밖의 조건을 대신 적어 주지는 않는다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

With no training data built for the material in front of it, a system separating particles from background in optical images of monolayer molybdenum disulfide ran at 99.7 percent accuracy.

The figure belongs to ATOMIC, an autonomous microscopy framework from a Duke University group in 2025. A review of the electron microscopy side points the same way. Evropi Toulkeridou and three colleagues released it as a preprint on July 11, writing that machine learning has widened microscopy from a descriptive imaging technique into a data-driven platform for structural interpretation, dynamic analysis and scientific inference.

A gap sits between the first two of those and the last. Questions that close inside a picture already belong to the machine. How many particles, what diameter, which site holds a defect: read every pixel and the answer is settled, and a boundary a human expert drew on the same image decides whether it was right. Why a structure appeared, and what it can do, are different. Precursor, temperature, time and a performance measurement have to exist first, and none of them is in the pixels. Raising segmentation accuracy to 99.9 percent does not cross that line.

The self-driving lab is the candidate for recording those values automatically. A 2025 paper in Nature Communications states that current hardware fails to capture most experimental metadata. Temperature, relative humidity and the other values anyone would need to reproduce a result are exactly what commercial instruments tend to skip.

In the first $320 million the US Department of Energy put into AI for science, 14 of the 51 selected projects went to robotics, automated labs and autonomous control of large experiments. A bigger model reads a picture better. It does not write down the conditions outside the frame.

At DataClinic, Pebblous looks at two things together: the quality of AI training data and the record attached to it.

## Facebook (EN)

An image keeps only what was in front of the lens.

How many particles are there, and how wide: a single frame settles both, and a boundary an expert drew on the same frame says whether the measurement was right. Machines are good at this already. Last year a Duke University group reached 99.7 percent accuracy separating particles from background in optical images of monolayer molybdenum disulfide, with no training data made for that material.

The questions an image never answers are of another kind.

At what temperature, over how many hours, from which precursor the sample came, and how it performed once it existed. However sharp the picture, those values are not in the pixels.

A review of nanoparticle electron microscopy AI, released in July, names that absence as the next task. Its proposal is to link synthesis conditions, structure and function into one chain, which asks for the experiment's record to travel beside the image.

The self-driving lab is the candidate for keeping that record automatically. A paper in Nature Communications last year states that today's instruments miss most experimental metadata. Temperature and humidity belong to that set, and so does every other value a person would need to make the same result happen twice.

Pebblous puts a quality figure on AI training data with DataClinic. Field names and units are usually settled long before any of that data reaches us. The microscope side is standing at the same spot.

"When was the last time you saw an announcement about which lab agreed to record which fields, under which names?"

Announcements carrying a model name arrive every month.

Nothing of that kind attaches to the news that one more field has been added to a record sheet.
