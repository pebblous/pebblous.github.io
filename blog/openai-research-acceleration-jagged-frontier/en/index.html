Executive Summary

OpenAI has published, for the second time, how much work its own researchers hand over to coding agents. According to the report it released on November 18, the share of research tasks that would take a person two to four hours and that an agent finished with no human stepping in rose from 23 percent in March to 46 percent in October. Four-to-eight-hour tasks went from 13 percent to 35 percent.

The part worth looking at is not the number but the sentence the report placed right beside it. As agents became more capable, it says, researchers may also have changed the kinds of tasks they hand over within each time bucket. Which means OpenAI cannot tell how much of the rise came from the agent getting better and how much from the people choosing differently. Broken out by task type, monitoring a training run clears 79 percent while writing inference and serving code clears 25 percent, a gap of more than three to one.

Sections 1 through 3 set out what the report says. Section 4, which holds that the same trap sits inside corporate "AI impact" numbers, is this article's own reading.

1

The Success Rate Doubled in Seven Months

On November 18 OpenAI published a report titled "Research acceleration: The jagged frontier." It is the second report on how coding agents are changing its own research organization, following the one in September that said the company had entered an "automated research intern" stage. This one covers January through October 18, 2026.

The metric at the center of the report is the zero-intervention success rate. It asks only whether a task a researcher handed to an agent was finished without a person stepping in partway. Tasks were bucketed by how long they would have taken a person to do directly, and the success rate was tracked month by month within each bucket.

For work that would take two to four hours, the zero-intervention success rate went from 23 percent in March to 46 percent in October. Four-to-eight-hour work rose from 13 percent to 35 percent. Short jobs under fifteen minutes were high to begin with and moved from around 77 percent to around 82 percent, while jobs over eight hours climbed from roughly 10 percent to roughly 29 percent. The ordering holds throughout: the longer the task, the lower the success rate.

The volume grew alongside it. The amount of work a single researcher handed to agents in a month went from half a day's worth in May to 5.7 days' worth in October. Median daily agent spend rose 62 percent to $2,400, and researchers in the top 10 percent spent $23,000. Priced at API list rates, the agents used internally now cost about twice what the company pays researchers in non-equity compensation. All of it, though, consumes only 2 percent of the company's total research compute.

The report also records where that growing use led. Over the four weeks ending October 18, a researcher running experiments logged 1.68 times as many experiment records as the 2025 baseline. Research time booked to safety and alignment work, counted in researcher-weeks, rose 77 percent between January and September. On a capability index that folds several evaluations into one score, OpenAI models have climbed 16.4 points a year since August 2025, steeper than the 14 points a year the external research group Epoch has measured since late 2024.

Before anything else, these numbers need a boundary drawn around them. The 46 percent is a share of the tasks researchers chose to delegate; it does not mean agents do 46 percent of OpenAI's research. The report makes that point and so does the outside analysis of it. On top of that, the figure is OpenAI's own estimate from its own internal logs, with no external audit behind it.

2

The Report Put a Caveat Next to That Number

In the passage explaining the success-rate chart, the report adds a sentence.

"As agents have become more capable, researchers may also have changed the types of tasks delegated within each duration bucket. As a result, changes in zero-intervention success rates within these buckets may reflect both capability improvements and researchers' changing choices about when and how to use agents."

One sentence, and it unsettles how the whole metric reads. A success rate is the number of tasks that succeeded divided by the number of tasks handed over. It goes up when the numerator grows, and it goes up when the work entering the denominator gets easier. What the report concedes is the second one. The label "two-to-four-hour task" stayed the same, but nothing guarantees that the work filing into that bucket in March and the work filing into it in October were the same kind of work.

Why that happens is not mysterious. Researchers do not pick tasks with dice. The more they learn about what an agent is good at, the more their sense of "this one is safe to hand over" shifts, and that judgment rewrites the denominator. It is what you get from a record of people dividing up their work however suited them each day rather than from a controlled experiment, and the report says as much itself.

There is another layer. Choosing what to delegate has itself become a research skill. How much of the increase belongs to better models and how much to researchers getting better at delegation cannot be separated out of this record. Both count as gains for the organization, but the sentence "agents became twice as capable in seven months" points at only one of them.

It is rare for whoever publishes a number to narrow the reading of it first. The report goes further and says that a rise in research activity metrics does not mean research progress sped up by the same amount, and that the important ideas behind recent models still came from human researchers. It put out numbers and said, in the same breath, what those numbers cannot be used to say.

One part of the report does not hang on the denominator at all: a survey. Asked what they would give up to keep frontier coding agents for three months, the median researcher answered 30 percent of their team's compute, or 40 percent of their team's headcount. It asks directly what people would sacrifice to keep the tool, regardless of which tasks were delegated or how many, so it stands on its own even if the success-rate chart wobbles. It is still a self-reported figure, of course, and it does not explain why the success rate rose.

3

One Agent, a Threefold Gap Between Tasks

The task-type numbers show why the caveat in Section 2 is not a light one. The report carves out August 1 to October 18 separately and tallies how the zero-intervention success rate splits by the character of the work handed over. Watching a training run and catching problems in it clears 79 percent; answering technical questions clears 72 percent. Further down, launching training, reinforcement learning or evaluation runs sits at 35 percent. At the far end sit building evaluation and reinforcement learning environments at 29 percent and writing inference and serving code at 25 percent. That last one is the code that puts a model in front of customers.

Highest to lowest is about 3.2 times. This shape is what the "jagged frontier" of the report's title points at. Agents have not become evenly a little better at everything; some work needs almost no human hand, and some work needs a human stepping in three times out of four.

Group the work more coarsely and the gap narrows somewhat. Sorted into the research and development activity categories of the O*NET occupational classification, Communicate came to 69 percent, Analyze to 58 percent, and Build to 43 percent. Reading, answering and watching score high; making something new scores low.

The report warns that these weak cells could become the bottleneck for overall research speed. However fast the strong cells get, if work queues up at building evaluation environments and writing serving code, the schedule bends to that queue. Those happen to be the cells where being wrong costs the most.

This spread is what gives the Section 2 caveat its weight. If success rates vary threefold by task type, then a change in how many of each type were handed over moves the overall average on its own. Even with agent capability perfectly flat, delegating more of the 79 percent work lifts the average. A single bucket average cannot separate those two movements.

4

Why Pebblous Is Watching This

Numbers of the same shape sit in plenty of company reporting decks right now. Handling time fell by some percentage after AI adoption, the automation rate reached some percentage, time per customer inquiry got shorter by this much. They refresh every quarter, and they are usually written as a single lump average.

The denominators under those numbers move exactly the way OpenAI's did. As a tool becomes usable, the kinds of work people pass to it change. At first only short, standardized cases go over; once people get comfortable, the long and ambiguous ones follow. Or, after one bad miss, the tricky cases get routed back to humans. Either way, this month's "handled automatically" is not the same population as last month's. Only the label matches.

OpenAI is an organization that can stack its researchers' task logs month by month and slice them by duration and task type. Even there, it wrote that it could not separate numerator from denominator. An "AI impact" number tallied the same way by an organization with a less dense measurement system is safer to treat as more fragile, not less.

None of which says to throw the metric out. What OpenAI did was not discard the number but write down, next to it, how to read it. Doing the same takes three things kept alongside the success rate itself.

  • Whether the figures broken out by task type are still on file. A lump average buries a change in composition under one number. Broken out by type, you can go back and see which type grew in the month the average rose.
  • Whether there is a record of when and why the delegation criteria changed. The day the guidance changed, the day the tool was swapped, the day an incident narrowed the scope: those are the dates to lay against the kinks in the metric.
  • Whether the denominator itself is kept with the success rate. Without how many tasks were handed over and what they consisted of, nobody later can explain that month's rise.

All three are questions about how the data is written down, not about the model. A success rate looks like a single number but has two inside it: how many got done, and what and how much was handed over. When the second one is empty the metric stops being comparable across time, and the fact that it is not comparable does not show on the surface either. That is part of why AI-Ready Data means more than values being clean. What population a number came from has to be written down with it for anyone to read it again later.

In the end there is one question to put to a rising number in an internal report. Did this go up because we got better, or because we handed over more of the easy work? Inside its own report OpenAI wrote that it cannot answer that question. Writing down that it cannot is the most useful thing in the report.

Editor's Note

All figures are OpenAI's own estimates from internal logs and have not been externally audited. The underlying data for the charts in the report was not released, so monthly sample sizes could not be confirmed. The O*NET grouping in Section 3 and the values for the under-fifteen-minutes and over-eight-hours buckets rely on secondary reporting that read them off the report's charts, and because outlets differ slightly below the decimal point we treated them as approximate. The reading in Section 4 is this article's own judgment and is not in the report.
