# National Court Administration Finds Two in Three Judges Have Seen Fake AI Citations

_In Korea_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> A court has counted for itself how often filings arrive carrying case law that AI invented. Korea's National Court Administration put the question to 360 judges in courts across the country between October 6 and 16, and published the results on November 2. Of those who answered, 243 said a filing like that had reached them at least once. That is two in three.

> The numbers in this survey carry two denominators. The 80.7% that the headlines carried is not the share of all judges who met fake case law. It is the share, among the 243 who said they had met it, who saw case law that does not exist at all. Read the two as one and the sentence comes out a notch stronger than the survey allows.

> Sections 1 through 3 carry what the National Court Administration published, and the first part of Section 4 carries the overseas tally and the sanction it describes. Setting the measures the judges chose beside three ordinary moves in data work, confirming the source, recording the tool and pricing the error, is this article's reading: the end of Section 4 and all of Section 5.

## The Court Asks Its Own Judges, for the First Time

This is the first time the court has put the question of AI hallucination to judges directly. The National Court Administration ran the survey from October 6 to 16 among 360 judges handling civil, family and administrative cases in courts at every level, and published the results on November 2. Filings citing case law or statutes that did not exist had been arriving one after another, and the stated purpose was to find out how far it had spread.

Of the 360 who answered, 243 said such a filing had reached them at least once. That is 67.5% of the whole, two in three.

The denominator changes at the question about which types they had seen. From there the base is not the 360 but the 243 who said yes. Of those 243, 196 (80.7%) saw case law that does not exist at all, 159 (65.4%) saw real case law whose content had been altered, 41 (16.9%) saw nonexistent statutes, and 10 (4.1%) saw nonexistent papers or other sources. Multiple answers were allowed, so the four add up to more than 100%.
