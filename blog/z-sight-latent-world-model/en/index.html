Executive Summary

A robot model has arrived that anticipates the scene it will be looking at a few seconds from now, and decides its motion from that anticipation, without ever rendering the scene as video. It is Z-sight, posted to arXiv by researchers at Maven Robotics and the University of Southern California. Bolting future prediction onto a robot model has until now meant generating those frames for real. Z-sight leaves the future in the visual tokens the model already uses to recognize the present. To a human eye there is nothing to see.

Two places carry the numbers. On the teleoperated tasks of SIMPLE, a simulation benchmark for humanoid whole-body manipulation, it averaged 81.9%, the highest of the models compared. On six real tasks with a Unitree G1 it carried 44 of 60 runs through to the end. The gap over the model that renders the future as video is 20 points on hardware, and the time it takes to decide one bundle of motion is shorter rather than longer.

Sections 1 through 5 follow the design and the figures the paper gives. Section 6, which asks what a prediction is worth when no one can inspect it by eye, is this article's reading.

1

The Future Sits in Tokens, Not in Pictures

Models that hand a robot its next move have split into two camps. One looks at the camera feed and the spoken instruction and returns a motion directly. Because seeing, understanding and moving are bundled into a single model, this camp is usually called VLA, and the paper's baselines π0.5, GR00T N1.7 and Ψ0 belong to it. The other camp inserts a step in between: it first renders what the scene will look like a few seconds from now, then decides the motion from that rendering. Fast-WAM stands in the table for this camp, known as world action models.

Rendering has an obvious virtue. A person can look straight at what the model expects to happen. It is also expensive. A video generator has to run once more, and the picture it produces is thrown away as soon as the motion has been decided. Part of that cost goes into drawing wallpaper patterns and shadows that have nothing to do with the motion at all.

Z-sight skips the picture. Its vision encoder has already turned the current frame into tokens, and the model predicts the tokens three seconds ahead inside that same coordinate system, the one it uses to recognize the present. The predicted tokens pass straight to the action side without ever being decoded into pixels. The paper calls this latent world modeling, and its title claims to unify VLA and this idea in one model.

Pictures are not entirely absent. The project page carries clips in which the predicted tokens are turned back into video and placed beside the scene that actually followed three seconds later. That decoder, though, was trained separately on real frames alone, and it takes no part in training or evaluation. It is a display attached from outside. While the model runs, the future exists only as tokens.

2

What Goes In, What Comes Out

Three things go in: the spoken instruction, the proprioceptive state the robot reads off its own joints, and the last few camera frames. Past frames pass through a causal video encoder that carries no parameters of its own, and only the spatial tokens of the final frame enter the backbone. The structure keeps the cues about motion while holding the token budget to a single frame's worth.

What comes out is a chunk of continuous motion. How many steps arrive at once depends on the robot: thirty on the G1 and in SIMPLE, sixteen on the tabletop benchmark LIBERO, fifty on the bimanual benchmark RoboTwin. On the G1 the chunk is streamed at 10Hz, and beneath it a whole-body motion tracking controller called SONIC takes over at 50Hz and drives the actual joints. One step of motion is 78-dimensional, holding the latent motion tokens SONIC consumes along with the joint targets of the two Dex3-1 hands. The state is 43-dimensional and is padded to 48 on the way into the model.

The whole model holds 3.18 billion parameters across three branches. The core is the Qwen3.5-2B vision-language model at 2.22 billion, flanked by two experts of 0.48 billion each: a foresight expert that predicts future tokens and an action expert that emits motion. At inference both start from Gaussian noise and walk the same ten Euler steps. The future is not finished first and the motion decided afterwards; the motion sharpens in step with the future as it takes shape.

That joint denoising is where the speed comes from. Running the two stages one after the other took 208.0 milliseconds per chunk, and running them together brought it down to 114.8. Both were measured on the SIMPLE checkpoint with three-frame 360×640 inputs. A separate table measured under batched conditions reports 109.5 milliseconds, against 120.5 for π0.5, 149.7 for Ψ0 and 155.9 for Fast-WAM on the same single RTX 4090. GR00T N1.7, which generates no future and walks only four denoising steps, is fastest at 60.0 milliseconds.

The backbone is shielded from being shaken by the future prediction. The two experts read the backbone's keys and values but send no gradients back along that path, and the foresight loss never enters the backbone at any stage. The paper calls the measure knowledge insulation. The targets the foresight expert has to match are produced by an exponential moving average copy of the vision encoder, a device meant to stop the encoder and its copy from chasing each other until the tokens collapse to a single point.

The action representation changes twice. While the backbone is woken on human video, motion is handled as discrete FAST tokens; once the experts are attached, motion comes out as continuous chunks. Discrete tokens suit training a backbone the way one trains it to guess the next word, and continuous chunks suit streaming into a 50Hz controller. The same model speaks about motion in a different form at each stage.

3

Feeding 4,731 Hours in Three Stages

Training breaks into three stages. First the backbone alone is woken on video shot by people from their own point of view. Then the two experts are attached and run together on data gathered from several robots. Finally the whole thing is tuned end to end on demonstrations collected on the target robot. The video and trajectories that go into the first two stages add up to 4,731 hours.

The first two stages run to 4,731 hours while the demonstrations gathered on the target robot amount to 6.8, close to one seven-hundredth. They carry that bulk precisely so the part a person has to collect by hand can shrink this far.

Keeping human video in the mix during mid-training is part of the design as well. Feed a backbone nothing but robot data and it tends to lose the grasp of language and scene it came in with, so human video stays at a fifth of the sample to hold that ability in place. The measure points the same way as the knowledge insulation described in section 2.

The Scorecard: Simulation and Hardware

4.1SIMPLE, in simulation

SIMPLE is a benchmark that measures, in simulation, a humanoid handling objects while it walks. Wei and colleagues introduced it at CoRL 2026, and it evaluates 17 tasks through a 50Hz whole-body controller. The 12 built by teleoperation form the main comparison set. Difficulty comes in three levels. L0 varies materials and distractors, L1 adds shifting light, and L2 moves the target's starting position as well.

Fast-WAM 66.0% 25.3% 54.1%
Ψ0 65.8% 20.0% 52.4%

▲ Success rates on the SIMPLE benchmark. | Source: arXiv:2611.24940

Broken out by difficulty, Z-sight scores 86.7% at L0, 85.8% at L1 and 73.1% at L2, where the target's position moves too. GR00T N1.7 in the same table sits below it in all three cells at 78.1%, 71.9% and 66.9%. But there too is a GR00T N1.7 variant whose backbone was trained as well, and that one averages 73.9%. The 9.6-point gap the paper puts forward is measured against 72.3%; measured against 73.9% the gap is 8.0 points.

Go down to individual tasks and the places the average was covering come into view. An appendix table lists all seventeen tasks one line each, and some of those lines do not belong to Z-sight. On BendHandoverTeleop, where the robot bends at the waist to hand an object over, it scores 78.9% against Fast-WAM's 85.6%. On the motion-planned task PickAndBendPlaceMP, picking an object up and bending to set it down, it falls well behind at 26.7% against GR00T N1.7's 56.7%. The other way round, the motion-planned task of placing an object on a sofa defeats every model, none of them clearing 30%, and on walking between tables to pick things up Z-sight leads the runner-up by 11.1 points, 84.4% against 73.3%. That appendix table is also where the 52.0% average across the five motion-planned tasks comes from.

4.2The Unitree G1 in the room

The hardware trials ran on a single Unitree G1 with one head-mounted camera and two 7-degree-of-freedom hands, across six tasks. Putting a toy dog into a box and picking up a snack box, turning around and placing it in a drawer are the tabletop tasks. Pushing a chair under a desk, carrying a book across the room and shelving it, throwing a bottle and a piece of paper into their respective bins, and carrying a plate of fruit to the fridge, opening it, putting the plate in and closing the door are the tasks that involve walking. The last two count as successes only if all four of their stages are cleared. Each task was run ten times from the same initial layout, 60 runs in all.

Z-sight carried 44 of the 60 through to the end, 73.3%. Counting partial credit for stages reached, the progress score is 81.7%. Fast-WAM, which renders the future as video, follows at 53.3%, then Ψ0 at 40.0%, GR00T N1.7 at 36.7% and π0.5 at 26.7%. The gap opens widest on the four tasks that have to be carried through several stages, where Z-sight averages 72.5%, Fast-WAM 50.0% and the VLA models at most 32.5%. Each of the two four-stage tasks finished 7 of its 10 runs.

There is a reason the six tasks are not listed one line each. Only one of them is written out as a number. The toy dog task appears separately in the table comparing training schemes, at a 90% success rate and a progress score of 95.0, and section 5 returns to other rows of that table. The remaining five appear only as bars in a figure and as per-stage pass counts in an appendix table. Those counts could be turned into success rates by counting how often every stage was cleared, but that would be a figure this article produced rather than one the paper reports. So it is left out.

4.3On tabletop benchmarks the lead changes hands

The same model was put on two tabletop benchmarks as well. On LIBERO, with all 40 tasks across four suites trained into a single policy, it averaged 97.70% and reached 96.6% on the longest suite. So far so expected. RoboTwin 2.0 is another matter. With 50 bimanual tasks trained into one policy it scored 82.44% under clean conditions and 39.00% once appearance and layout were perturbed, and the lead under perturbation belongs to π0.5 at 46.00%. Fast-WAM collapses to 1.92% under the same conditions.

It is not ahead everywhere, in other words. The ground the paper stakes out is whole-body tasks that chain several stages while walking, and on perturbed bimanual tabletop work it yields the lead to a VLA.

5

With No Picture, How Is Quality Measured?

A world model that renders video lets a person judge by eye whether a prediction is any good. Z-sight's predictions are tokens, so that route is closed. What the authors did instead was damage the prediction on purpose. They swap something else in where the model's own future would go, and measure how far the success rate falls.

The three columns across the top of the table below are test conditions. SIMPLE Hard is the 12 teleoperated tasks of section 4.1 run at the hardest setting, the one that moves the target's starting position, ten runs per task for 120 in all. That is why the 73.1% on the first row matches the L2 score in section 4.1. The other two split the bimanual benchmark RoboTwin 2.0 from section 4.3 into its clean condition and its perturbed one. The six down the side are a ladder of what was placed in the foresight slot, from sound at the top to broken at the bottom. With so many variants, evaluation was run once per entry, which is why the perturbed figures sit about a point away from the scorecard in section 4.3.

Noise, flagged as uncertain 61.7% 79.0% 33.9%
The same noise, flagged as confident 16.7% 50.5% 15.3%
An intact prediction from another episode 11.7% 7.5% 4.0%
A straight copy of the current frame 5.8% 7.8% 2.0%
The foresight path cut off entirely 7.5% 5.8% 1.8%

▲ Intervening on the foresight stream. The same weights run closed-loop with only the future tokens swapped. | Source: arXiv:2611.24940

Two things read off the table. First, noise that arrives with a signal saying the model is unsure only drags the score from 73.1% down to 61.7%. The same noise labelled as confident brings it crashing to 16.7%. The action side is watching not only the content of the prediction but how trustworthy that prediction declares itself to be. Second, an intact prediction borrowed from another episode drops the score to 11.7%. A plausible future is not enough; it has to be the future of this scene.

The value of a prediction has also been converted into action error. Feed in the tokens that actually occurred three seconds later as ground truth and the error on the action chunk falls by 11.9%. Feed in the current frame's tokens clean instead and it rises by 28.3%. That is a yardstick measuring the quality of a future prediction not by the picture but by how much better the next motion lands, and to this article it is the most practical passage in the paper.

There is also a comparison that leaves the architecture untouched and switches off nothing but the future-prediction loss. The parameter count is unchanged, yet SIMPLE Hard falls to 25.0%, RoboTwin's perturbed condition to 11.6%, and the toy dog task from 90% to 30%. The same table carries a variant with the foresight branch removed altogether, which lands at 68.3% on SIMPLE Hard, 22.0% on perturbed RoboTwin and 40% on the toy dog task. Keeping the branch and never teaching it, then, is considerably worse than not having it at all.

The authors read that difference as evidence that the gains come from the predictive supervision itself rather than from the parameters it adds. How strongly the evidence holds varies by condition, and that is written into the table as well. Removing the foresight branch costs only 4.8 points on SIMPLE Hard and 2.4% on LIBERO, while on the hardware tasks and the perturbed bimanual condition it collapses by roughly half. The reading is the authors' own, and no other team has yet reproduced the experiment under the same conditions.

The authors set down one limitation themselves. The foresight stream predicts the future from observations alone and is not conditioned on which action the robot is about to choose. As a result the current architecture cannot try several candidate actions, compare the outcomes and pick one. The authors write that conditioning on action would let the model plan inside its own representation space. Z-sight as it stands imagines a future, but it does not weigh one future against another.

6

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Paper

A world model is, in the end, a way of making data about something that has not happened yet and putting it to work. Until now what it made was video a person could watch, and the questions about quality were shaped to fit. Is the cup of water floating in mid-air? Does the hand have four fingers? Inspection meant opening the file and looking.

Z-sight proposes keeping that output in tokens only the model reads. The efficiency is plain. The cost of drawing disappears, the future is settled in one pass alongside the action, and the capacity once spent on getting the wallpaper right goes into the motion. At the same time the window for inspection closes. Data that is created mid-pipeline, teaches the model and vanishes becomes data no person can open.

That is what lifts the intervention experiment in section 5 above a performance story. What the authors did there was define the quality of generated data as usefulness. How much worse does the next motion get when this prediction is swapped out? That question took the place of asking whether the picture looks plausible. Any system running on intermediate output a person cannot see needs a yardstick of this kind before it can talk about quality at all.

The share demonstration data holds also needs a second look. It looks as though a model that starts imagining futures for itself should need less data gathered by people, but the arrangement in this paper shows the other side as well. What the foresight expert has to match is the scene that actually followed. The reference point for the quality of an imagination always sits in measurement. It is also why 6.8 hours collected on the target robot still matter after 4,731.

So that nothing here arrives mixed together, the contents of this article divide three ways by where they came from. The figures the paper measured and wrote down, the causal reading the authors drew from them, and what Pebblous has laid on top carry different weights. The further down the list, the easier it is to argue with.

Our reading Treating the intervention experiment as a quality standard for generated data, holding that the window for inspection closes, and holding that measured demonstrations remain the reference point for imagination are this article's reading. The paper does not take data inspection or quality management as its subject.

A team that trains on generated data could take this paper and ask three questions of itself.

  • If our intermediate output comes in a form no one can look at, what are we measuring its quality with? The intervention experiment is a yardstick that needs no special equipment to run.
  • Are we recording how much the model trusts its own prediction? Against a baseline of 73.1%, the same noise flagged as uncertain came out at 61.7% and the one flagged as confident at 16.7%.
  • As imagined data grows, where do we keep the measured kind? In this model, measurement sits in the answer key rather than in the training volume.

Set beside the earlier article in this series, ScaleVLA: Does a Bigger Robot Brain Need Fewer Demo Videos?, the axes separate. That one looked at growing the model to spend fewer demonstrations; this one looks at a model generating its own future and using it as a training signal. The article just before it, X-Nav, was a third route, replicating a site to layer a thin skin of data over it. All three try to cut the volume of data people have to gather, and they differ in what they put in its place.

Editor's Note

The figures in this article were checked against the body and appendices of the paper posted on arXiv. It has been up for less than ten days, it has not been peer reviewed, and no other team has yet reproduced the experiment. There are 15 authors, with Maven Robotics and the University of Southern California listed as affiliations. The project page links to a code repository, but whether the weights have been released is not stated. The hardware trials amount to one robot, six tasks and ten runs per task, and the baseline scores were reproduced and measured by the authors themselves. The 109.5-millisecond latency was measured on a single RTX 4090, so it moves with whatever computer ends up on the robot.
