# Microsoft Signs a Contract Promising Not to Train AI on Student Data

_Ten requirements written with the American Federation of Teachers, open to every US school district from November 1. A district that finds them broken can cancel the contract and claim damages_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> Microsoft has agreed to carry a condition in its contracts: student and staff data will not be used to train AI models. The condition comes from the National AI Safety & Privacy Standard, signed on September 7 by Microsoft and the National Academy for AI Instruction, the body founded by the American Federation of Teachers (AFT) and the United Federation of Teachers (UFT). Since November 1, any US school district can ask for those clauses to be written into its own contract without reopening the negotiation. A district is the American education unit that runs several schools together, and it is also the party that signs the contract with the provider.

> The ten principles do not stop at a list of prohibitions. The third principle tells the provider to keep an administrative window the school can look through. A school has to be able to confirm which kinds of data are being processed, how long they are kept, where they are stored, which other firms they are handed to, and who can reach them. What the document leaves out is how fast that window has to answer, and the yearly written confirmation that nothing went into training arrives only when the school asks for it.

> Sections 1 through 5 follow what can be confirmed in the full agreement published by the AFT, in Microsoft's announcement and fact sheet, and in the reporting that carried them. Reading the administrative access clause as a requirement for a data lineage system that runs all the time, in section 3, and carrying that structure over to organizations outside schools, in section 6, are this article's interpretation.

## A Contract Fills the Space the Law Left Empty

Federal law covering student data already exists in several forms. FERPA protects education records, COPPA protects children under 13, and IDEA governs special education records. None of them was written to aim squarely at a generative AI product taking the prompts and conversation logs it collects in a school and feeding them to a model. Randi Weingarten, president of the American Federation of Teachers, said the union had to hammer out a privacy agreement with real teeth because nobody, the federal government included, had done the substantive work.

So it is worth being precise about what this standard is in law. The 31-page document signed on September 7 is neither statute nor regulation but a memorandum of agreement (MOA). The two signatories are the National Academy for AI Instruction, founded by the AFT and the UFT, and Microsoft, which came in under the role of AI Provider. The term runs two years and extends if both sides renew in writing. Neither union holds authority to contract on behalf of every school in the country, so this one document does not place every school in the nation under its protection automatically.

Binding force takes one more step. The preamble carries a clause written so that districts outside the Academy can request the same protections. Once a district asks, the provider has 90 days from the effective date to fold the standard's substantive protections into that district's privacy contract or license addendum through a simple process. The wording does not have to be copied word for word, as long as the level of protection is equal or stronger. Microsoft stated in its fact sheet that no contract renegotiation or renewal is required, and the day that door opened was November 1.
