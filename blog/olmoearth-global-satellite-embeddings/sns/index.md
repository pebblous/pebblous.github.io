## LinkedIn (EN)

To pick out where illegal gold mining spread through La Pampa in Peru, the Allen Institute for AI trained nothing.

The release is OlmoEarth v1.3, published on October 22. A year of observation from Sentinel and Landsat was reduced to 128 numbers for every 10-meter square, and that computation was finished in advance for the entire planet and made available to download. In the mining case the institute pulled out the numbers already computed for each pixel in two separate years and held them against each other. No labels describing what a gold mine looks like were used.

The scores came out alongside it. The embeddings placed first on 20 of 27 evaluations, measured against Google's AlphaEarth Foundations and the academic Tessera v2. Training took 53% of the compute time the previous version used.

What you can do with it comes with limits. Worldwide coverage runs across three years, from 2023 to 2025, and for the six years before that it varies country by country. When the gaps are not written down beside the data, the person using it reads them not as missing but as nothing having changed.

The next question for an organization holding data is less whether to publish it than how far along to compute it before handing it over. ## Facebook (EN)

"Did it actually stay quiet after that?"

The question had been sitting over La Pampa, in the Madre de Dios region of Peru, for more than seven years.

In February 2019 the Peruvian government pushed the illegal gold mines out. By then more than 1,200 hectares of forest in the buffer zone around the Tambopata reserve had been stripped away.

Answering a question like that normally means downloading several terabytes of satellite imagery and finding a model, and the GPUs to run it, that can read the whole stack.

Which is why questions about land have belonged to whoever owned the equipment.

With OlmoEarth v1.3, released by the Allen Institute for AI on October 22, that earlier step arrives already done.

A year of radar and multispectral observation was reduced to 128 numbers for each 10-meter square, and the computation was carried out ahead of time for the entire planet.

Computation finished in advance, distributed as data.

In La Pampa the institute pulled those numbers for two separate years and held them against each other. That was all of it.

No model was taught what a gold mine is, and no labels describing one were used. Wherever the two years' numbers pulled far apart, the land had changed.

The line that stopped me was not in the scoreboard.

It was the one saying the answer came not from starting to train once the question arrived, but from holding already computed summaries against each other.

Which leaves a question behind it.

"Of the data we hold, what is worth computing in advance?"

Not everything can be treated that way, so some test is needed. Do different questions keep arriving against the same data? Does the summary outlive the question?

And this release has its empty places too.

Worldwide coverage runs across three years, 2023 to 2025. For the six years before that, some countries are there and some are not.

When the gaps are not written down beside the data, the person using it reads them not as missing but as nothing having changed.
