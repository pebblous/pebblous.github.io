## LinkedIn (KO)

데이터에 라벨 붙이는 도구를 팔던 회사가 그 라벨을 직접 붙여 납품하기 시작하자, 열일곱 달 만에 회사 값이 13억 달러에서 35억 달러가 됐다.

스노클AI가 9월 22일 시리즈 E로 3억 5,000만 달러를 받았다고 밝혔다. 이 회사는 2019년부터 고객의 데이터에 규칙과 모델로 라벨을 자동으로 붙여 주는 소프트웨어를 팔았다. 데이터를 만드는 일은 고객 몫으로 남고, 그 일을 빠르게 해 주는 연장을 파는 구조였다.

지난 1년 사이 파는 물건이 바뀌었다. 전문가가 손을 댄 완성된 데이터셋, 모델을 훈련시킬 강화학습 환경, 모델의 실력을 확인할 평가 과제를 회사가 직접 만들어 납품한다. 고객으로는 프런티어 연구소와 하이퍼스케일러, 신생 모델 기업, 미국 정부 기관이 발표문에 나열돼 있다.

값이 정해지는 방식이 그 자리에서 달라진다. 라이선스를 팔 때는 고객 쪽에서 몇 명이 쓰느냐가 값을 정하지만, 데이터셋을 팔 때는 그 데이터가 얼마나 어렵고 얼마나 많으냐가 정한다. 매출 곡선이 꺾인 자리는 새 기술이 나온 시점이 아니라 파는 단위를 바꾼 시점이다.

창업자 알렉스 랫너는 이 변화에 이름을 붙였다. 물량과 인력 배치의 문제였던 앞 단계가 데이터 1.0이고, 무엇을 어떤 차례로 먹일지 정하는 연구와 기술의 문제인 뒤 단계가 데이터 2.0이다. 선임 엔지니어가 며칠이나 몇 주를 붙들고도 헤맬 만한 과제라야 지금은 쓸 만한 코딩 학습 데이터가 된다는 것이다. 사람을 빼겠다는 말은 아니다. 사람이 서 있을 자리가 작성에서 검수와 판정으로 옮겨 간다는 쪽에 가깝다.

다만 회사가 내건 매출 숫자는 그대로 옮겨 적기 어렵다. 연환산 매출 3억 7,500만 달러는 12개월 동안 들어온 돈이 아니라 최근 실적을 1년치로 늘려 놓은 값이다. 18배라는 설명을 그 값에 맞춰 되짚으면 1년 전 출발점은 2,100만 달러 언저리가 된다.

게다가 발표문은 새로 시작한 데이터 사업이 18배 컸다고 쓰고, 테크크런치는 회사 전체의 연환산 매출이 18배가 됐다고 쓴다. 같은 배수이지만 잰 대상이 다르다. 경쟁사와 나란히 놓을 때도 자가 다르다. 테크크런치는 머코어와 핸드셰이크 같은 회사들이 내건 총액의 60~70%가량이 실제로 일을 한 도메인 전문가에게 그대로 나간다고 덧붙였고, 스노클AI는 자사의 경우 그 돈이 매출이 아니라 매출원가로 잡힌다고 설명한다.

이번에 값이 매겨진 것은 모델이 아니라 데이터를 만드는 일 자체다. 그리고 대부분의 회사에서 그 일은 아직 예산서의 줄이 아니라 담당자의 업무 시간 속에 흩어져 있다. 질문은 그래서 조직 쪽으로 돌아온다. 우리 데이터 작업이 막히는 이유는 손이 모자라서인가, 아니면 무엇이 맞는 답인지 정하기가 어려워서인가.

## LinkedIn (EN)

A company that used to sell software for putting labels on data started putting the labels on itself and delivering the result. Seventeen months later its valuation has gone from $1.3 billion to $3.5 billion.

Snorkel AI said on September 22 that it had raised $350 million in a Series E. Since 2019 the product had been software: rather than have people label every record by hand, customers used rules and models to apply the labels automatically. Building the data stayed the customer's job, and Snorkel sold the tool that made the job faster.

Over the past year the thing being sold changed. Snorkel now builds and delivers finished datasets that experts have worked over, reinforcement learning environments for training models, and evaluation tasks for checking what a model can do. The post lists the buyers as frontier labs, hyperscalers, neolabs, vertical AI leaders, enterprises and U.S. government agencies.

Pricing changes at that boundary. Sell a license and the invoice is set by how many people use it. Sell datasets and it is set by how hard the data is and how much of it there is. The bend in the revenue curve sits where the unit of sale changed, not where some new technology arrived.

Chief executive Alex Ratner has a name for the shift. The earlier phase, Data 1.0, is "all about volume, and is largely a staffing and logistics problem." The later one, Data 2.0, is "primarily a research and technology problem." Usable coding data now has to approximate development problems a senior engineer might struggle with over days or weeks. None of which means taking people out. It moves where they stand, from authoring toward review and adjudication.

The revenue figure needs reading before it is copied down. The $375 million is an annualized run rate, a recent stretch of performance projected out to a year rather than twelve months of receipts. Work the stated eighteenfold backward from it and the starting point a year ago lands near $21 million.

There is also a mismatch in what the multiple measures. Ratner's post says the new data business has grown more than eighteenfold; TechCrunch says the company's run rate grew eighteenfold over twelve months. Same number, different subject. Comparisons across the sector use different rulers too. TechCrunch notes that peers such as Mercor and Handshake pay roughly 60 to 70 percent of headline revenue straight out to the domain experts doing the work, while Snorkel says that money lands in cost of goods sold on its books rather than in revenue.

What got priced here is not a model but the work of making data. In most companies that work is still not a line in the budget; it is scattered through somebody's week. Which turns the question back on the reader's own organization. Is the data work slow because there are not enough hands, or because it is hard to decide what the right answer is?

## Facebook (EN)

There is a line I went looking for in this year's budget and could not find.

Inference costs had a line. Training runs had a line.

Gathering the data those models feed on, cleaning it, deciding what the right answer even is: that had no line anywhere.

It was not missing. It was scattered, sitting quietly inside a few people's working weeks.

Last week I watched a price tag get attached to that work.

Snorkel AI announced a $350 million Series E on September 22, at a valuation of $3.5 billion, close to three times what the company carried seventeen months ago.

For years what this company sold was software that put labels on your data automatically. That is selling the tool. What it sells now is finished datasets and reinforcement learning environments, built and delivered by the company itself.

The tool gave way to the judgment.

Alex Ratner, who founded it, has named the boundary. The earlier phase, Data 1.0, was about gathering cheap data in volume, a staffing and logistics problem. The later one, Data 2.0, is about making difficult data properly, which he calls a research and technology problem.

The numbers still want careful reading. The revenue figure is an annualized run rate, a recent stretch projected out to a year rather than money that arrived over twelve months, and the eighteenfold growth starts from a correspondingly small base. What stayed with me was not the multiple. It was that the boundary had moved.

At Pebblous we ask what standard a value was judged against before we ask whether it is internally consistent, and the reason sits in the same place. As long as the criteria survive, and the people who can set them, preparing data stays something you can put a price on rather than something you absorb.

So the question the announcement left me with was not really about Snorkel.

"Where does our own data work stall: at not enough hands, or at not knowing what the right answer is?"

If it is hands, adding people fixes it. If it is the answer, ten times the people will not.

Telling the two apart is not hard. Watch how often two reviewers looking at the same record disagree.

We are going to start by rebuilding that missing line in our own budget.
