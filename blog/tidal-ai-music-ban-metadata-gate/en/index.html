Executive Summary

The music streaming service Tidal has decided to clear songs made entirely by AI off its platform. According to the announcement TechCrunch reported on 24 November, deletion begins on 9 December and reaches more than 29 million tracks by the end of the year. After that, the intake stage will turn away 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day. Distributors are also barred from delivering fully AI-generated tracks in the first place.

The part worth more attention than the scale is what gets looked at to call a track AI. TechCrunch wrote that Tidal refuses intake "if songs' metadata says they are made fully using AI." Nowhere in the article is there any mention of analysing the audio. So this gate runs on how the uploading side declared its own track. Which also means it filters only when the uploading side is honest.

Sections 1 through 4 set out facts stated in the announcement, in press coverage, and in material published by Deezer and by industry bodies. The passage weighing where a filing-based gate goes empty, and the whole of Section 5, are this article's interpretation.

1

What Tidal Said on 24 November

Tidal, the music streaming service owned by Block, said on Tuesday 24 November that it will no longer accept fully AI-generated tracks and will remove the ones already on the platform. Deletion starts on 9 December, and more than 29 million tracks will be gone by the end of the year. Once the clean-up is done, the same test stays at the intake desk. The company expects 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day to be sent back there. The act of a distributor delivering such tracks falls under the ban as well.

This is not the first move. In June, Tidal labelled every fully AI-generated track and cut off payouts. Robert Anderson, who leads Tidal's music division, wrote on X that the step had been taken "to protect the integrity of real artists." Even so, more than 43% of the tracks that arrived over the last 90 days were detected as AI-generated. Anderson's next sentence is short: "Something more drastic needs to be done…" What that 43% counts, though — only fully generated tracks, or AI-assisted ones too — is stated neither in Anderson's post nor in the body of the article. This article writes only "AI-generated," as the source did.

The reason the company gave in its official post is less about lost revenue than about burial. It first grants that not every track draws a response immediately and that artists need time to gather fans, then says the present situation is different: "at this scale, real music and artists that Tidal subscribers want to hear and discover get buried." The catalogue holds 27 million tracks classified as fully AI-generated, 6.3% of the total. Of those, 96% have never been played once.

The premise behind this decision is that cutting off payouts did not slow the uploads. Either some of the uploads were never after money, or a reason to upload survives even where the revenue is blocked. The figure that 96% have never been played points to the possibility that these tracks were not put up with listeners in mind.

One pair of numbers does not lock together inside the article. Tracks currently classified as fully AI-generated number 27 million, while the tracks to be deleted by year end come to more than 29 million. The article does not explain where the gap comes from. It could add the inflow that will keep arriving until the deletion finishes, or it could be a count on a different classification basis. This article keeps the two figures apart and leaves each in its own context.

2

The Grounds for Rejection Sit in a Filing Field

Filtering 80,000 tracks a day takes a criterion. The one TechCrunch wrote down runs to a single line: Tidal refuses intake "if songs' metadata says they are made fully using AI."

Metadata is the information that travels with a track file. Title, artist, release date, genre, and — the field at issue here — whether the track was made with AI. What fills that value is not the streaming service but the uploading side: the distributor, and the creator behind it. The value that decides whether a track clears the gate is typed in by the party trying to clear it.

The article contains the phrase "more than 43% of uploads over the last 90 days were detected as AI-generated." Whether "detected" there means the audio was analysed, or means a tally of tracks that arrived with that declaration in the filing field, is not something the article separates. Nor does any of the primary coverage say whether Tidal holds detection technology of its own. That is as far as the confirmable facts go. The published basis for the judgement is metadata filing, and nothing more. That does not amount to an absence of in-house detection.

A gate that leans on filings works well in front of honest suppliers. A creator with no reason to hide a track made with an AI composition tool ticks the box honestly, and that track gets filtered out. The problem is the side that loses by being caught. Leaving the field blank, or writing "made by a human," sits on the same screen as the honest option, and choosing it costs nothing.

Why Deezer Went to the Audio, Not the Filing

The French music streaming service Deezer met the same flood. The position Deezer chose was not the filing field but the sound. It filed two patents in December 2024 which, by the company's description, cover two methods for finding the distinctive signals that separate synthetic content from real content. The claim is that a track made with a widely used generative tool such as Suno or Udio can be identified from the audio alone.

The figures Deezer published on 29 January 2026 explain why it picked that position. Streams generated by fully AI-generated tracks are not many, running between 1% and 3% of all streams on Deezer. Yet of those streams, as much as 85% in some months of 2025 were judged fraudulent. Across the whole catalogue that year, the fraudulent share of streams was 8%. The difference is more than tenfold. The gap carries the implication that AI tracks go up mainly not to be heard by people but to inflate play counts and collect royalties.

Anyone uploading tracks to siphon off royalties has no reason to write "this track was made entirely by AI" in the filing field. In a gate that filters by reading filings, the side it most wants to catch is the side that slips through most easily. Deezer did not put its reasons for going to the audio in those words, but that is the direction its published figures point. The same reading exists on the industry side. In a 14 July article, TechTimes wrote that "fraud actors will not voluntarily label their content," adding that catching fraud requires separate automated detection tools "like those developed by Deezer."

A gate that reads the sound can count even when nothing is filed. In the same announcement, Deezer said that over the month of January 2026 an average of 60,000 AI-generated tracks arrived per day, about 39% of all audio delivered daily. That looks close in size to Tidal's 43%, but the two cannot be set side by side and compared. The counting periods differ, January against November, and there is no guarantee the two companies count the same things as AI-generated. What can be compared is not the size but where the value came from. Deezer's 39% is a figure the company says it obtained by analysing audio; what Tidal's 43% was read off has not been made public.

Setting the two gates side by side, by where each one stands, makes the difference plain.

Other places handle the same problem in other ways. TechCrunch reports the responses platform by platform. Bandcamp has banned AI music outright, and Deezer has added a setting that lets users filter AI music out while also supplying its detection technology to other streaming services. Spotify and YouTube Music stand on the milder side, concentrating on attaching an AI-generated label rather than filtering anything out.

4

Who Writes "AI-Assisted" in the Filing Field

What Tidal is blocking is fully AI-generated tracks. Tracks a person made with help from AI keep coming in. The ban line has been drawn on top of a single word, "fully." Few will argue that the line is unreasonable. AI runs through music work today, from composition assistance to stem separation, mixing and mastering. Blocking all of it would be less a ban line than a shutdown.

The problem is where the line between full and assisted falls. A track whose lyrics a person wrote while the melody, the vocal and the arrangement were all generated — which side is that on? A generated track taken in, with one bar fixed and the mastering done by hand — which side is that on? How Tidal judges this boundary has not been made public.

The boundary is not entirely unwritten elsewhere. On 10 July this year, eight bodies including the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) and the International Federation of the Phonographic Industry (IFPI) jointly put out an AI disclosure standard for streaming services. There are two labels. If AI made all or a substantial part of the creative elements of a recording, it is "AI-generated"; if a person made it in the main, carrying human creativity, with some expressive elements made by AI, it is "AI-assisted." The latter carries a condition that the lead vocal and the main instrumental performance must come from a person. It is the same pair of names Tidal uses. Tidal has never stated that it follows this standard, but the place where the industry drew its line also sits on the word "fully."

This standard, however, is not a gate, on two counts. First, it is voluntary. At the time of publication not one streaming service had said it would use the labels. Second, "all or a substantial part" is something to be judged, not measured. On who does the judging, TechTimes summarised the scheme as one where "labels and distributors report AI usage and platforms display the result."

Published criteria or not, the structure seen in Section 2 holds here too. The person who first judges full against assisted and writes it into the filing field is the one uploading the track. The ambiguous region passes to the discretion of the party doing the filing, and that discretion leans towards getting its own track accepted. Once the removal of 27 million fully AI-generated tracks is done, the path for the same tracks to come back marked "assisted" is open by design.

Up to here is this article's reading of the design as reported, and how Tidal will close that path has not been made public. What can be said is that a gate which does not publish its classification criteria also hides any means of checking the judgement of whoever does the classifying.

Why Pebblous Is Watching This

This reads as music industry news, but in structure it is the same problem every organisation handling data faces right now. Out of a pile of unlabelled data, "made by AI" has to be told apart from "made by a person," and the grounds for telling them apart sit not in the data itself but in a value somebody typed in. Tidal is solving that problem in a live catalogue, and the answer it picked was self-reported metadata. What happens when quality hangs on values the supplier files has come up before, from the data market side, in the economics of synthetic data contamination.

Picture an internal data warehouse and the question moves across at once. Of the documents, images, code and support logs piled up over the last two years, how much came from a generative model — can that be counted today? If it can, where do the grounds sit? Three distinctions make it quickly obvious which box an organisation is in.

  • Is it a filed value or a measured one? A "written by a human" box ticked by the author is a filing. A record the system left automatically at the moment of creation is closer to a measurement. Put the two in the same column and they cannot be told apart later.
  • Does the party filing it have a reason to be honest? What happens when a filing works against the filer decides the real performance of the gate. Accuracy measured in an environment where nothing is at stake does not hold in an environment where something is.
  • When the value is empty, is that a pass or a hold? Tidal's gate rejects a track when it says "fully AI-generated." A track where it does not say so is not judged at all; it simply passes. If the default in an internal pipeline is pass, unlabelled data keeps flowing towards the pile.

There is also the Deezer road of analysing the content itself, but that road requires building and maintaining a detector fitted to your own data. Not every organisation can carry that cost. If the decision is not to carry it, though, it is better to at least know that the labels in use are filings. Build quality metrics while mistaking filings for measurements and those metrics will reflect only the most honest suppliers. There is also the route of moving the question away from judging item by item and towards measuring the share of AI-generated material across the whole, an approach covered separately in the proposal to use AI watermarks the way wastewater testing works.

What remains in the end is a question of records. Which data arrived when and from where, and what was written down alongside it at the time. Records like these do not get created retroactively at the moment a judgement becomes necessary. Tidal could classify 27 million tracks because it had attached labels back in June. That is the place Pebblous points at when it talks about AI-Ready Data.
