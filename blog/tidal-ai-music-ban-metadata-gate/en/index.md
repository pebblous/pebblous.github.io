# Tidal Is Deleting AI Songs, but How Does It Know Which Ones?

_Deletion starts 9 December and passes 29 million tracks, and what counts as AI is whatever the distributor declared_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> The music streaming service Tidal has decided to clear songs made entirely by AI off its platform. According to [the announcement TechCrunch reported on 24 November](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/24/tidal-to-ban-and-remove-millions-of-ai-generated-tracks-as-ai-slop-inundates-music-streaming/), deletion begins on 9 December and reaches more than 29 million tracks by the end of the year. After that, the intake stage will turn away 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day. Distributors are also barred from delivering fully AI-generated tracks in the first place.

> The part worth more attention than the scale is what gets looked at to call a track AI. TechCrunch wrote that Tidal refuses intake "if songs' metadata says they are made fully using AI." Nowhere in the article is there any mention of analysing the audio. So this gate runs on how the uploading side declared its own track. Which also means it filters only when the uploading side writes down what is true.

> Sections 1 through 4 set out facts stated in the announcement, in press coverage, and in material published by Deezer and by industry bodies. The passage weighing where a filing-based approach goes empty, and the whole of Section 5, are this article's interpretation.

## What Tidal Said on 24 November

Tidal, the music streaming service owned by Block, said on Tuesday 24 November that it will no longer accept fully AI-generated tracks and will remove the ones already on the platform. Deletion starts on 9 December, and more than 29 million tracks will be gone by the end of the year. Once the clean-up is done, the same test stays at the intake desk. The company expects 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day to be sent back there. The act of a distributor delivering such tracks falls under the ban as well.

This is not the first move. In June, Tidal labelled every fully AI-generated track and cut off payouts. Robert Anderson, who leads Tidal's music division, wrote on X that the step had been taken "to protect the integrity of real artists." Even so, more than 43% of the tracks that arrived over the last 90 days were detected as AI-generated. Anderson's next sentence is short: "Something more drastic needs to be done…" What that 43% counts, though — only fully generated tracks, or AI-assisted ones too — is stated neither in Anderson's post nor in the body of the article. This article writes only "AI-generated," as the source did.

The reason the company gave in its official post is less about lost revenue than about burial. It first grants that not every track draws a response immediately and that artists need time to gather fans, then says the present situation is different: "at this scale, real music and artists that Tidal subscribers want to hear and discover get buried." The catalogue holds 27 million tracks classified as fully AI-generated, 6.3% of the total. Of those, 96% have never been played once.

> [!callout]
> The premise behind this decision is that cutting off payouts did not slow the uploads. Either some of the uploads were never after money, or a reason to upload survives even where the revenue is blocked. The figure that 96% have never been played points to the possibility that these tracks were not put up with listeners in mind.

One pair of numbers does not lock together inside the article. Tracks currently classified as fully AI-generated number 27 million, while the tracks to be deleted by year end come to more than 29 million. Where does the gap come from? The article does not explain. It could add the inflow that will keep arriving until the deletion finishes, or it could be a count on a different classification basis. This article keeps the two figures apart and leaves each in its own context.

## The Grounds for Rejection Sit in a Filing Field

Filtering 80,000 tracks a day takes a criterion. The one TechCrunch wrote down runs to a single line: Tidal refuses intake "if songs' metadata says they are made fully using AI."

Metadata is the information that travels with a track file. Title, artist, release date, genre, and — the field at issue here — whether the track was made with AI. What fills that value is not the streaming service but the uploading side: the distributor, and the creator behind it. The value that decides whether a track gets through is typed in by the party trying to get through.

The article contains the phrase "more than 43% of uploads over the last 90 days were detected as AI-generated." Does "detected" there mean the audio was analysed, or a tally of tracks that arrived with that declaration in the filing field? The article does not separate the two. Nor does any of the primary coverage say whether Tidal holds detection technology of its own. That is as far as the confirmable facts go. The published basis for the judgement is metadata filing, and nothing more. That does not amount to an absence of in-house detection.

A gate that leans on filings works well in front of honest suppliers. A creator with no reason to hide a track made with an AI composition tool ticks the box honestly, and that track gets filtered out. The problem is the side that loses by being caught. Leaving the field blank, or writing "made by a human," sits on the same screen as the honest option, and choosing it costs nothing.

## Why Deezer Went to the Audio, Not the Filing

The French music streaming service Deezer met the same flood. The position Deezer chose was not the filing field but the sound. It filed two patents in December 2024 which, by the company's description, cover two methods for finding the distinctive signals that separate synthetic content from real content. The claim is that a track made with a widely used generative tool such as Suno or Udio can be identified from the audio alone.

The figures Deezer published on 29 January 2026 explain why it picked that position. Streams generated by fully AI-generated tracks are not many, running between 1% and 3% of all streams on Deezer. Yet of those streams, as much as 85% in some months of 2025 were judged fraudulent. Across the whole catalogue that year, the fraudulent share of streams was 8%. The difference is more than tenfold. The gap carries the implication that AI tracks go up mainly not to be heard by people but to inflate play counts and collect royalties.

Anyone uploading tracks to siphon off royalties has no reason to write "this track was made entirely by AI" in the filing field. In a gate that filters by reading filings, the side it most wants to catch is the side that slips through most easily. Deezer did not put its reasons for going to the audio in those words, but that is the direction its published figures point. The same reading exists on the industry side. In a [14 July article](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/320433/20260714/streaming-platforms-back-ai-music-labels-fraud-actors-exploit-self-reporting-gap.htm), TechTimes wrote that "fraud actors will not voluntarily label their content," adding that catching fraud requires separate automated detection tools "like those developed by Deezer."

Reading the sound makes counting possible even when nothing is filed. In the same announcement, Deezer said that over the month of January 2026 an average of 60,000 AI-generated tracks arrived per day, about 39% of all audio delivered daily. That looks close in size to Tidal's 43%, but the two cannot be set side by side and compared. The counting periods differ, January against November, and there is no guarantee the two companies count the same things as AI-generated. What can be compared is not the size but where the value came from. Deezer's 39% is a figure the company says it obtained by analysing audio; what Tidal's 43% was read off has not been made public.

Setting the two gates side by side, by where each one stands, makes the difference plain.
