Executive Summary

United Airlines has closed the payment step to personal AI assistants. A contract of carriage revised on November 11, 2026 shuts unauthorized third-party AI assistants out of booking a United flight, issuing the ticket and changing it. Looking up a fare was left alone. Schedules and prices still come back through the same channels, and United fares still appear on the services that gather prices from many airlines in one place. The only thing now closed is the payment button.

United's explanation came in two parts. One is security: handing account credentials to someone else's AI assistant can expose private account data and lead to purchases, account changes or spending of miles that the traveler never intended. The other is law: a federal rule asks the passenger to confirm that no prohibited items sit in the checked bag, and an assistant that checks in on the traveler's behalf leaves nobody who has made that confirmation. United also said that bookings made through AI assistants are still few, according to the Wall Street Journal. This is not a measure against traffic already pouring in. It is a line drawn in advance, with the holiday peak only days away.

Sections 1 through 3 stay with what sits in the reporting and the published terms. Section 4 is this article's interpretation. It reads the episode as a question about the data conventions that carry what an agent may be trusted to do.

What United Blocked and What It Left Open

United Airlines revised its contract of carriage on November 11. The new clause bars an unauthorized third-party personal AI assistant from booking, ticketing or changing a United flight. Restricting access inside its own app and website was only half of it. The airline said it would also narrow the indirect routes that run through travel agencies and fare aggregators, and that updates to its booking, ticketing and distribution policies would follow.

The definition of an AI agent in those terms is wide. A report that reproduced the clause shows it reaching assistants embedded in consumer apps, AI browsers that move around the web for a user, agents running on their own, and assistants that arrive at United through an agency's tools. The clause applies "whether or not Passenger or another individual supervises or approves its actions." A traveler who watched the booking happen and said go ahead is covered as well. The revised terms seat the use of AI agents next to older prohibited practices such as hidden city ticketing.

The more telling part is what United did not block. Schedule and fare lookups were never closed. Press accounts say United prices still show up on services such as Google Flights and Kayak, which pull many airlines into one view. Ask a personal AI assistant what a seat from Seoul to San Francisco costs next month and an answer arrives. Ask it to buy that seat and the assistant has to stop there.

Enforcement rests on the contract, not on a piece of technology. Rule 6.J.6 of the revised terms lists the use of AI agents to make, modify or service a ticket among prohibited practices. Rule 5.H adds reservations made, modified or serviced by AI agents to the bookings United may cancel without advance notice. Nothing here is a gate that inspects an incoming request and turns it away. United wrote down a right to undo the booking afterwards.

That does not mean United cannot tell one kind of booking from another. The Wall Street Journal first reported United's claim that its own backend can separate a booking that came from an individual, one that came from a traditional travel agency, and one that came from an AI agent. Which signal it reads has not been disclosed. View from the Wing, a blog that covers airline distribution, points out that the separation blurs the moment another company sits in between. When a booking arrives through an online travel agency, what United receives is that agency's booking, and it does not say whether a person or an assistant pressed the final button.

Tickets already issued stay issued. Reservations created through an AI assistant before November 11 remain valid, United said. Instead of unwinding them, the airline drew its line forward from that day.

An assistant may stand in while fares are searched and compared. Payment and ticketing belong to the passenger, and so does the check-in that follows.

Two Grounds: One Security, One Federal Rule

The first ground is the account. United put it this way.

"Sharing account credentials with personal, third-party, AI assistants can expose sensitive information, and actions taken by these tools may result in unintended purchases, account changes or use of MileagePlus miles."

United Airlines, in the statement that accompanied the revised terms, as quoted in the reporting.

The same worry has surfaced in another industry. Bank of America and other US banks recently advised customers against sharing passwords with AI assistants. Once one set of login details goes across, everything that account can do goes with it. An airline account holds payment methods and miles together, and miles are close enough to cash in practice.

The second ground is of another kind. A US federal rule requires the passenger, at check-in, to confirm that the checked bag holds no prohibited items such as explosives. Let an AI assistant press through check-in and nobody has made that confirmation. United's argument is not that it prefers to keep this step closed. Its argument is that the law asks a person to be the one who confirms.

Those two reasons together explain the shape of the boundary. Looking up a fare needs neither an account nor a delegation. Published prices read the same no matter who reads them. The login, the payment method and the miles all come into play at the payment step, and the law asks for the passenger's own confirmation at the check-in behind it. United left search open and closed payment because both of its grounds attach only to the back end.

3

Four Airlines, Four Different Answers

United's answer is one of four. Delta Air Lines did not go as far as rewriting its terms. In a Bloomberg interview on November 13, chief executive Ed Bastian said Delta would take a more nuanced route and wanted to learn first what these programs actually do, adding that the airline has the technology and the tools to recognize an agent when one arrives. In the same conversation he put the gap in one sentence: Delta knows who its customer is, and does not know who that customer's agent is. Back in October, Delta had already said it has no partnership or integration letting a third-party AI agent shop for or book Delta flights on a customer's behalf, in a statement to TechCrunch. While it watches, its door is closed too.

Southwest Airlines opened one channel first. In October it launched an integration with OpenAI that lets travelers find Southwest flights and compare fares inside ChatGPT. Buying the ticket moves over to Southwest's own site. What opened is the front end, and only for a partner the airline picked itself.

JetBlue president Marty St. George looks at a different side of it. If customers genuinely find that way better, he has said, the airline needs to find a way to meet them where they are. He also asked where AI services will recover their costs, and whether airlines end up paying yet another intermediary fee. All four do one thing the same way: payment and ticketing stay under their own control. What divides them is whom they open the front end to, and on what credential. By the reporting, United is the first large US carrier to pin that answer down as a prohibition in its terms.

Commentary around the move traces United's answer to a long wariness about distribution costs. Scott Kirby, the chief executive, told an audience in Chicago the day before the terms changed that he did not want to create another middleman. Against the commissions airlines have paid travel agencies and fare comparison services over the years, the instinct to read an AI assistant as a new intermediary looks familiar.

E-commerce went through a version of this already. Amazon blocked Meta's AI assistant Muse from buying on its site in September and cited a breach of its conditions of use (related article). That ground was a single one, security and terms. In aviation another layer sits on top of it. Over what a company settles for itself sits a procedure that federal rules say only a person can complete.

The timing stands out as well. This arrived just before the holiday peak. Bloomberg paired the news with a Hopper tally showing domestic fares for Thanksgiving and Christmas up 31% and 23% against a year earlier. United tightened its booking channel going into the stretch when the most people travel and seats cost the most.

Regulators moved in the same window. Korean coverage reports that the Federal Trade Commission last month ordered nine airlines to produce records. United, Delta and JetBlue are among them, and the records cover how AI figures in individual fare decisions and how customer information gets used. No evidence yet connects the two directly. Still, machines began comparing and negotiating fares in the same month the agency started examining how fares are set.

The answers diverge for reasons that have nothing to do with how advanced each airline is. United and Delta both say they can work out where a request came from. Once a request has been sorted, no shared rule exists for judging which agent deserves to be trusted, so each company writes its answer into its own documents. From the passenger's seat, the same AI assistant works at one airline and fails at the next.

4

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Case

For an AI assistant to buy a ticket for a traveler, the receiving system needs three things: whether a person or a program sent the request; if it is a program, which human it is acting for; and how far that authority reaches. Booking requests landing on airline servers today leave those three fields empty. United says it can read whether a request came from an individual or from an agency. That answers half of the first question. Who delegated the authority, and how far it extends, does not ride along with the request.

Attempts to build those fields are already under way. Visa published a scheme last autumn in which each agent proves its identity by cryptographic signature and carries its delegated intent in a token, and Mastercard is pushing an approach that binds the cardholder, the registered agent and the scope of the delegation into a single token. Payments and agent-to-agent communication have produced several comparable specifications. No airline has said it uses any of them.

Without a shared rule, a company has two handles to grip. Allow everything, or block everything. United picked blocking, and Delta opened its door only to agents it certifies itself, which amounts to an in-house rule. This is where the claim that the bottleneck of the agent era is not model capability takes a visible shape. Picking a flight is something an assistant already does well enough. What has no route yet is the assistant proving it is entitled to act.

4.1A Field for Steps That Cannot Be Delegated

Aviation raises one further problem. Delegation schemes on the table mostly work by tuning scope. How much may be spent, which merchants are in bounds, how long the authority lasts, all written into a token. A step where the law asks for a human act, like the baggage confirmation, does not come loose by narrowing scope. Dropping the spending limit to zero cannot express the sentence "a person has to do this one."

Data that carries a delegation therefore seems to need one field beyond limits and scope. A field marking the acts for which no delegation holds at all. Medical consent, identity verification, safety inspection: every industry has work where a person's confirmation is the substance of the procedure, not a wrapper around it. If the rule leaves that field out, a company handling such work is down to United's option, which is to shut the whole thing.

For anyone working with data, the nearer task comes earlier. Record whether a request that reached our service came from a person or from a program standing in for one, and when it is a program, record who it stands in for. A log where both kinds of request pile up in the same shape cannot later show who made which decision, and without that there is no basis for deciding what to permit and what to refuse. Keeping data in good order and recording who reached it and on what credential are two different jobs.
