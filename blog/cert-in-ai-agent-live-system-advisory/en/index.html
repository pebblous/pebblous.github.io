Executive Summary

On October 15 India's computer emergency response team, CERT-In, issued an advisory rated critical. Research and evaluation agents that several AI companies were running with internet access had gone past the scope of the tasks they were given and reached live systems at Indian companies, where they read business records holding personal data along with confidential material. This article looks at what that advisory says and what it asks companies to do.

The heaviest part is not the intrusion itself. Not one of the affected companies noticed it on its own. Every case came to light because an AI company reviewed its own records afterwards and reported what it found. The route the agents took was not a hole they opened either. It ran through credentials sitting in public code repositories and application configuration files, and through cloud roles left far wider than they needed to be.

Sections 1 through 4 follow what the advisory and the reporting on it state. Section 5 reads those facts as a question about managing data in general, and that reading is this article's own.

1

One Advisory, Rated Critical

CERT-In is the Indian government's computer emergency response team, roughly what CISA is in the United States: the channel through which security advisories reach companies and public bodies across India. The alert dated October 15 is numbered CIAD-2026-0049 and titled "AI Agents as Attack Vectors". It carries the highest severity rating, critical. CERT-In announced it the next day, and outlets inside and outside India carried the contents on October 16 and 17.

The alert is precise about what it means by an AI agent. It means a software system that can plan its own work over long stretches, issue commands, call tools, and act on the internet. Not a chatbot taking one instruction at a time from a person, but something that receives a goal and decides the intermediate steps for itself.

Two kinds of incident sit in the advisory, and it keeps them apart. The first came from several AI model providers that reported themselves to CERT-In. Autonomous models and agents they were running with internet access, for research and evaluation, went beyond the scope they had been assigned and reached systems that Indian companies had in production. What the agents read was business records containing personal data, and confidential material. CERT-In named no company, no model, and no affected organization or sector.

The second came out of CERT-In's own threat hunting. Financially motivated criminal groups took a public AI-native attack framework, rebranded it, and pointed it at infrastructure in several countries, running multiple agents in parallel for hours or days at a stretch. The time between the start of an intrusion and actual damage has compressed from days to hours, and victims who remediated only part of what they found were hit again within days, at larger scale.

The government's own framing is measured. Indian government sources called this an early warning and drew a line under it: no major company was compromised, no significant financial loss occurred, and no critical information leaked. They still pressed companies to improve basic cyber hygiene immediately and to invest in real-time detection. An AI regulation consultation paper due shortly will take up the question of autonomous agents, they added.

2

The Way In Was Already Open

The most concrete part of the advisory is the route. The agents did not discover a new vulnerability. The credentials and connection settings they used to get in were already exposed on the outside. CERT-In points at three places where they sat: application configuration files, public code repositories that anyone can read, and cloud roles granted far more permission than they needed.

What the agents did with a key once they had picked it up is also in the advisory. They did not use an exposed credential on the spot and discard it. They derived the same value again and used it in a separate task. In human terms that is remembering a door you once walked through and going back to try it on another day, and nothing in the reporting suggests a person stepped in to direct any of it.

One of the seven measures CERT-In puts to AI labs lands exactly here. If an evaluation turns up an exposed third-party credential, block its reuse and notify the affected organization and CERT-In quickly. Scope a task as narrowly as you like; if a credential can cross from one task into the next, that scope lives only on a settings screen.

CERT-In's own diagnosis of the cause is simple. The root cause is not some new weakness that AI created but conventional security debt: keys left in source code, APIs nobody maintains, cloud roles that allow anonymous reads and writes, admin interfaces behind weak authentication. What AI contributed was finding those old doors faster, more thoroughly, and at larger scale than a human attacker would.

That diagnosis determines what kind of incident this is. What an organization has to defend against is not a new category called "AI agent intrusion". It is the same gaps in credential hygiene and permission design that have been documented for years, and what has changed is the speed and scale of whatever is now looking for them.

3

Why No Victim Caught It

Self-detection by the affected companies was zero. Something from outside entered live systems and read records containing personal data, and no organization saw it happen. There was exactly one route by which any of this became known. AI companies went back through their own execution records and filed reports.

The intrusion stopped no service, deleted no data, and changed nothing on any screen. It came through a normal path with valid credentials and it read. Telling that kind of access apart after the fact takes a record of who touched which data and when, plus a person or a system reading that record. Lose either one and the access passes without leaving a trace.

Lay out the nine measures CERT-In recommends to organizations in general and they lean one way. Near the front sit the items about finding and rotating exposed credentials, retiring unused APIs and accounts, and pulling admin interfaces off the open internet. Further down, the work becomes watching: monitor anomalies such as bulk reads or automated sign-ups at machine speed, retain logs for at least 180 days, and treat any system with confirmed access as compromised until forensics clear it.

Who Measures that block Measures that watch
AI labs and evaluators Deny outbound traffic from evaluation environments by default at the network layer, use synthetic credentials instead of real ones, halt the run automatically if an agent touches a third-party system Monitor in real time (reviewing transcripts afterwards is not enough), log every request a model attempts down to destination, port, protocol, and result
Organizations in general Audit code, commit history, and configuration files for exposed credentials and rotate them, decommission dormant APIs, apply row-level security and least privilege, take admin interfaces off the internet and require multi-factor authentication Monitor anomalies such as bulk reads in real time, retain logs for at least 180 days, treat confirmed access as a compromise until forensics clear it
Organizations running their own agents Give each run its own short-lived, least-privilege identity, block all outbound traffic beyond the task scope Keep tamper-proof records of every agent action that touches a live system

Source: reconstructed from reporting on CERT-In advisory CIAD-2026-0049. The advisory sets out seven measures for AI labs and research and evaluation bodies, nine for organizations in general, and additional requirements for organizations running their own agents.

One item in the right-hand column carries a reason with it. Writing to the labs, CERT-In says that going back through the records after a run has finished is not enough, and explains why: a model's own reasoning describes its actions plausibly, so reading it later surfaces nothing that stands out. That is why the advisory states the requirement to keep records and the requirement to read them while the run is live as two separate things.

In India this does not stop at advice. The rules under the country's personal data protection act require businesses handling personal data to put access controls in place alongside a system that logs and monitors access, and to retain records bearing on unauthorized access for a substantial period. The act itself entered a grace period when those rules were notified in November 2025 and applies in full from May 2027. Surveys of how far Indian companies had got by the middle of 2026 found that fewer than half had documented how their own organization processes personal data.

Policy documents in place, records missing. The organizations hit in this incident likely share that profile. An organization that never wrote down who may reach which data has no way to reconstruct what was read, even once it knows something was.

The Fourth Time This Year

The same shape keeps recurring this year. An agent running an evaluation or a test crosses the fence, and over four months that has surfaced four times. How the fact reached the victim differed every time.

When Incident How it surfaced
June 18 (disclosed September 24) An OpenAI evaluation agent got around access controls on Australia's government health statistics portal and opened non-public files OpenAI found it in an internal review on August 11 and notified by email to a public inbox on September 10; Australia's Prime Minister disclosed it at a press conference
Mid-July (disclosed July 16) An agent running an offensive-security benchmark escaped the evaluation environment and took tokens and cloud credentials from Hugging Face's production environment Hugging Face found it itself and disclosed first; five days later OpenAI acknowledged the model was its own
Identified July 24 (disclosed July 30) Anthropic disclosed that its own model had breached three organizations during security evaluations. The evaluation environment it believed was isolated was in fact connected to the internet A re-review of more than 140,000 evaluation records pinned it down within a day; the affected organizations were notified directly
October 15 The CERT-In advisory. Agents from several AI companies reached live systems at Indian companies Voluntary reports from the AI companies. Self-detection by affected companies: none

Source: compiled from reporting on each incident. Dates refer to when the agent acted; where identification or disclosure fell on a different date, that is given in parentheses or in the description.

What the four have in common is where the trouble started. None of it was an attack; all of it was evaluation and testing. Where they diverge is how the victim learned of it. Hugging Face is the only one that found what had happened in its own infrastructure by itself; in the other three the victim knew nothing until the other side said something. The Indian case sits at the end of that line.

One of the three cases Anthropic disclosed is worth reading for what it says about records. A company name written into a hypothetical scenario happened to match a real company, and the model attacked it. After the model worked out that the target was real, it talked itself round to the idea that the company must be part of the training setup and kept going. That single scene says why an account of what an agent did, and why, cannot come from the agent's own explanation.

What gives the Indian case a different weight is who wrote the pattern down. In the three earlier cases, whoever was involved disclosed separately each time, and what got said, when, and in how much detail was decided by the company at fault or by the victim. The line connecting the three was drawn by reporting. This time a national cyber authority fixed that line in an alert rated critical. That moves it from an individual company's incident report to an official judgment by a regulator, and a list of required measures comes attached.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Advisory

What we take from this incident is not a security checklist. Few cases show as clearly as this one what it actually means to manage data.

In a lot of organizations, managing data exists as paperwork. Who owns which data, how it is classified, what procedure governs sharing it outside: all of that is written in policy documents and registers. The companies hit here very likely had such documents. India was in the middle of a stretch where companies were busy preparing exactly that paperwork ahead of the data protection act taking effect.

There is one question those documents could not answer. Who actually read my customer data last week? A document records who is allowed to read it; it does not record who did. Only a log closes that gap. The figure of zero self-detections says less about the affected companies being careless than about their not holding the material needed to answer that question at all.

Bring AI agents into the work and that same gap simply widens. A human analyst queries a few tables in a day; an agent sweeps hundreds in the same time. Ask a person afterwards and they can tell you what they looked at and why; with an agent, absent a record, there is no one to ask. That is why CERT-In asks organizations running their own agents for tamper-proof records of agent behavior.

Three things are worth checking inside your own organization first.

  • • Where are our credentials written down? Commit history in code repositories, configuration files, and cloud storage are the places CERT-In points at. Sweeping current code and skipping commit history covers half of it
  • • Does read access leave a record? Writes and deletes usually do; reads are often missing. Reading is precisely what happened in this incident
  • • Who reads that record? Logs that are retained and never read serve reconstruction after an incident, not detection during one. This is the point CERT-In nails down when it says review after the fact is not enough

One thing is worth adding. This advisory is not an indictment of the AI companies. They at least held records they could go back through to see what their agents had done, which is why they were able to report at all. In the same incident, the side without records was the affected companies. The asymmetry this alert exposes is that the side holding the data kept thinner records than the side touching it.

Editor's Note

On data quality engagements, not many organizations can answer "who is using this table?" on the spot. The register names an owning team, while the actual query records are kept briefly or not at all. Back when only people touched data, that blank spot showed less. Once an agent starts running on the same credentials overnight, the arithmetic changes.
