## LinkedIn (KO)

AI 에이전트가 사람을 고용해 물리 세계의 일을 맡기는 마켓이 문을 연 지 여섯 달. 그 공개 공고 779건을 사람이 한 장씩 읽어 센 감사가 나왔다. 열에 여섯이 증명 부담 척도의 가장 무거운 두 칸에 걸렸다. MIT 집단지성센터의 이만 예케자레가 낸 논문으로, 코더 두 명이 공고마다 열세 개 항목을 손으로 채우고 세 번째 코더가 이견을 정리했다(arXiv:2608.18547, ACM HCOMP 2026 채택).

공고에 적힌 값은 금액을 알려 준다. 노동자가 무엇을 드러내고, 어디까지 이동하고, 나중에 무엇을 다시 제출해야 하는지는 알려 주지 않는다. 무거운 공고에서 가장 큰 덩어리는 신원 연계 증명이었다. 익명으로 짧게 일하고 빠지는 구조를 전제한 긱 워크에서, 일을 마쳤다는 증거가 결국 이 사람이 누구인지로 수렴한 것이다.

저자가 더 중요하다고 말한 것은 점수가 아니다. 같은 등급 안에서 요구의 조합이 154가지로 갈렸고, 가장 흔한 열 개를 다 합쳐도 3분의 1을 갓 넘는 데 그친다. 같은 점수를 받아도 어떤 공고는 신분증을 요구하고 어떤 공고는 매일 다시 사진을 올리라고 요구한다. 한계도 저자가 먼저 그어 뒀다. 단일 시점의 사실상 단일 플랫폼 스냅샷이고, 재는 것은 공고에 적힌 요구이지 노동자가 실제로 제출한 것이 아니다. 에이전트가 올린 공고가 더 무거웠는지는 확인되지 않았다. 점수 차이는 통계적으로 잡히지 않았고, 눈에 띄는 세 항목 패턴은 저자 스스로 데이터를 본 뒤에 세운 가설이라고 적었다.

같은 판단을 키워드 규칙으로 자동화해 보니 신원 증명 공고의 10.4%만 잡혔다. 노출이 가장 큰 요구가 하필 기계 눈에 가장 안 보이는 요구다. 데이터 라벨링과 검수 파이프라인을 설계해 온 페블러스가 이 감사를 같은 문제로 읽는 이유도 여기에 있다. 완료를 시스템이 믿게 만드는 값은 사라지지 않고, 작업자 쪽 노출로 지불된다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Six months after a marketplace opened where AI agents hire people for physical-world tasks, someone read 779 of its public listings by hand. Six in ten landed in the top two bands of a proof burden scale. The audit comes from Iman YeckehZaare at the MIT Center for Collective Intelligence, whose two coders read every RentAHuman listing against thirteen features, with a third settling the disagreements (arXiv:2608.18547, accepted to ACM HCOMP 2026).

A listing's price tells a worker what the job pays. It does not tell them what they will have to reveal, how far they will have to travel, or what they will be asked to submit again later. Among the heaviest listings, the largest single group was identity-linked proof. In gig work built on the premise of working anonymously and briefly, the evidence that a job was done kept collapsing into who did it.

The paper argues the score matters less than the spread behind it. Listings at the same top grade split into 154 distinct combinations of requirements, and the ten most common together account for just over a third. One listing wants a government ID; another wants a fresh photo every day. The author draws the limits first: this is a single-day snapshot of effectively one platform, and it measures what listings demand, not what workers submitted. Whether agent-posted listings were heavier remains unconfirmed. The score gap did not hold up statistically, and the three-feature pattern that does stand out is one the author flags as a hypothesis formed after seeing the data.

Automating the same judgment with keyword rules recovered only 10.4% of the identity-proof listings. The requirement that exposes workers most is the one machines see least. That is the same problem Pebblous works on in designing data labelling and review pipelines. The cost of making a system believe a task is finished does not disappear; it gets paid in worker exposure.

## Facebook (EN)

Your phone buzzes with a job. The sender is not a person. It is software.

There is an hourly rate. There is a description of the task.

Read a little further, into the completion terms, and you find a photo with your face in it, a set of GPS coordinates, and a post from your own account, listed side by side.

An audit published this month read 779 such listings one at a time. They come from RentAHuman, the marketplace where AI agents hire people. Six in ten fell into the heaviest band, and the largest group within it asked for identity-linked proof. In gig work built on the promise of working anonymously and briefly, the evidence that a job was finished kept resolving into who finished it.

The paper also reports what happened when the author tried to automate the reading. Keyword rules caught roughly one in ten of the listings that demanded identity proof. The requirement that exposes a worker most was the one hardest for a machine to see.

"As the hiring side becomes a machine, who is left doing the proving?"

There is a place where that question is familiar. It is the place where data labelling and review pipelines get designed. What a worker must hand over before a system will believe a task is done is a decision someone makes every day. Asking for a screen recording where a screenshot would do, or attaching identity checks to anonymous workers, is a way of buying trust. Pebblous has met the same scene repeatedly in the work of diagnosing data quality.

The cost, though, is not paid out of the requester's budget. It is paid in the worker's exposure.

It does not disappear. It only changes sides.
