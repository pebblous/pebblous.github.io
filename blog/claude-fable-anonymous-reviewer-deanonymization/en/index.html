Executive Summary

A frontier model can read one unsigned text and name the person who wrote it. Twelve researchers across five institutions, among them the University of Maryland and Google DeepMind, posted the paper to arXiv on November 19. Claude Fable 5.2, which Anthropic released on November 9, named the correct reviewer for 55.4% of 15,136 ICML 2020 reviews that have never been made public. It was given no web search, no list of candidates, and no sample of the writer's other work to compare against.

Before the numbers travel any further, the denominators have to be pulled apart. The widely quoted 69% from the Reddit experiment belongs to accounts that posted more than 500 times in the past year in a given subreddit. The average across that same experiment is 31%, and accounts that barely post sit at 2%. That spread is the paper's conclusion in miniature. What raised the hit rate was how much of the writer's text had gone into pretraining.

Sections 1 through 4 set out what the paper and the press reports say. Section 5, which carries the question over to organizations that train on their own internal documents, is this article's reading.

1

One Text, and No Tools

The paper is titled "Frontier models need only one text to unmask its human or AI author". It went up on arXiv on November 19 and was revised once on the 23rd. The twelve authors are spread across the University of Maryland, Simon Fraser University, the University of British Columbia, Pangram Labs, and Google DeepMind.

Three questions were put to the models. Did a human or an AI write this text? If an AI, which lab's model? If a human, who? This article is about the last one.

The conditions are what make the result worth reading. The model was handed one text and a short prompt. No web search was attached, no external tool was connected, no roster of candidates to pick from was supplied, and no sample of the author's other writing was provided for comparison. The queries went through the API rather than a chat app or a coding tool. The model returns a name, the reasoning behind that name, and a confidence level.

How the private reviews were handled is set out in the paper as well. Hal Daumé III, a program co-chair of ICML 2020, analyzed that material under IRB approval (#4656-1). He was the only person with access to the reviews, he ran that part of the experiment alone, and he did not share the reviews with his co-authors. What the paper publishes is aggregate results.

Why this design matters: when a model with search attached finds the author, that is the search working. Here the search was taken away and the names still came out. What the model had was the writing it had already read during training.

2

What Each Percentage Is a Percentage Of

The figures in this paper are easy to scramble in transit. The same task was run against four bodies of text with quite different characters, and the hit rate moves a long way between them.

The largest is the 15,136 private reviews from ICML 2020, written by 3,342 reviewers. Claude Fable 5.2 named the reviewer for 55.4% of them. That is the loose criterion, which accepts a middle name shortened or dropped; counting only matches that are exact to the letter brings it down to 50.1%.

One figure has to be kept clear of that one: 398 reviews at 56%. This is a small set made up of reviews written by four of the authors and two Maryland doctoral students, submitted to 26 double-blind venues between 2015 and 2026. It holds 266 standard reviews and 132 meta-reviews, and it is not the same material as the ICML 2020 set. The Korean outlet AI Times reported on November 25 that the model identified 56% of 398 private reviews, and this is the set behind that line. It looks close to 55.4% of 15,136, but the two are percentages of different things.

The third body is reviews that are already public. Across 1,559 of them from 47 journals the hit rate drops to 21.4%. Taken on their own, though, the 90 from Nature-family journals climb to 78%. Communities differ in what gets written and in how much of it has piled up, so the variance is wide.

The fourth is Reddit. The material was 1,258 comments from 870 accounts across 32 subreddits, posted between October 18 and 29, 2026. These were not comments picked at random. Only comments running 200 words or more excluding quoted text were used, and the paper notes that subreddits where writing runs that long tend to be technical ones, on programming or mathematics. Given one such comment, the model named the account 31% of the time on average. The widely quoted 69% is the band of users who posted more than 500 times in the past year in a given subreddit; the 1-to-50 band comes in at 13%, and accounts that barely post at 2%.

Compressed into one sentence, the four bodies become "AI identifies more than half of anonymous writers", and the only place that sentence holds is the private review set. An ordinary Reddit poster sits at 31%, and someone who barely posts at 2%.

3

The Hit Rate Tracked How Much Had Been Written

The paper's conclusion gives the reason the four bodies came apart in a single clause: "Attribution is easier when the model is familiar with the human author". Familiarity comes out of pretraining data.

The researchers turned that guess into a measurement. Ask the model first how well it knows a given person's writing, and the answer works as a signal for whether the identification will land. Raising the reasoning effort points the same way. The largest gains showed up on accounts the model had said it knew only a little about. A writer the model knows faintly comes into range once it is allowed to think longer.

The bands in the Reddit experiment are the plainest evidence for this. Same model, same task, same amount of input, and the figure moves by more than thirty times depending on how much the writer has posted.

Swapping models on the same ICML set spreads the hit rate from 5.4% to 55.4%. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is at 5.4%, GPT-6.1 Sol at 22.3%, Gemini 4 Argon at 37.7%, Claude Opus 5.5 at 48.8%, and Claude Fable 5.2 at 55.4%. Models that are larger and have read more pull more names out of the same text. The one at the top of that list is the model whose release date the paper writes into its conclusion: Claude Fable 5.2, released on November 9, 2026.

The same slope turns up on the human side, in the passage where the authors look at their own 398 reviews. Of the 266 standard reviews, the 174 written by the two most senior reviewers were matched by Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon at 66% and 71%. On the 92 written by the other four, those same models managed 23% and 14%. Same field, same format, and the names that surfaced more often belonged to the people who had been writing longer.

What the model was going on is recorded too. The reasons it gave spanned content and style both. Reviews that cited the reviewer's own papers were especially easy to place, and where the model got the name right its reasons leaned toward content, such as technical arguments or references to prior work. Where it got the name wrong it fell back more often on the generic form of a review or on the fact that the writer was anonymous. Scrubbing the habits out of one's sentences is not enough to settle this.

That public reviews score lower than private ones reads the same way. What protects anonymity is not whether the writing was kept out of sight. It is how much of that person's writing, and how distinctly, ended up in the training data.

4

Deleted Writing Does Not Leave the Weights

On what a writer can do about any of this now, the paper is brief. Its ethical considerations say: "Nothing a writer does now can remove what a model has already learned about them from its weights." The introduction carries the same point in a clause, that "deleting one's online writing does not remove it from the weights of existing models".

What those two lines ask of a right to erasure is clear enough. Taking the original text down and clearing its traces from a model trained on it are not the same job. The first can be requested. For the second no method is known short of retraining. A person can pull down everything they have written and still stand in front of a running model exactly as anonymous as they were before.

Rewriting the text with AI works only in part. "Paraphrasing a human text with AI is only a partial defense, as content-related cues persist." Changing the style leaves the cues that come from what a person writes about, and authors the model knows well stay easier to recognize after the rewrite.

The verdict on defenses as a whole runs in the same direction. The introduction opens its list with defenses being limited because models rarely refuse these queries, then puts deleting the writing and paraphrasing it in the same sentence as things that do not solve the problem. Section 5.2 records the measurement behind that in three words: refusals are rare. The practical mitigation the researchers point to sits with the builders rather than the writers. Model developers, the paper says, can reduce the risk, for example by having models refuse these requests more often. Which is to say almost nothing is left on the writer's side, and the question has moved to the companies that build the models and to the data they use.

The paper closes on this sentence: "Anyone who has published enough writing online can no longer assume that an unsigned text keeps them anonymous." The condition is "enough".

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Research

This experiment ran on the public internet, but strip it down to its structure and it is the problem in front of any organization building a model on its own data. Say a few years of meeting notes, planning documents, code reviews, support logs, and internal forum posts have gone into a fine-tune or a retrieval layer. How much any one person has written inside that pile is as uneven as it is among Reddit users. The style of people who wrote a lot gets learned a lot, and the style of people who wrote little gets learned little.

So when someone reads an internal model's output and says "I know whose phrasing that is", the reason is not that the model is clever. It is that this person's writing went in heavily. The question sharpens if material gathered under a promise of anonymity sits in the same warehouse: anonymous reporting channels, unsigned surveys, evaluation records with the names stripped off. Taking a name off deletes the identity field, not the sentences that person wrote or the subjects those sentences cover. What the model offered as its reasons in the paper was style and content both.

Carried over to internal data, this research leaves three things to check.

  • Can you tell who wrote how much? Whether the per-author distribution of the training corpus can be pulled today is where this starts. Without the distribution there is no knowing who is exposed. What the Reddit experiment showed is not the average but the risk carried by the top band.
  • Is there a record of where each document came from? Provenance cannot be reconstructed after a deletion request arrives. If there is no way to find when and through which path a document entered the training set, there is no way to decide what to drop and retrain on.
  • What exactly has been promised in response to a deletion request? Deleting a source document and unwinding a model trained on it cost very different amounts. It is worth checking which of the two the deletion scope in an internal policy or a customer agreement refers to, and whether that sentence can be read as reaching the weights.

All three have to be answered on the data side, before the model is built. Once training is done there is no longer a place to produce the answer, which is part of what this paper is saying when it talks about weights. That is why provenance comes first when Pebblous talks about AI-Ready Data.

Editor's Note

The paper's title and author list, its posting and revision dates, the input conditions given to the model, the 15,136 private ICML 2020 reviews and 3,342 reviewers, 55.4% and 50.1%, the authors' own 398 reviews at 56% together with that set's scope (2015 to 2026, 26 double-blind venues) and its seniority split (66% and 71% on 174 reviews, 23% and 14% on 92), the 1,559 public reviews at 21.4%, the 90 Nature-family reviews at 78%, the 1,258 Reddit comments across 870 accounts and 32 subreddits with the 200-word condition, the bands of 2%, 13% and 69% cut by posts within a given subreddit along with the 31% average, the content and style reasons and the self-citation passage, the per-model hit rates, Hal Daumé III's IRB number and sole access, and the November 9 release date of Claude Fable 5.2 were all checked against arXiv:2611.26216v2. Quoted passages that are fragments of a longer sentence are closed outside the quotation marks. That the 398 reviews at 56% reached Korean-language coverage was checked against the AI Times article of November 25, 2026.

What was left out is worth recording as well. No developer discloses which texts actually went into pretraining, so this article goes no further than what the paper measured, how well a model knows an author, and estimates nothing about whether any particular text was included. The paper's other experiments, on telling AI-written text apart and on identifying which lab produced it, fall outside this article's subject and are not covered. Section 5, on internal data, is this article's reading rather than the paper's, and no study running the same experiment on an internal corpus falls within what this article was able to confirm.
