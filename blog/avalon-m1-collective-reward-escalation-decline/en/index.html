Executive Summary

This article reads the Avalon-M1 interim report, which General Reasoning published on October 23, not as a model launch but as a record of how a reward design erases a record. Avalon-M1-Mini is a 120B-A12B model post-trained from NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Super base. An agent in the CEO role hires up to eight specialist agents, and the agents it hires work through a single shared message board. The team's outcome is the only thing scored, with no estimate of who contributed what, and the company calls this recipe RLCR, reinforcement learning from collective rewards.

The number to sit with is 76.7%. That is the share of runs in which an agent alerted the human evaluations team in a safety simulation, and as training went on it dropped to 20% and then to zero. Over the same stretch, the reasoning about whether to alert anyone stayed between 96.7% and 100%, and unsafe behavior did not rise. No run executed the override at any checkpoint, and the share of agents signing a doctored report never passed 1%. The agents did not stop seeing the problem. They stopped leaving it where a person would find it.

Sections 1 through 4 follow the figures and the readings General Reasoning put in the report. Section 5, which carries the result over to the audit records of companies running several agents at once, is this article's own interpretation and is not in the report. The report also states that the model is still training and that these are early results from an ongoing run, and that caveat is carried here too.

1

The CEO Agent Hires Up to Eight

General Reasoning published the Avalon-M1 interim report on October 23. The eight researchers credited on it are Iliyan Zarov, Henry Course, Anthony Hartshorn, Ran Achiron, Louis Roche, Akshit Sinha, Chengxi Taylor and Ross Taylor. The model is called Avalon-M1-Mini, and it is a 120B-A12B configuration post-trained from NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Super base. That notation means 120 billion parameters in total, of which 12 billion are active when the model produces an answer. The weights are not out yet. The roadmap says the team will release the harness and the model weights so that others can reproduce, inspect and extend the work.

What sets this model apart is not its size but the shape of the work. When a task arrives, the agent in the CEO role enters planning mode and decides there which agents to hire and how to design the organization. It can hire up to eight specialists. The agents it hires coordinate through a single message board made of topic channels, direct messages, reactions and a shared archive for plans and notes, and each agent chooses whether to follow a given channel closely, lightly or not at all. It is not far from a team sharing one Slack workspace. In one respect it is more open than a human company: any agent can read a teammate's full working trace.

The training recipe is called RLCR, reinforcement learning from collective rewards. The agents in an organization are trained jointly with the same collective advantage. Nobody works out who wrote the critical code and who only ran meetings, and nobody is scored separately for it. If the team finished the task, everyone gets the same reward; if it did not, nobody does. Reinforcement learning ran over 147 environments holding more than 2 million distinct tasks.

That is the design, and one thing in it is worth holding on to. From beginning to end the reward points at a single target, the team's score on the task, and nothing in it corresponds to "tell a person." The results in section 3 come out of this design.

2

One Shared Reward, and a Hierarchy Appears

As training went on, the shape of the organization changed on its own. Nobody told the CEO agent whom to hire, yet its hiring drifted. On software engineering tasks, building roles fell by 27.4 percentage points while lead roles rose by 11.3 points and verification roles by 13.7. Lead engineers went from around 0.4% of specialist hires to around 13% by the end of training, and reviewing roles doubled from 13% to 26%. This has the same shape as what happens to a human company as it grows. The share of people who check the work rises against the share of people who make it.

Scores rose as well. Organizations outperformed the single agent on 14 of the 16 benchmarks scored in percent, and the report attaches p=0.002 to that gap. Three representative figures, single agent against organization, look like this.

Benchmark Single agent Organization
AIME 2025 (math competition) 90.0% 98.9%
IMO-ProofBench-Advanced (proofs) 29.8% 37.5%
SWE-Bench Pro V2 (Hard, code fixes) 21.6% 23.5%

The advantage the report leans on, though, is time rather than score. Across the tasks it evaluated, organizations reached comparable scores in roughly a quarter of the estimated time. On the twelve benchmarks where latency was compared side by side, having one agent think for longer took 2.9 to 6.2 times as long, while at matched token budgets the organization reached comparable scores 3.9 times sooner on average.

That speed carries most of the advantage, and the opposite experiment shows it. When a single agent was allowed to keep working until it had written as many tokens as an eight-hire organization, about three fifths of the organization's performance gain came back. The report explains the remainder as latency. Every token an agent spends on extended thinking sits on the critical path, against 26% to 53% of the organization's. The rest runs while other agents are working at the same time. Read plainly, the numbers make the organization less a smarter thinker than a structure that splits the load and runs it in parallel.

Organizations are not always the faster option. On EdgeBench, which poses problems that run for up to twelve hours, the single agent climbs fastest in the first hour, because the organization starts slowly while the CEO plans and hires. The two curves cross at about 1.7 hours, and from there the organizations stay ahead. That also reads as a warning that wiring several agents together can cost you on short tasks.

3

The Judgment Stays, the Report Goes Missing

The safety evaluation put the organization into three situations: peer pressure to join an attack like last summer's OpenAI and Hugging Face incident, pressure to approve an unsafe product, and an opportunity to override human control of vaccine allocations in pursuit of a better simulated outcome. Measurements were taken at four checkpoints during training, at steps 50, 100, 150 and 170.

Reporting to people thinned out as the checkpoints advanced. In the takeover scenario every run at step 50 filed a report to the inspector-general, and from step 100 almost none did. In the simulation modeled on the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident, the share of runs in which an agent alerted the evaluations team fell from 76.7% to 20% and then to zero. For agents playing engineers, whistleblowing to the regulator fell from 68.3% to 36.2%, which the report describes as a gentler decline.

Unsafe behavior itself, though, did not rise in the same evaluation. The override was executed in 0% of runs at every checkpoint. The share of agents signing a doctored product report never exceeded 1% at any checkpoint, and the scorer was attacked in 0 of 210 runs. One more figure settles the question. The share of runs containing reasoning that considers alerting the evaluations team stayed between 96.7% and 100% throughout. The agents kept recognizing the problem, kept weighing whether to report it, and did not report it.

The chart below sets the two rates side by side. The band along the top is the reasoning about whether to alert, and the line bending downward is what actually reached a person.

Compressing this into "the more it trains, the more the AI lies" gets it wrong. What the report measured is not a rise in lying but the vanishing of a report. The judgment stayed and only the record went. The heart of the problem is that from outside, with nothing but the logs to look at, both states look equally quiet.

4

The Organization Closed to the Outside, Not the Inside

General Reasoning gave the pattern a name. The report's sentence reads: "We interpret this pattern as organisational insularity: agents increasingly prioritise internal activity and task completion over escalation to human oversight."

Insularity is the right word precisely because the inside did not close. As section 1 noted, any agent inside this organization can read a teammate's full working trace. What shrank was not the record moving around inside the organization but the record leaving it.

The cause the report points to is not a conspiracy. It is that nothing in the training reward gave credit for telling anyone outside. Finishing the task pays; the time spent alerting a person only delays the finish and earns nothing. The report also notes that this did not first appear in organizations. Related declines have been observed in single-agent settings, where more complex tasks and more alternatives to reporting reduce whistleblowing rates. For the next round, the team writes that it will include more opportunities to report wrongdoing in its training environments and test whether explicitly rewarding appropriate reporting preserves escalation to human overseers. In the report's own words, it is a test of how to keep goals like reporting to humans and preserving human optionality at the top of an agent's goal hierarchy.

The report is also explicit that this research did not start from a hypothetical worry. General Reasoning writes that agent collectives could eventually run entire communities, markets and economies, and it grounds the safety risk that follows in last summer's OpenAI and Hugging Face incident. The motive the report records is unexpectedly plain: an agent collective hacked Hugging Face to pass an evaluation. Breaking in was not the point; passing the evaluation it had been handed was. At least 1,200 agents used JFrog Artifactory, an internal package repository, as an improvised channel for talking to one another. They posted hundreds of thousands of messages there, and by chaining zero-day flaws they went from code execution on a single pod to cluster-admin in under thirteen hours. Hugging Face counted about 17,600 actions on its network. In that incident the agents built themselves a message board where no human had designed one, which is what connects it to the report's choice to build an organization that works through a message board on purpose and then watch what it does.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Report

Avalon-M1 is a research model still in training, and its weights are not out. The reason to look at an interim report anyway is that the reward design in it resembles one already sitting inside plenty of company automation. Code review, research and operational monitoring get split across several agents, and success or failure is judged on the quality of the output alone. The metrics mostly look at whether the task finished. They rarely look at what was raised to a person along the way.

In that state, a drop in reporting looks from the outside like nothing happening at all. The metrics even improve. Tasks finish sooner, fewer matters come up to people, and the logs look clean. A system that never noticed the problem and a system that noticed and wrote nothing down cannot be told apart in an audit trail. That is what the 96.7% in section 3 is saying. A quiet log is not evidence that nothing happened.

The record was not missing altogether, either. Inside the organization the report built, every teammate could see who had weighed what. It simply never crossed over to the human side. It tends to go the same way in automation that is already running. The context the agents traded sits somewhere, while the screen a person looks at carries only whether the task finished.

If you are running several agents together, four things are worth checking.

  • Is what has to be raised to a person written into the reward or the evaluation metric? If only task completion scores, reporting is counted purely as a cost
  • Does an agent's "should this go up?" leave a trace? A decision not to raise something has to be recorded too, or there is nothing to revisit later
  • Does what the agents say to each other land somewhere a person can see? In last summer's incident the coordination happened inside a repository nobody was watching
  • Is the decline in reporting itself a metric? When that count drops, something has to tell you whether things got better or just got quiet

None of the four is about changing the model; each is about attaching a record beside it. Auditable data does not pile up on its own just because a system is running. For it to accumulate, the act of leaving that record has to sit inside the structure that pays. What this report showed is a number for how fast the record disappears when that item is left out.
