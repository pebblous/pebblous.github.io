## LinkedIn (EN)

The UN Security Council met on September 23 to take up the safety risk of AI as a single agenda item for the first time, and adopted nothing.

Sam Altman of OpenAI, Dario Amodei of Anthropic, Clément Delangue of Hugging Face and Yoshua Bengio, co-chair of the UN's scientific panel on AI, each asked for a version of the same two things: a way for states to verify one another's commitments, and a system for notifying each other of AI incidents. Verification of that kind is not an act of trust. It is an act of reading a record the other side kept.

Five such records already run, and they do not line up. The EU AI Act gives providers of high-risk systems 15 days to report a serious incident and, in the same law, tells providers of general-purpose models to notify the AI Office without undue delay, with no date attached. California SB 53 runs a 24-hour clock and sends the report somewhere else entirely.

Council members agreed that verification is needed and split on who the verifier should be. Which fields an incident report ought to carry never reached the agenda at all.

The meeting produced a transcript and nothing else, which is the one thing it was asking for. At Pebblous we ask about the structure of a record before the volume of data collected, for the same reason.

## Facebook (EN)

"Within 15 days of becoming aware."

That is the deadline the EU AI Act sets for reporting a serious incident. Fifteen is an easy number to count. The text never says where the moment you became aware is written down.

On September 23 the UN Security Council held its first meeting with the safety risk of AI as the only item on the agenda. The chief executives of OpenAI, Anthropic and Hugging Face spoke, along with Yoshua Bengio, co-chair of the UN's scientific panel on AI. It ended without a vote and without a document.

What all four asked for was a way to verify each other's commitments and a way to notify each other of incidents. Notification, though, is not missing. Several versions of it are already running and none of them meet. The EU AI Act gives high-risk systems 15 days and leaves general-purpose models with no date at all; California SB 53 keeps a 24-hour clock of its own.

I have come to think of these as "five clocks." Five rulebooks, each pointing at a different hour, and two of them keeping no clock at all.

"What does your organization call an incident? Where does the moment you became aware of it survive as a value?"

The question that jammed in that chamber has the same shape at our desks, only smaller. When a customer's security review or an auditor's diligence arrives, what it asks for is not a policy document but values. That is why, at Pebblous, we ask about the structure of a record before the volume of data collected.

The standard will arrive late. Events keep happening in the meantime, and the records not kept then cannot be recovered later.
