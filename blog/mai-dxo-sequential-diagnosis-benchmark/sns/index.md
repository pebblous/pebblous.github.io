## LinkedIn (EN)

An AI that got 83.2% of hard diagnostic cases right did not beat specialists.

The study appeared in Science on October 22, from Microsoft. On cases difficult enough to be published as teaching material by the New England Journal of Medicine, the diagnostic orchestrator MAI-DxO scored 83.2%. The physicians who sat the same test averaged 36.1%. On a second set drawn from emergency department records, the order came out the same.

There were 56 physicians, and every one of them was a generalist. No specialists sat the test, and neither did multidisciplinary teams. The authors set that boundary themselves. The paper states the result does not show the system is better than specialists or than real clinical care, and all 15 authors work for Microsoft.

The test is not a living patient either. SDBench is built from 629 cases that had already been diagnosed and closed, rewritten afterwards as a sequential interview that withholds the record and releases it one piece at a time.

Cost depends on which setting gets quoted. In the setting that produced the highest accuracy, the one with no budget ceiling, MAI-DxO ordered $5,431 of tests per case, against a physician estimate of $3,049 to $4,725 on the same cases. Put a ceiling on the budget and the figure drops well under the doctors. Both sentences come from the same study.

Pebblous puts a number on the quality of AI training data with DataClinic. When a report says a model score has gone up, the first two questions are the same ones. What data was it measured on, and is that data shaped like what will arrive in production. A score with no comparison group and no data provenance next to it is not a result yet.

## Facebook (EN)

"AI beats doctors."

That headline went past me three or four times this week.

I read it the same way at first. Going by the numbers alone, it is not a wrong summary.

The system got 83.2% of the hard cases right, and the physicians working the same cases came in at 36.1%.

But there is a seat the headline almost never mentions.

Who was sitting across the table.

Fifty-six physicians sat the test, and every one of them was a generalist.

No specialists. No multidisciplinary teams, the kind where several departments look at one patient together.

Nobody hid this. It is written into the paper, and the authors said they expect the gap to narrow against specialists.

What happens is later. The headline gets cut, that line slides below the fold, and translation shaves it off once more.

The test paper has a similar story.

Those 629 cases are not living patients. They are records of diagnoses that were made and closed some time ago.

The researchers rebuilt them afterwards as a sequential interview. A few lines about why the patient came in, then one more piece of the record each time a question or a test is requested.

It is a text whose ending is already written, covered over and uncovered one layer at a time.

The cost figure does not fit on one line either.

The highest accuracy came from the setting with no budget ceiling, and in that setting the tests ordered came to $5,431 per case. The physician estimate on the same cases runs from $3,049 to $4,725.

With a ceiling on the budget, the figure falls well below that.

Both sentences come from the same study and both are true. Strip the condition off either one and both become false.

"Who was sitting across the table from this number?"

Microsoft did not bury the conditions. They go missing a step later, at the point where a number becomes a headline.
