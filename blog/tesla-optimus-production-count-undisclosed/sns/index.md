## Facebook (EN)

"Why is that cell empty?"

I was reading the factory table in Tesla's third-quarter update from October 21 and caught on one row.

Each vehicle line carries a number: how many units a year that plant can build.

The two Optimus rows carry no number. California reads 'Early Ramp.' Texas reads 'Construction.'

The same update said Optimus production has started on the high-volume line at Fremont, on the floor where the Model S and Model X assembly line stood until it was torn out over 46 days.

How fast the line runs, how many have been built, what one costs, when a customer could buy one, which generation is on the line. None of that was in the announcement.

What I read twice was not the silence. It was another number from the same room.

Elon Musk said that getting the data to teach a robot its work means building 10,000 or 20,000 of them and having them play against themselves.

The count it needs, Tesla gave as a figure. The count it has, it did not give at all.

Line up the numbers from that day and their character shows. One million a year is the design capacity of the line. Twenty thousand a week is a target at least a year out. Late 2027 is when outside deliveries are expected.

Every one of them is written in the future tense. The only figure missing is the one that speaks for today.

Here is where robots part from cars.

FSD has a fleet already on the road sending data back. Optimus has no such fleet. Gathering the data means building the robots first, and the number built becomes the total hours available to practice.

So not knowing the count is also not knowing how much this company learns in a day.

Tesla is not alone in it. Unitree is reported to have shipped around 5,500 units in 2025, and the deployed scale of 1X Technologies swings between the hundreds and the thousands depending on who is counting.

Robot manipulation data is meanwhile already priced and sold by the hour. One seller's rate card lists bimanual dexterous manipulation at $1,200 an hour.

"The price is set. Divided by what?"

Whether the expensive end costs what it costs because the data is better, or because there are only so many robots in the world that can make it, is a question you need the supply to answer.

Pebblous documents the quality of AI training data with DataClinic. For that record to say what it represents, how many robots and over how many days has to be written down first.

There is nothing strange about Tesla holding the count back. Early-ramp yield and rate are the first things a competitor reads.

But a car company reports every quarter how many it built and how many it handed over. The same company writes down only the name of a stage for its robots.

As long as the robot is a learning device before it is a product, that cell may stay empty. The price is already being set on top of it.
