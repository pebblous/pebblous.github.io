Executive Summary

Building a large model on brain imaging has meant reaching for a cohort like the UK Biobank, which you get to only through an application and a review. A public release now pushes against that assumption. Open-fMIND gathers 322 functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies that sat scattered across the public repository OpenNeuro, runs every one of them through a single version of the same preprocessing tool (fMRIPrep 25.2.3), and publishes the result as 4.35TB. Not one new scan was taken.

The evidence the authors offer is a comparison experiment. They pretrained one brain model on this corpus and another of the same architecture on the UK Biobank, then put both through six downstream tasks. The public corpus came out ahead on two, level on one, and behind on three. Saying it comes close to a gated cohort holds; saying it is equal would overstate the result. All three encoders were pretrained exactly once, so the small gaps call for more caution still.

Sections 1 through 5 set out what the paper, the dataset card and the code repository state, together with the limits the authors wrote down themselves. Section 6, which asks what this corpus adds for anyone preparing data for AI training, is this article's own reading.

1

Not a single new scan went in

OpenNeuro is a public repository where brain imaging researchers deposit their raw data alongside their papers. More than a decade of deposits has pushed it past several thousand studies, but each one is small, often a few dozen participants, and the state in which it arrives varies. Some groups uploaded preprocessed derivatives, others the output straight off the scanner. Even the names for the same brain region differ from study to study. The data is there and cannot be used as it stands, and that is what "we don't have enough data" usually looks like from up close.

What Open-fMIND did was strip one layer off that state. The authors pulled human fMRI datasets from OpenNeuro and reprocessed the 322 that survived curation through a single version of fMRIPrep, 25.2.3. fMRIPrep is a widely used preprocessing tool in the field: it corrects head motion and places every brain in the same standard coordinate space. Here everything was resampled onto the 2mm grid of the MNI152 template in one pass, and the whole job ran inside a version-pinned container so that the execution environment matched too.

Then comes parcellation. Working voxel by voxel across a whole brain is unwieldy, so a predefined map groups the brain into dozens or hundreds of regions and one timeseries is extracted per region. That map is called an atlas. Open-fMIND used 52 atlases, from a 7-region one up to a 1,024-region one, and ran every run through all 52. Rather than leave the atlas choice to the user, it precomputed the entire menu.

How many families those 52 came from is where the two sources part. Table 2 of the paper counts 14 parcellation families (Schaefer 14, Yan 12, DiFuMo and BASC 5 each, Smith and Tian 3 each, Harvard-Oxford and Yeo 2 each, and six more families with one apiece), while the dataset card says 15. This article follows the paper, the source that lists a count per family. Either way, what matters to a user is that the 52 are not spread evenly across families, and that skew comes back in section 5.

That is what went in. The shape of what came out sits in the table below, in the order anyone should ask about a dataset: size, how it was collected, labels, license, known defects.

Read the top two rows alone and this is a story about size, but what the corpus actually sells is the bottom three. Where the labels came from, how many license types are in play, what was deliberately not filtered out: all of it travels with the files as a record. Each run carries a sidecar JSON holding the name of the atlas used and the order of its region labels, the repetition time, the confound set, the bandpass window, the motion threshold, the number of volumes removed and the motion metrics. On top of that sits a processing chain with the software versions and the commands that ran, the accession number and version of the source OpenNeuro dataset, and the digest of the fMRIPrep container. As the later sections show, this part outlives the performance numbers.

From 1,066 studies down to 322

In work that takes a public repository as raw material, the number that matters most is not the final size but the attrition. What dropped out, and why, defines the character of what remains. Open-fMIND's curation is written up step by step in the paper, and laid out in order it comes to four bars.

The starting point is 1,066: every human fMRI dataset returned by a query against OpenNeuro's public API, holding 45,067 participants, 67.0TB of raw data and 1,913 distinct task names. Of those, 387 were downloaded and taken as processing candidates. Curation removed 27, with reasons attached. Twenty-four had no BOLD imaging at all, two were derivatives of other studies, and one was excluded at the explicit request of its authors. Subtract one more that failed to download and 359 remain, holding 14,016 participants and 52,505 runs.

The largest loss arrives in the next step. Of those 14,016 participants, 2,637 did not make it through fMRIPrep. The released count of 11,379 participants is exactly that difference. Close to two in every ten dropped out at the preprocessing stage, which is also evidence that pushing repository data through one pipeline is not as simple as the sentence makes it sound. Why the dataset count fell from 359 to 322 the paper does not say. Since the participant counts reconcile exactly, the remaining 37 are most likely datasets whose participants were all lost, but that inference is this article's arithmetic rather than anything the paper states.

Whether a curation table with reasons travels with a dataset makes a bigger difference than it looks. In a corpus where you cannot see what was removed, there is no way to tell whether what is missing is accident or systematic bias. If the 2,637 participants lost in preprocessing cluster on one scanner model or one year of acquisition, the character of what remains changes, and without a curation table you cannot even put the question.

There is a gap on the snapshot side as well. For 17 datasets no record survives of which version the authors received, so the latest version at download time was assumed. OpenNeuro datasets get updated, so for anyone trying to reproduce the same work later those 17 remain an uncertain spot. The authors recording the fact on the dataset card does not fill the gap, but the difference between recording it and not is plain enough.

3

Neither the labels nor the quality metrics filter for you

In a dataset write-up the labelling entry usually describes who assigned ground truth and by what standard. Open-fMIND has no such step. Task names are taken as they already stand in the BIDS filenames of the original studies. What the authors added is one mapping table recording, for each (dataset, task) pair, whether it is a resting state or a task-based acquisition. The table is task_type_mapping.tsv, and no harmonization of meaning goes beyond it. Two studies using the same task name are not guaranteed to have run the same task.

The size of that gap is measurable. The 1,066 datasets at the query stage carried 1,913 distinct task names, and the released 322 still carry 429, counted case-insensitively. That is more than one name per dataset on average, and even where names coincide, whether the stimuli and the instructions matched is something you learn only by opening the original paper. What the corpus guarantees is which of two boxes each of those 429 names falls into, resting state or task.

Quality metrics work the same way. qc.tsv records head motion per run: the mean and maximum framewise displacement, the fraction of frames above 0.5mm, and the total volume count. The table holds one line for every run in quasiraw, and the other release is a subset of it, so one table filters either one. Yet the authors did not drop runs on those metrics. They set the threshold loosely on purpose and wrote that anyone needing stricter conditions should filter from qc.tsv themselves. They also noted that residual motion effects remain because of the loose threshold.

That choice is why there are two releases. quasiraw carries the fMRIPrep output and the confound files as they are, while preproc is the side with the authors' default denoising applied. The two differ in size. Denoising scrubs the frames above 0.5mm, and a run left with fewer than 50 volumes afterwards is dropped from preproc. So the same dataset comes out with different numbers.

Release Datasets Participants Functional runs Volumes
quasiraw 322 11,379 45,731 11.39 million
preproc 320 11,320 45,019 10.92 million

Source: the Open-fMIND dataset card. Volumes are three-dimensional time points, and the preproc figure excludes the scrubbed frames.

A gap of 712 runs and two datasets is small against the whole, but how to read it is not. It puts in numbers the fact that the size of "the same dataset" changes with where the quality bar sits. Two papers drawing on the same material can report different sample sizes depending on which figure they wrote down. That is likely why the authors released both and printed both sets of numbers side by side.

4

Six columns against the UK Biobank

The authors' claim converges on one point. Pretraining on this public corpus reaches downstream performance comparable to pretraining on the UK Biobank. The UK Biobank is a large cohort holding brain scans from tens of thousands of UK adults, and using it means submitting a research plan and obtaining approval. If the comparison holds, a similar starting point can be built from material anyone can download without approval.

The experiment is set up like this. Two copies of a transformer encoder called Brain-Semantoks were prepared, one pretrained on Open-fMIND and one on the authors' existing UK Biobank checkpoint. A third encoder with random weights served as the baseline. All three were frozen as feature extractors with a simple linear model on top, and scored on three public benchmarks. HCP-YA covers 955 healthy young adults, ABIDE up to 1,035 people in an autism spectrum context, and ADHD-200 up to 298 in an attention-deficit hyperactivity context.

The paper reports results as a bar chart rather than a table, with the numbers written into the text. Collected, the six columns in the text read as follows.

Benchmark / task Metric Open-fMIND UK Biobank Random init
HCP-YA / general cognition factor Pearson r 0.415 0.409 0.380
HCP-YA / sex AUROC 0.945 0.945 0.877
ABIDE / diagnosis Balanced accuracy 62.0% 63.1% Not in the text
ABIDE / sex Balanced accuracy 62.8% 65.7% Not in the text
ABIDE / age, 5 classes Balanced accuracy 45.6% 40.6% Not in the text
ADHD-200 / sex Balanced accuracy 67.2% 70.0% Not in the text

Source: Barbano et al., arXiv:2611.21215, main text. The random initialization figures for ABIDE and ADHD-200 appear only in the figure, not in the text.

Start with what is confirmed. Open-fMIND leads in two of the six columns: 0.415 against 0.409 on the HCP-YA general cognition factor, and 45.6% against 40.6% on five-class age in ABIDE, a margin of 5.0 points. HCP-YA sex is level at 0.945. It trails in three, by between 1.1 and 2.9 points. And on the ADHD-200 diagnosis and age tasks, the paper records that neither pretrained model was clearly better than random initialization.

The authors bundle these numbers into a claim of approaching a gated resource. This article's reading is that the phrasing is accurate and that calling the two equal would overstate it. Three columns trail, and the 0.006 lead on the HCP-YA cognition factor is small next to the 0.095 standard deviation across folds that the paper reports. The centre of gravity in this comparison, though, sits on the starting line rather than on the ranking. Either pretraining beats random initialization clearly, 0.945 against 0.877 on HCP-YA sex, and that gain came from material downloadable without an approval process. That is what the experiment demonstrates.

How much weight the numbers can carry is also written into the paper. Each of the three encoders was pretrained exactly once, and the error bars on the chart capture only the variation from resampling cross-validation. Variation across repeated pretraining runs was not measured. The cross-validation designs differ by task as well. The HCP-YA cognition factor used nested cross-validation with family relationships kept inside a single fold, 10 folds repeated 20 times, giving a standard deviation over 200 folds. HCP-YA sex used 5 folds repeated 20 times, and ABIDE and ADHD-200 used stratified 5-fold repeated 10 times. Read that design before reading a gap of 1.1 to 2.9 points as a ranking.

Taken at face value, the two pretraining resources are strong in different places. The UK Biobank side leads on tasks like sex and diagnosis, where a large cohort's homogeneous acquisition helps, and Open-fMIND leads on tasks like age banding, where having seen a wide span of ages and acquisition conditions helps. Given how far apart the two populations and scanning conditions are, that split is not a strange distribution. The paper does not analyse why it happens, though, and the explanation above is this article's.

5

What it is good for and what it is risky for

The authors set out intended uses in both the dataset card and the paper. On the good-fit side there are three.

  • Pretraining that needs no labels. Masked reconstruction, contrastive learning, next-timepoint prediction: objectives that learn representations without ground truth are what this corpus was designed for. The weakness of unharmonized task labels does not bite here.
  • Using the atlases as multiple views. Because the same run exists under 52 atlases, one acquisition can be treated as seen from several angles. The authors advise picking a family first and a variant within it, since the 52 are spread unevenly across families. Schaefer's 14 and Yan's 12 come to 26 between them, exactly half, so drawing uniformly from the 52 puts half the views in two families.
  • The route for supervised use. If you need targets, build them from the original studies' metadata, and use the provenance attached to each file to trace which OpenNeuro dataset it came from. That is the authors' guidance.

The risky side is spelled out more concretely. The authors advise against merging phenotypes across studies as they stand, because identical column names do not mean identical values. The same "age" is a whole-year figure in one study and a band in another. Stack them vertically while instruments, units and coding schemes differ from study to study and you manufacture a distribution that never existed. The corpus does not harmonize that difference for you.

A warning is attached on the atlas side too. The 52 views are deterministic transforms of the same run, so they add no independent information about a subject. They are a device for learning diversity, not a 52-fold increase in sample size. The authors state that the effective sample stays at 11,379 subjects and 45,731 runs. Write 2.38 million timeseries down as a sample size and you inflate it 52-fold against runs and more than 200-fold against subjects.

The population statement is equally plain. The authors say not to assume the corpus represents any particular population, and the numbers show why. Sex is known for 69.4% of participants (38.3% female, 31.0% male, 0.1% a third category) and age for 71.7%. Among those with a known age the mean is 28.7 and the median 24.0. Of the 322 datasets, 263 uploaded a participants file, and those 263 hold 9,428 participants. Having the file does not mean the cells are filled. Converting 69.4% into people gives roughly 7,900, so more than 1,500 slots sit empty even among the 9,428 covered by a participants file (that subtraction is this article's, lining up two figures the paper reports separately). Research recruitment being what it is, the corpus clusters around university age, and anyone with a clinical application in mind should look at that distribution first.

Motion thresholds and site differences are left to the user as well. Since the authors declared the loose threshold a choice and removed no runs, re-filtering with qc.tsv becomes the user's own work in any analysis needing stricter conditions. They also note that differences arising from scanner and site remain and should be accounted for.

The license is not one sheet laid over the whole corpus; it differs by dataset. Of the 322, 251 are CC0 and 30 carry no license statement and were treated as CC0 under OpenNeuro's terms. But the 3 CC-BY-SA datasets require the same license on derivatives, and the 1 CC-BY-NC dataset bars commercial use. Treat the whole as CC0 and four datasets catch you out. Anyone planning to train a commercial model has to screen those out of LICENSES.tsv first, and should keep separate the fact that the code repository's MIT license covers the code only.

6

Why Pebblous is watching this dataset

"We don't have enough data" carries two meanings at once. One is that the volume genuinely is not there. The other is that the volume is there but not in a usable state. The two call for entirely different prescriptions. The first means collecting or buying more; the second means tidying what you already hold. In practice the two go unseparated, and the first prescription arrives first. Collection is easy to put in a budget line, and tidying does not look like an accomplishment.

What makes Open-fMIND interesting is that it put numbers on how far the second prescription alone gets you. The material was 322 studies anyone could already download. Three things were added: pinning the preprocessing tool to a single version, extracting every result into one fixed form of 52 atlases, and attaching to each file a record of where it came from and what it passed through. Specification, preprocessing and lineage are exactly the trio Pebblous points at when it talks about AI-Ready Data.

Of the three, lineage is the most underrated. Performance comparisons have to be measured again when the model architecture changes, and the processing chain in a sidecar does not. If you know which study the material came from and which version and which command processed it, then when something goes wrong later you can count how far back you have to roll. Finding the blast radius when a dataset's authors ask for withdrawal, and separating out which parts need rerunning when the preprocessing version moves up, come from the same record.

The point is how far down that record goes here. Every run carries the accession number and version of its source dataset and the digest of the fMRIPrep container that processed it. A withdrawal request means pulling the affected runs by one accession number; a preprocessing version bump means selecting only the runs with a different digest and rerunning those. One query each. The first release already contains one dataset excluded at its authors' request. A corpus where you cannot count what to delete when the next such request arrives, and one where a single number counts it, are not the same asset. That is also why those 17 datasets that entered without a version record remain a gap.

There is a clear limit to generalizing from this case, though. The work was possible because the 322 studies were already uploaded under a shared specification called BIDS. That is a foundation the brain imaging field spent more than a decade building, and only on top of it does reprocessing everything through one pipeline hold together. Even so, 2,637 of 14,016 participants did not make it to the end. Attempt the same thing on in-house data with no shared specification and the attrition will be higher. The conclusion that reprocessing what you own beats buying more only arrives once you count the cost of meeting a specification alongside it.

So the question to take from this case narrows to one. If we reprocess the data our company already holds to a single specification, do we reach the same performance for less than buying more? Answering it means counting three things: how many pieces of scattered material there are and in what formats, what share of them survive a single pipeline end to end, and what it costs to attach provenance and processing records to the survivors. Open-fMIND can serve as a comparison because it published all three.

The previous article in the Data Quality series took the same question from the regulatory side. Korea's PIPA AI Exception Lets You Train on Raw Data If You Can Prove It set out what a company must prove before reusing personal data it already holds for training. This article looks at the same reuse from the technical side. That the records the two demand overlap is no accident. Writing down where something came from, what it passed through and what dropped out is the same starting point for a regulatory response as it is for securing training resources. Editor's Note

The size figures, the curation steps, the preprocessing settings, the atlas composition, the performance comparison and the usage cautions were confirmed against the text of arXiv:2611.21215, published on November 16, 2026, and the Hugging Face dataset card. For storage size and shard composition, the license distribution, and the contents of qc.tsv and task_type_mapping.tsv, the dataset card is the more detailed source and this article followed it. Because the paper carries performance as a figure rather than a table, only the six columns written into the text are reproduced here, and the random initialization figures for ABIDE and ADHD-200 are left out.

The two sources diverge in three places. The paper counts 14 parcellation families and the card 15. The paper gives 4.32TB across 1,787 shards while the card gives 4.35TB across 1,821 (this article used the card figure, where the per-folder breakdown adds up). The 6,044-hour total appears only on the card, not in the paper. The statement that the code repository's MIT license does not cover the data is not something the repository says; it is this article's reading, drawn from the paper recording a separate license per dataset.

Why the count fell from 359 to 322 is absent from the paper, and inferring the character of those 37 from the fact that the participant counts reconcile exactly is this article's work. So is the subtraction in section 5, matching the 9,428 participants covered by a participants file against the 69.4% with a known sex. The closing box in section 4, which explains the split in task strengths by the difference in populations; section 6, which separates "we don't have enough data" into two meanings; and the caveat that the cost of meeting a specification has to be counted alongside are all this article's readings too. The note that each encoder was pretrained only once, and the cross-validation designs, are carried over from what the paper states itself. Whether Open-fMIND is being used in later research could not be confirmed in a primary source, so it is not stated here.
