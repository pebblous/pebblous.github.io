## LinkedIn (EN)

Researchers at MIT and NYU have ranked 774 US occupations by how closely robots already sit to the work, and the order runs against nearly every AI-and-jobs forecast of the past three years. Pile-driver operators and sewing machine operators are at the top. Professors of foreign languages and of political science are at the bottom.

Read the same occupation table through the generative AI exposure index and the ranking roughly inverts: across occupational families the two correlate at −0.93. Lower wages and less required schooling both track with higher robot exposure, and the creative share of the work tracks it most steeply of all.

On the map the spread widens. In Wyoming 31% of workers are in high-exposure industries; in Washington, DC the figure is 7%.

The index, though, was not built by watching robots. The team ran 78 academic, industry and standards documents through a language model to pull out 16 robot capabilities, then scored that list against O*NET's catalogue of workplace abilities. The authors ask that ROE be read as rank order rather than absolute level, and describe it as an upper bound on where physical automation could reach.

What held attention longest was the material rather than the result. Documents record demonstrations that worked. Work nobody has yet managed, and that therefore appears nowhere, is not on the capability list at all. The material of the measurement, not its subject, set the shape of the conclusion.

## Facebook (EN)

Running a pile-driver. Pushing fabric through a sewing machine. Operating a logging rig.

Those are the jobs a paper from last week puts closest to robots, at the very top of its list.

At the bottom of the same list: professors of foreign languages and literature, professors of political science.

The paper is the Robotics Occupational Exposure index, posted to arXiv on October 16 by researchers at MIT and NYU. It scores 774 US occupations against what robots can do, and reading the same table through the generative AI exposure index turns the order roughly upside down.

The lower the wage, the lower the schooling required, the smaller the creative share of the work, the higher the index runs.

Standing in front of that table, I realised how one-directional the AI-and-jobs conversation has been for me these past few years. Office work first, I had been told. Credentialed professionals first.

What I kept returning to, though, was not the ranking but what the ranking was made of.

Nobody on the team watched a robot. They ran 78 documents through a language model to pull out 16 robot capabilities: academic papers, industry material, government and standards texts. Then they scored that list against the ability catalogue in the US occupational database.

So what the index is measuring is a robot on paper.

Documents record the demonstrations that worked and the research that succeeded. Lifting a box in a warehouse, walking over rough ground: these come up often enough in papers and conference decks to settle into the list under clear names. Work nobody has yet managed, written down nowhere, is not on the list at all.

The material of the measurement, rather than its subject, set the conclusion. The authors say as much themselves, as their second limitation.

One question stayed behind.

"If the capability list for robots were written from field records instead of documents, how would it change? And where are those records piling up right now?"

The answer lands awkwardly. The data that trains physical AI is gathered in warehouses and on farms, at construction sites and in logistics terminals. Those are the industries this index scores highest.

One set of sites is both where the data comes from and where the machines built on that data arrive first.

Pebblous weighs the quality of AI training data with DataClinic. With robot data, training runs fine on trajectories, sensor values and success flags alone, which is exactly why which site, under what conditions, watching whose motion goes missing so easily. Leave that column blank and there is no retracing later where the data may be used and where it may not.

The index counts how far the technology could reach. It does not count who is standing there now.

If the data that came from someone's hands is teaching the machine that would take over their work, what else belongs in the record beside it?
