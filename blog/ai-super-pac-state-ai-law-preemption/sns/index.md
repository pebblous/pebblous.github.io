연방 상원은 주(州) AI법을 무력화하는 선점 조항을 99대1로 삭제했습니다. 국회에서 완패한 싸움을 AI 업계는 이번엔 슈퍼팩 자금으로 다시 열고 있습니다. 두 최대 AI 팩이 발표·모금 기준 2억 달러를 넘겼고, 표적은 38개 주가 제정한 AI 규제법입니다.

이 중 캘리포니아 AB 2013은 학습 데이터의 출처와 저작권 상태, 개인정보 포함 여부를 공개하도록 요구합니다. 그래서 "연방 선점이 이기느냐"라는 관할권 다툼은, 데이터의 언어로 옮기면 "학습 데이터의 출처를 밝힐 의무가 살아남느냐"가 됩니다.

다만 이 자금은 정책이 아니라 사람을 겨냥합니다. 뉴욕 RAISE Act를 발의한 알렉스 보레스에게, 한쪽은 지원금을 보냈고 다른 쪽은 330만 달러짜리 공격광고를 집행했습니다. 그 광고는 정작 AI 규제를 거의 언급하지 않았습니다. 규범의 운명이 법 조문 토론이 아니라 인물 선택으로 갈리는 구조입니다.

AI-레디 데이터의 규범은 기술 표준 위원회보다 선거 자금에서 먼저 결정될 수 있습니다. 어느 규제 모델이 이기든 데이터의 계보를 증명하는 일은 여전히 실무의 몫으로 남습니다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Whether to regulate AI, not how, is becoming a question that money is trying to settle first.

The U.S. Senate stripped a clause preempting state AI laws by a vote of 99 to 1. Having lost decisively in Congress, the AI industry is now reopening the fight through super PACs. Two of the largest AI-aligned PACs have raised more than $200 million, and their target is the body of AI laws that 38 states passed last year.

Among them, California's AB 2013 requires generative AI developers to disclose the sources, copyright status, and personal-data content of their training sets. Translated into the language of data, the fight over federal preemption becomes a narrower question: whether the duty to disclose where training data comes from survives.

The money aims at people, not policy. Alex Bores, who authored New York's RAISE Act, drew support from one PAC and a $3.3 million attack campaign from another. Those ads barely mentioned AI regulation at all. The fate of a norm is being decided by who gets elected, not by debate over statutory text.

The norms of AI-ready data may be settled in campaign finance before they ever reach a standards committee. Whichever regulatory model wins, proving the provenance of your data remains the practitioner's job.

## Facebook (EN)

There are two ways a law can disappear.

One is to lose a vote in Congress. The other is for the person who wrote it to lose an election.

What is happening in the United States right now looks more like the second.

The Senate struck down a clause that would have preempted state AI laws, 99 to 1. The bipartisan resistance was clear. Yet the industry that lost that vote turned its attention to the people who cast such votes. The instrument was the super PAC.

A single primary in New York shows the method in miniature. A state lawmaker who authored an AI-safety bill drew millions of dollars at once, some to support him and some to defeat him. The attack ads barely mentioned AI regulation. What the money aimed at was not a policy but a person.

For anyone who works with data, this carries a sharper meaning. Some of the targeted state laws require disclosing where training data comes from. "Does that obligation survive, or does it get swept away with the flow of campaign money?"

While the norms sway with the money, one thing stays within reach: keeping a clear record of where your own data came from. Whichever side of regulation prevails, the question it asks is the same. What data was this model built on?
