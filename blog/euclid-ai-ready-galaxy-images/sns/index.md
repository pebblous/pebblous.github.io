우주망원경이 찍은 은하 사진을 AI가 바로 쓸 수 있게 다듬어 놓고, 가장 이상한 것을 찾게 했다. 처음 걸려 나온 1,681개는 대부분 아무것도 찍히지 않은 빈 화면이었다.

중국과학원 국가천문대와 국가천문데이터센터의 연구자 다섯 사람이 9월 26일 arXiv에 올린 작업이다. 유럽우주국 유클리드 우주망원경이 찍은 은하 사진 365,513장을 모두 같은 화소 규격으로 바꾸고, 사진마다 384개 숫자로 된 특징값을 미리 계산해 함께 공개했다. 논문 제목에 붙은 'AI-Ready'가 가리키는 것은 정확히 이 둘이다.

규격이 고정되면 같은 원본을 연구자마다 다시 자르고 다시 특징을 뽑는 일이 사라진다. 분류도, 닮은 은하 검색도, 이상 탐지도 한 번 뽑아 둔 같은 숫자 위에서 돌아간다.

다만 규격을 고정하는 일이 문제가 풀린다는 약속까지 하지는 않았다. 라벨을 1%만 주고 푼 형태 분류 네 문제 가운데 셋은 곧바로 쓸 만한 점수가 나왔다. 나머지 하나, 나선팔이 있느냐는 문제는 아무것도 학습하지 않고 다수 쪽으로만 답하는 기준선 아래로 떨어졌다. 정답을 전부 주어도 거기 머물렀다. 나선팔이 있다고 확신을 가지고 표시된 은하가 너무 적은 탓이다.

'AI가 바로 쓸 수 있게 준비했다'는 말은 성능에 대한 약속이 아니라 재료 규격에 대한 계약이다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

A pipeline built to make a space telescope's galaxy images ready for machine learning was asked to find the strangest ones. The first 1,681 it returned were mostly blank frames.

Five researchers from the National Astronomical Observatories of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and the National Astronomical Data Center posted the work to arXiv on September 26. Every cutout from Euclid's visible-light camera, 365,513 of them, was resized to one pixel format, and a feature vector of 384 numbers was precomputed for each and released alongside. That pair is all the paper's title means by 'AI-Ready'.

Fix the format and nobody has to cut the same original twice or extract the features again. Classification, similarity search and anomaly detection all run on the same numbers.

What fixing the format did not do was promise that the problems would come out solved. Given 1 percent of the labels, three of four morphology tasks scored well. The fourth, deciding whether a galaxy has spiral arms, landed below the majority-class baseline and stayed there even when every label was supplied. Too few galaxies are marked as having arms with any confidence.

'Ready for AI' is a contract about materials, not a promise about performance.

## Facebook (EN)

A black square with nothing in it.

Asked to pick out the strangest things among a space telescope's galaxy images, the pipeline put frames like that near the top of its list.

Cutouts sliced off at the edge. The cross-shaped flare of a bright foreground star. Two galaxies caught overlapping.

These are the 1,681 anomaly candidates that surfaced after researchers at China's National Astronomical Observatories put some 365,000 Euclid galaxy images into a single format.

It was the kind of place where you might expect an unknown object. What actually came out were flaws left by the exposure and the processing, and the authors said as much: useful for data quality control, not a confirmation of anything astrophysical.

I did not read that as a failure.

Gather data in one place for the first time and measure it against a single ruler, and what jumps out first is usually missing values, truncation and noise. The sky turns out to behave the same way.

The paper's title carries the phrase 'AI-Ready'. Two things, and only two, were fixed to earn it. One pixel format for the images, one coordinate system for the features.

In exchange for fixing those two, it promised nothing about which problems would come out solved.

On those same feature values, four questions about galaxy shape went different ways. Three yielded when only 1 percent of the answers were supplied. The fourth, whether a galaxy has spiral arms, refused even when all of them were. Not because a machine cannot see spiral arms, but because too few galaxies are marked as having them with any confidence.

I have started thinking of this as a contract about materials. Matching the specification so the same material never has to be cut twice. What dish it becomes is a separate question.

The same order shows up in data quality work at Pebblous. Asked to find the outliers, the first list we hand back is usually not a discovery but a flaw in collection. Until that list is cleared, there is no moving on.

"When your organization says the data has been made ready for AI, what exactly was fixed?"

The format, or the performance. Writing that list out tends to show the empty column first.
