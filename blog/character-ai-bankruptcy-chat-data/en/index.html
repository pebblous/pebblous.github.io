Executive Summary

This article reads the Chapter 11 petition that Character Technologies, the operator of Character.AI, filed in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware on October 12, 2026. Alongside it runs a second question: where, inside those same court papers, do the chat logs its users built up now stand? This is not a liquidation but a court-supervised sale of the company, and the app and its subscriptions keep running while the sale proceeds.

The number that catches the eye in the filings is 163 million. It appears in a first-day motion filed the following day as the count of current and past platform users. In that same motion, Character Technologies stated that it does not believe those users are creditors to whom notice must be provided. Reaching all of them even by email would be cost-prohibitive, the filing argues. Yet that document also asks the court to strike users' names and addresses from the public record. Within one filing, a user is both an individual who must be protected and a party who need not be told.

The facts and figures in this article come from PPC Land's analysis of the court filings, from reporting by TechCrunch and CBS News, and from the press release and the notice the company issued itself. Section 4, which turns the question toward companies that hold data, is nobody's claim but this article's reading.

What Disney Bid On Is Not the App

Character.AI is a chatbot platform where users build their own characters and talk to them. Noam Shazeer, a co-author of the Transformer paper, and Daniel De Freitas founded it in late 2021; both had been Google engineers, and both went back to Google in a 2024 deal. During the platform's fastest stretch of growth, in September 2022, the company's own count was more than 2 billion messages exchanged with its bots in five months, with active users averaging more than two hours on the platform daily. By October 2024, monthly active users were reported at 20 million.

Character Technologies, the company that runs it, filed a voluntary Chapter 11 petition in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware on October 12. The case number is 26-11603, and assets and liabilities are each listed in the $50 million to $100 million range. The process, as the petition puts it, is a sale meant to draw out as much value as possible. The reasons set down in the court papers run along two lines, cost and liability. The field of AI entertainment has proven costly to develop and grow, and those costs, together with potential litigation-related liabilities and regulatory engagement, left the company unable to generate sufficient cash flow.

The leading bidder is Disney. Disney Worldwide Services offered $20 million, and Character Technologies has asked the court to name Disney the stalking horse bidder. A stalking horse sets the floor price at auction, so a better offer can still change the outcome, and the designation itself needs court approval. The bigger question is what the $20 million is attached to. Disney is buying a non-exclusive perpetual license to certain Company intellectual property and certain Company claims; the chatbot platform itself is not in that package. If no buyer steps forward to carry on operations, the platform will be wound down.

Plenty of coverage sets the $20 million next to the $2.7 billion Google paid in August 2024 for a license and the return of the founders. The two numbers are not prices on the same thing. The $2.7 billion was the total on a deal that bundled a technology license with people, and the $20 million is a floor price on a non-exclusive license to certain intellectual property. To write that a company's worth has fallen to a fraction of itself, the numerator and the denominator have to point at the same object, and here they do not.

Disney is not putting up money alone. The press release the company issued on the day of the filing sets out the stalking horse terms as "$20 million and Disney's provision of transition services using former Company employees it has hired." Those transition services, the release says, "will help the Company safely operate using its platform for a limited period of time and support its sale process." The people Disney hired away earlier now come back, on Disney's side of the ledger, to help keep the platform running until the sale closes.

That Disney is here at all marks a turn within a single year. In September 2025, Disney sent Character.AI a cease-and-desist letter over its copyrighted characters being posted on the platform without permission. In September 2026, Disney announced that it had hired Karandeep Anand, the former chief executive of Character.AI, as the first chief technology officer in its history; he started on October 2. The scale of the staff movement appears in several analyses citing the wage-related motion. As of September 30 the company had roughly 86 full-time employees, of whom 66 had already accepted Disney's offers of employment before the bankruptcy filing, and 56 of those had submitted their resignations. Eighteen full-time employees remained on the filing date. The name at the top of the filings' list of 20 largest unsecured creditors is Google, with a trade payable of $4,148,944.

The Clause That Already Allowed Disclosure in a Sale

The company posted a notice on its blog the day it filed. The platform remains up and running, every service can still be used, subscriptions stay active and billing is unchanged for now. On data, it put the matter this way: "Your information continues to be protected under our Privacy Policy, and our data privacy and security practices are unchanged."

CBS News, covering the filing, pointed to what that privacy policy says. A clause in it states that the data collected from users, meaning chat logs, voice recordings, images and video, may be disclosed in connection with a sale. The two statements do not contradict each other. They fit together exactly. Because the policy is unchanged, the clause permitting disclosure in a sale is still in place.

This clause was not created by the bankruptcy. It had been there since the moment anyone signed up and started a first conversation. It is a sentence written in advance for the situation in which data gets handled as part of a company's assets, and it is not a rare sentence in this industry. Most of the time it does nothing at all; it works once, when the company is sold. That once is now.

One thing the public filings have yet to answer is whether user data is among the assets being sold. PPC Land, which read the court documents directly, noted that the petitions do not address the point, and the Disney bid is described only in terms of certain intellectual property and certain claims. What becomes of accumulated chat logs if no buyer emerges and the platform is wound down is likewise written down nowhere. The clause permitting disclosure exists; a sentence saying what actually goes where does not.

A user has two handles to pull right now: delete the account, or export the data. Both can be done from the app and the website, according to the company. The problem is how long it takes. According to RoboRhythms, citing the company's help center, an export request takes three to four weeks to arrive, and the download link that comes by email expires seven days after it is sent. The sale, meanwhile, is targeted to close in late November or early December, roughly seven weeks from the filing date.

Laying the two bars over each other shows how thin the margin is. Three to four weeks against seven weeks is the arithmetic for somebody who requests an export today. Put it off a week and the arrival date moves that much closer to the close; put it off several and the result lands after the sale. The seven-day expiry is sensible security design in normal operation, but if the email goes unopened for a while, whether the same company is still there to re-request from becomes an open question.

The path RoboRhythms laid out runs like this. On the web, go from your profile to Profile Settings, then Account, then Account and Data Management, then Data Export; in the app, go from your profile to Settings, then Legal and Privacy, and reach that item. The email can land in a spam folder, so it is worth checking there, and the file is best downloaded the day it arrives. Deleting the account is safer once the file is in hand, and canceling a subscription and deleting an account are separate procedures, so doing one does not carry the other along with it.

The Company Does Not Count Users as Creditors to Notify

On October 13, the day after the petition, the company filed a number of first-day motions. A motion to keep paying wages and benefits, a cash management motion and a motion to approve bidding procedures went up together. This article follows the three among them that PPC Land read directly in the court record. Document 6 asks for authority to pay up to $2.5 million to critical vendors, the suppliers of servers, data hosting and trust-and-safety services. Character Technologies told the court that without their continued service it "risks losing all of the data, code, intellectual property, and more that make up its entire business." Document 7 is an automatic-stay request to prevent contracts from being terminated on the sole ground of the bankruptcy filing.

This article's subject sits in Document 8. In it, the company asks the court for permission to redact "the names, home addresses, email addresses, and other personally identifiable information and personal data of all natural persons" from the papers it files. Records in a bankruptcy case are public by default, so left alone, individuals' names and addresses stay in public documents. The proposal is to file redacted versions publicly and send unredacted versions only to the court and designated parties. Where that request actually bites can be seen in the creditor list. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania and the Commonwealth of Kentucky appear as litigation creditors, and beside them is a minor plaintiff whose name and address are redacted.

Two laws are cited as grounds. One is the California Consumer Privacy Act; Character Technologies judged itself likely to fall within the law's scope, since most of its operations and staff are in California and it receives personal information from consumers numbering in the millions. The other is the GDPR, and the argument the company makes there is unusual. It cannot rely on Article 6(1)(c), compliance with a legal obligation, as a basis for disclosing personal data. The legal obligations that provision speaks of are limited to obligations under UK or EU law, the reading goes, and a foreign law such as the U.S. Bankruptcy Code does not count among them. That leaves Article 6(1)(f), legitimate interests, as the only remaining basis, and the filing cites UK Information Commissioner's Office guidance dated March 23, 2026, alongside it. PPC Land also noted that the guidance is recorded as last accessed on October 9. That is the day after the board set up a restructuring committee on October 8, and three days before the bankruptcy filing. How to handle personal data was a question already worked through at the desk where the petition was being written.

The figure of 163 million comes from a footnote in that same document. It is the count of current and past platform users, and this is the first time a figure of that size appears in the bankruptcy record. Serving all of them, the footnote says, even through email, would be cost-prohibitive. And the position taken in Document 8 itself is this: the company "does not believe its current and past platform users are creditors to whom notice must be provided," adding that it has historically communicated with them through the platform, blog posts and online community pages. Official creditors are to receive notice under the bankruptcy rules; users are to be told through the claims agent's website, a post on the company blog and a link inside the platform. The claims agent is Stretto, and the case page is at cases.stretto.com/chartech.

The press release that went out that day reaches for different words. By its account, once the first-day motions are approved, the company will be able to continue paying wages and benefits to employees, "honor commitments to platform users" and satisfy obligations to vendors and partners. In the press release, users are the party to whom commitments are owed; in the court filing, they are not creditors to whom notice must be provided. The two sentences came out of the same company on the same day.

Another detail deserves a note. PPC Land pointed out that Document 7, filed the same day, describes the platform as "an online platform and application that hosts tens of millions of users across the globe." Within one bundle of filings, in other words, the size of the user base is written two different ways. Neither figure is a count of monthly active users. The 20 million reported in October 2024 was the monthly active figure, and 163 million is a cumulative count that includes past sign-ups. The three numbers should not be read against the same denominator.

Inside the single filing that is Document 8, users appear in two capacities. They are subjects whose personal data must be kept out of the public record, and they are not the party the progress of the case has to be reported to. The reason to shield the data comes from data protection law; the reason not to give notice comes from bankruptcy law. While each law says something correct from where it stands, what lies in front of a person wondering where their own chat logs sit on a list of assets for sale is one blog post the company published and a claims agent's website where legal documents in English pile up. And that post says nothing of Disney, of the transition services, or of the employees who moved across.

4

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Case

This news usually gets read as an episode in the rise and fall of AI chatbots, or as a scene in the argument over protecting teenagers. From where data is handled, something else shows up first. When an AI service closes, data is written at the head of the list the papers grab for in a hurry. The order of the words used in Document 6, the motion asking to pay vendors first, runs exactly that way: data, then code, then intellectual property. No filing yet says which column that data goes into in the sale. The reason to protect it was written down in haste; the terms for handing it over were not written down at all.

Which leaves the mirror question standing. What status does the data we hold on behalf of our customers have once our company is gone? Putting a clause about data transfer in a sale or merger into the terms of service is an industry standard, and because it is standard, nobody reads that sentence on an ordinary day. The day the sentence takes effect is the company's most chaotic day, and on that day the sentence cannot be changed.

So there are three things worth checking while the days are ordinary. First, whether the inventory of data assets we hold records, alongside each item, the scope of the consent under which it arrived. Second, whether the terms of service and that inventory agree on how far the consent travels when the company is sold or wound up. Third, whether the time budgeted for handling deletion and export requests was designed only for ordinary conditions. Three to four weeks is a conscientious design in ordinary times; in front of a seven-week sale it means something else.

One more point. The right to data portability is guaranteed in law, but conversations accumulated in an AI service have no standard format to move into, so there is a wide field where the right does not actually work. This blog took up that gap in July, in When an AI Companion Shuts Down, the Conversation Has Nowhere to Go. This bankruptcy shows what shape that general argument takes when it meets an actual case.

Thank you for reading this far. If you use Character.AI, the data export is not worth putting off. The company notice is here, and the analysis that read the court filings is here. We would be glad to hear how your own terms of service put what happens to customer data when the company is sold, if you have a sentence on it.
