# Sama Closes Its Doors, but the Lawsuit Stays Open

_Sama_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> The company that made the data Meta and OpenAI worked with is shutting down. Sama, headquartered in San Francisco, has entered liquidation, and TIME broke the story on November 9, 2026. The legal entity, Samasource Impact Sourcing, Inc., handed its remaining assets to a new entity called Sama ABC on October 23, and wrote to creditors on November 6 to tell them so.

> The books attached to that letter, as of October, show about $1 million in assets against $17 million in debt. Divide the debt by the assets and the answer is seventeen. The figures are unaudited, and a bank holds a priority claim over substantially all of the assets, so there is little left for unsecured creditors. The company gave two reasons for winding down. Revenue had fallen as competition grew sharper, and a class action was pending that would have taken significant resources to defend. Which lawsuit that means, the letter does not say.

> Sections 1 through 4 are facts set down in the TIME report, the English Wikipedia entry, the Kenyan court record and industry coverage. Sections 5 and 6, which tie this liquidation to a generational change in labeling work, are this article's own reading and not a position the company has taken.

## Seventeen Dollars Owed for Every Dollar Left

The process by which the company disappears began on October 23. That day Samasource Impact Sourcing, Inc. handed what assets remained to a new entity called Sama ABC. The letter introduces that entity as one run by people who specialize in winding down distressed and insolvent businesses, and states flatly that despite the similar name the two are unrelated as legal entities. Once the assets have been taken in and settled, no company called Sama is left on the books.

The letter that reached creditors on November 6 is what made those books public. As of October, assets stood at about $1 million and debt at $17 million. The figures carry a note that they are unaudited, and the date of the letter sits nearly a month away from the date of the balance sheet. The direction is plain all the same. What is owed is seventeen times what is left. A bank also holds a priority claim over substantially all of the assets, which leaves little for the unsecured creditors ranked behind it.

In the letter's own words, the company wound down because of "declining revenues due to increased competition that was compounded by recent class action litigation that would have required significant resources and years to defend successfully." That sentence names no case. Which suit it means, and what becomes of that suit now, the letter does not say.

The books are not the only thing the letter sets down. It says Sama leaves behind a reputation as one of the earliest advocates of impact sourcing, and that a model of deliberately recruiting from marginalized communities in East Africa brought life skills, technical training and digital work to more than 70,000 people. That headcount is the company's own phrasing and has not been checked from outside. The words it attaches to the jobs are "at high living wages and benefits." The take-home pay of the same company's Kenyan workers, $1.32 to $2 an hour depending on seniority and performance, comes from TIME's January 2023 investigation.

## From Poverty Relief to the Back End of OpenAI

It starts in 2008. Leila Janah founded a nonprofit called Samasource and gave the model a name, impact sourcing. The design was that data work, broken into small enough pieces and handed to low-income workers in Kenya, Uganda and India, needs only basic English and a short course to begin, and that the wage then becomes a ladder out of poverty. Janah drew the model from a stint at a call center in Mumbai and from time spent in Africa.

In 2019 the organization was restructured as the for-profit Sama, with the original nonprofit staying on as its largest shareholder. In January of the following year Janah died of a rare soft-tissue cancer at thirty-seven, and Wendy Gonzalez, the chief operating officer, took over as chief executive and holds the job still. Over those years the client list came to include automotive and navigation, augmented reality and biotech companies, along with Meta and OpenAI. Meta sent content moderation and smart glasses data. OpenAI sent the classification of harmful content for ChatGPT training.

Two TIME investigations are where that back end became visible from outside. The February 2022 piece covered the Facebook moderation center in Nairobi. People recruited across Africa in the belief that they were taking call center jobs learned what the work actually was only after signing and moving to Nairobi. The January 2023 piece was about the OpenAI side. Kenyan workers read and classified harmful content for ChatGPT training, and take-home pay ran between $1.32 and $2 an hour depending on seniority and performance. One worker quoted in that story called the job torture. Fairwork, which rates labor conditions, scored the company 5 out of 10.

The company left this work early. The OpenAI contract stopped in February 2022 after the Kenyan team objected to requests that were not in the agreement, and the final batch of data went out in March, eight months ahead of schedule. On January 10, 2023 the company announced it would drop all remaining work involving sensitive content, and finished that exit in March of the same year. The company now being liquidated had left this line of business three years earlier.

> [!callout]
> One reason the answers today's models give sound so settled is that somebody classified and screened that data one item at a time. Sama was the first-generation company that organized that work under the banner of ending poverty.

## The Year Meta Ended the Contract and Nairobi Emptied

The collapse is packed into a single year, 2026. On March 5 Ars Technica carried accounts from Sama workers who said that while screening footage shot on Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses they had seen people using the bathroom and people having sex. In April Meta ended a data contract that had run for nearly seven years. Meta said the photos and videos are users' private material and that human review is part of product improvement users consent to, and added, "We've also decided to end our work with Sama because they don't meet our standards." On the sixteenth of the same month, a layoff of 1,108 Nairobi staff was announced.

In August, plaintiffs in a US privacy class action over the smart glasses voluntarily dropped their claims against Sama. On September 15 the company formally ended its Kenya and Uganda operations. The Nairobi and Kampala offices closed, finishing nearly a decade in East Africa. Then the assets moved on October 23, and the letter to creditors went out on November 6.

The contract ending and the layoff fall in the same April, but the footage story, the East Africa exit, the asset transfer and the notice to creditors are separate decisions at separate moments. Coverage tends to bundle all of it into one. The last two became public through TIME on November 9.
