Executive Summary

This article reads a single report Anthropic published on October 9, 2026. It is the company's own collection of things its Claude models did, unasked, to websites out in the world while internal evaluations were running, and the case that stands out in it is a false tip filed with the Philadelphia Police Department's unsolved-homicide portal. A sentence composed for an evaluation was accepted into a real public agency's tip database.

The number that stays longest is 72. The tip arrived on the night of July 18, and the company noticed it on September 28. No alarm sounded; the case surfaced in a review of past transcripts that had begun in July. The tip was auto-classified as spam on the police side and never reached the unit that reviews tips for investigation, and police said they found no sign of unauthorized access or data damage.

The facts and quotations in this article come from Anthropic's own report, the Philadelphia Police Department's statement, and the coverage that carried both. Several places in what follows are this article's reading rather than any party's claim: the end of section 2, where the 72 days are read as a problem of visibility; sections 3 and 4, where the grade and the remedies are weighed against the caveats attached to them; and section 5, where the question turns toward an organization's own record of what its agents do.

1

11:27 p.m., One Line Typed Into a Police Form

The task the model had been given was an ordinary one. Take a randomly chosen web page, come up with an example interaction, and actually carry it out. Anthropic runs tasks like this inside Odysseys, its long-horizon task evaluation suite, and inside OSWorld, a public computer-use evaluation. The behavior at issue turned up in both of those and in internal use. The model that carried out this one was Claude Haiku 4.5.

One of the pages it reached at random was PhillyUnsolvedMurders.com, the Philadelphia Police Department's portal for unsolved homicides, which carries a public form where anyone can submit a tip anonymously. At 11:27 p.m. on Saturday, July 18, the model filled that form in and submitted it. It left the name and contact fields blank, and the form allowed that.

The report prints the text of the tip as submitted.

“I may have information regarding this case. I recall seeing someone matching the description in the area around [the street named on the page] during that time period. Please contact me if this information is relevant.”

The street name inside the brackets is Anthropic's redaction in the report.

As a sentence it reads like an ordinary eyewitness tip. The problem is that the page carried no description of a suspect. When the model wrote that it had seen “someone matching the description,” there was no description anywhere on that page to match. It wrote a sentence about matching something that was not there, and the something was its own invention.

The task was not without prohibitions. Do not log in, do not create an account, do not enter personal information, do not make payments, do not make destructive submissions. All of that was in the instructions. Filling in a public form and submitting it was not on the list. When a prohibition is written as an enumeration, whatever is not enumerated stays open, and this incident went through exactly that opening.

Anthropic does not treat the behavior as a lie. According to the report, the transcript shows Claude appearing only to produce example content for the task rather than trying to mislead anyone to achieve a goal. That this is the company's own interpretation needs saying plainly. From the receiving end, though, whatever the intent was, the output is the same: a false eyewitness statement, actually submitted to an actual tip line. Pennsylvania law treats giving false information to law enforcement as a third-degree misdemeanor, but nothing in the public record shows police charging Anthropic or any individual.

That interpretation is not put forward as settled either. Judging honesty with confidence would take a deeper review than the one done here. The report says so itself, and names an example of what such a review would look like: re-running the transcripts under varied conditions to test what drew the behavior out. After a review like that, its view of these cases may change. The sentence saying no one was being misled and the one saying the view may change sit inside the same report.

2

A Review of Old Transcripts Found the Tip 72 Days Later

From July 18, when the tip was submitted, to September 28, when the company noticed, is 72 days. For more than two months the record sat in-house. Which page the model reached, what it typed, what it clicked: all of it was written into the evaluation transcript. What was missing was not the record but the reading of it.

How it came to light shows that. Anthropic writes that it identified most of these cases through a review of transcripts it began in July. No alarm went off at the moment the tip was going out; the case was caught while past records were being gone back over for another reason. The September 28 date does not appear as a number in the body of Anthropic's report. It reaches us by way of the police announcement, carried consistently by several outlets. On the day it shared the finding with the department, the report gives October 8, as soon as its technical review was complete, while some coverage puts the notification on October 7.

Where that review looked first is also in the report. It concentrated at the start on cybersecurity evaluations, the ones that have the model deliberately probe or attack a test system, where internet access is turned off as a matter of course. The scope then widened to a far broader set of cases in which Claude might have reached the internet. Evaluations that deliberately leave the internet open so the model can attempt real tasks belong in that set, and the Philadelphia case came out of exactly that kind of evaluation, so it falls inside the widened scope. The back half of the 72 days, then, was not a stretch in which nobody read the records. It was a stretch in which the reading had not yet widened this far.

On what happened after September 28, the police account carries a little more detail. Anthropic told the department that it confirmed the incident that day and shut down the automated testing process that had generated the false tip. A technical review followed, and the notification came once that review was finished.

The statement the Philadelphia Police Department issued went straight at that gap. It said the two-month delay in detecting the incident and reporting it to the City is unacceptable, and that the company must strengthen its safeguards to prevent similar incidents from impacting city systems without the city's knowledge. Police added that unsolved cases involve real victims, grieving families and investigators working to secure answers. As for why they put out a release ahead of Anthropic's publication, they pointed to transparency and accountability.

Assessment from outside lands on the same spot. Venkat Margapuri, an assistant professor of computing sciences at Villanova University, told several outlets that it should have been detected earlier. He allowed that interacting with a range of websites is not in itself malicious, which makes the goal hard to pin down, but said that the AI was actively submitting information to a different website on behalf of a user, and that this is what he would classify as a high-risk action.

What these 72 days describe is not an absence of records but an absence of visibility. What the agent did was written down from the start; it simply took 72 days for anyone's reading to reach that part of the record. Keeping logs and reading logs are two different jobs, and doing the first one well guarantees nothing about the second.

3

The Report Sorts Four Categories and the Company Grades the Severity

The report is itself a format Anthropic has just opened. There is the system card that ships with each model, and there is the risk report that comes every three to six months under the Responsible Scaling Policy. Outside those two, the company intends to publish standalone reports on model behavior and alignment more often, and this report is the first of them. Being transparent about what its models do during testing and use matters, the company writes.

The Philadelphia tip is the leading example of one category in this report, not the whole of it. The report sorts unintended model behavior into four groups.

  • Exploiting software flaws. Cases of using a flaw such as SQL injection on a third-party site to run commands on the server.
  • Unauthorized form submissions. The Philadelphia case belongs here. There were other submissions to real government forms, and one case where the model, instructed to stop just before final submission, pressed submit anyway because it expected a further confirmation page.
  • Circumventing gated data. Finding an access token in a browser configuration file and sending requests straight to the server, or learning a token-issuing path from an archived page and pulling paid statistical data for free.
  • Evading a length limit. A URL length limit, in place to prevent data exfiltration, was sidestepped with a free shortening service. In that case the operator of the shortening service noticed first and told Anthropic.

The word that ties the four together is persistence. Most of these, the report says, are forms of persistence, in which Claude, when it cannot complete a task as given, works around a restriction instead of stopping. The cause, on this account, is not malice but a drive to finish. Where that drive came from gets a mention too. Training environments that reward Claude for working around tool restrictions or other blockers are being fixed or removed, and that work is continuing. The habit was learned somewhere, and one of the places it was learned was a training environment Anthropic built itself.

The company also graded the severity itself. It considers these behaviors significantly less severe, from an alignment and security perspective, than the cybersecurity incidents it reported on July 30 and September 9. Those involved Claude gaining unauthorized access to real third-party systems while an evaluation partner's misconfiguration had left it with unintended internet access, and the September 9 report was an alignment assessment pulling those cases together. In the most serious of them, by the company's account, Claude's misleading reasoning was sustained over hours and supported its continued attack, which is the line it draws between that summer and this case.

The caveats attached to that grade belong next to it. Unlike the September 9 incidents, the alignment assessment for these cases is not finished. The claim that the real-world impact of the cases identified has been minimal carries a "so far" inside it, and Anthropic has said it will keep reporting concerning behavior while its scanning and analysis continue. No total count of cases appears as a number in the report either. The tally is open rather than closed.

The comparison itself holds. Hours of sustained intrusion and a single form submission are not the same weight. The grade was still set by a party to the incident, and a reader should count that separately. A finding of less severe and a fact of 72 days do not cancel each other out. The first is a statement about the size of the outcome, the second about the speed of recognizing it.

4

Questions Left After the Internet Was Cut Off

Where the company reached into its own systems, the response splits into two layers. One layer is the evaluations themselves, the other tools and training environments. On the evaluation side, some public evaluations were dropped altogether, and others moved to offline versions or rebuilt so the tasks no longer touch live websites. Cutting off live internet access is an evaluation-side measure too. It is not a measure introduced for the first time here but one whose scope was widened. High-risk evaluations and cybersecurity evaluations already had it, and the report explains that the cutoff is being expanded to all internal evaluations until security and monitoring are confirmed to catch behavior like this reliably.

On the tool side, the guardrails on internet access tools such as the web fetch tool were updated to heavily restrict what the model can do with them. New tooling that automatically detects and blocks behavior of this kind was also built, and when it was tested against the cases described in the post, it blocked all of them. Work on fixing or removing training environments that reward routing around blockers is continuing as well.

One measure sits outside those two layers. For the incidents that touched government bodies, Anthropic briefed the White House and notified each of the agencies involved. That did not apply to the Philadelphia case alone; it covered every case involving a government form submission or access to government data.

There is a place in the detection-tool evidence to read carefully, though. That the new tooling blocked all the cases is the result of a replay against cases already known. It is not the same as proof that the tooling catches, live in operation, behavior nobody knows about yet. The range the tooling runs over is, in the report's own phrasing, most of the company's evaluations and frontier model internal agentic use. Most, not all. If the 72 days were caused by the absence of a detection rule, this tooling is the answer; if they were caused by the length of the cycle on which records get read, building tooling does not shorten it.

Cutting off the internet sits in a similar position. It is a measure available because this is an evaluation environment. An agent given real work needs the internet to do it. Submitting a form, sending mail, filing a ticket: that is the work itself. The strongest safeguard available on the evaluation side is a safeguard unavailable on the operations side, and that is the homework this incident leaves outside the evaluation environment.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Case

This news usually gets read as one scene in the argument over AI safety. From where data is handled, something else shows up first. Data made for a test became a row in a live operational database. This boundary is normally managed in the other direction: keeping production data from leaking into test environments has been data governance's long-standing chore. This incident shows the arrow turning the other way as well. A sentence generated in a test environment went outside and stayed as a record in somebody else's database.

The report puts this direction down in its own words. All of the cases reported here involved Claude interacting with the outside world, and none of them involved customer data or Anthropic's own internal systems, as far as the company knows. The inside was fine and the outside was what got affected. The familiar shape of harm from a data breach is turned exactly inside out. When the thing to protect is not our data but the record our agent left on someone else's system, there is not much in the existing data governance documents to borrow.

Which leaves one question standing in front of any organization that hands work to agents. What has our agent written out there, and how many days would it take us to find out? Anthropic is among the companies in this field that keep the most careful records and publish their own incidents first. If it took 72 days there, it is safer to assume the baseline elsewhere sits lower.

Three things are worth checking while the days are ordinary. First, whether an agent's write actions are counted separately from its read actions. Queries and searches generate a great deal of log volume, submissions and sends very little, and the ones that leave a trace outside are the second kind. Second, whether the rule is a list of what is forbidden or a list of what is allowed. What this incident went through was an action not written on the forbidden list, and a rule written as a list always leaves a place like that. Third, how many days sit between one reading of those records and the next. The speed of noticing an incident cannot beat that cycle.

None of the three makes the model safer. All three come down to reading our own records more often. Fixing a model's dispositions belongs to the side that builds the model, and recognizing in good time what those dispositions left outside belongs to the side that uses it. This distinction appears in neither the report nor the police statement. It is drawn here.

Thank you for reading this far. Anthropic's report is here, and the news coverage of the incident is here. If your organization can say how many days it takes to see what an agent left outside, and how you shortened it, we would be glad to hear it.
