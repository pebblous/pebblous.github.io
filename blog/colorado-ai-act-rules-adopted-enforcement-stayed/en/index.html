Executive Summary

The rules for an AI law are written out in full, and the state that wrote them cannot enforce it. That inability is seven months old in Colorado. On November 19 the Colorado Department of Law adopted 76 pages of rules implementing the Automated Decision-Making Technology in Consequential Decisions Act (SB 26-189) and the Chatbot Safety Act (HB 26-1263). The document puts calendar dates on when a company has to disclose an adverse decision, have a person look at it again, and keep the record.

A company can ignore all of it and the state attorney general still cannot open an investigation. An order entered by a federal magistrate judge on April 27 bars him from initiating enforcement, "including but not limited to the initiation of an investigation." What lifts the bar is a ruling on a preliminary injunction motion that xAI has yet to file, and as of November 16 nothing had been filed. The side holding the key to the freeze is not using it.

Sections 1 through 4 set out what the rules and the case record say. Section 5, which asks how a company should read the period after obligations begin on January 1, 2027, is this article's own reading.

The Rules Put Dates on What Companies Owe

The document the Colorado Department of Law adopted on November 19 is 4 CCR 904-6, the Automated Decision-Making Technology & Conversational Artificial Intelligence Service Rules. Seventy-six pages hold Rules 1 through 15, and two statutes sit underneath them. SB 26-189, the Automated Decision-Making Technology in Consequential Decisions Act, was signed on May 14, and HB 26-1263, the Conversational Artificial Intelligence Services Act, was signed on May 29. The rules call the second one the Chatbot Safety Act. Both statutes live in sections 6-1-1701 through 6-1-1710 of the Colorado Revised Statutes, and both take effect on January 1, 2027.

When the rules themselves take effect is written differently. The fixed date of January 1, 2027 that sat in the draft is gone from the adopted version, and in its place the rules take effect on the date the Secretary of State publishes, becoming final twenty days after publication. No publication date has been set. That single line comes back as a problem in section 4.

On the automated decision side, most of what a company owes is written as a deadline. A decision that goes against someone has to be disclosed in plain language within 30 days, and a request from that person for more information has to be answered within 15. A request for human review has to be acknowledged within 10 days and completed within 45. One extension of up to 45 days is available, except where the decision touches food, shelter or required medical care. Requests to see or correct personal data also run 45 days, and records of human review have to be kept for three years. Printed notices sent by mail cannot be set in type smaller than 12 points.

What goes into a notice is set out in the rules that follow. The first 30-day disclosure has to carry the decision itself, the role the system played in reaching it, how to ask for more information, and the right to access, correct and request human review. A company answering a request for more information within 15 days has to name the system, its version and its developer, and then give the types, categories and sources of the personal data used in the decision. Sources have to be identified by name where the deployer knows them. The example the rules reach for is the data broker. A company that weighed a broker's data has to say which broker.

The chatbot side sets intervals for disclosure and methods for checking age. A minor has to be told the service is AI at the start of every interaction and at least once every three hours. An adult gets told at the first interaction of each calendar day, after which the disclosure either stays on screen or repeats at three-hour intervals. Sixty consecutive minutes without a response from the user ends the interaction. Age cannot be established by a government ID alone, nor by a self-declared age, nor by a payment method a minor could also hold.

Next to the list of prohibitions sits a list of five methods that do count. One is a system conforming to ISO/IEC 27566-1:2025. Another is a cryptographic method such as a zero-knowledge proof, which establishes whether the user is a minor without revealing anything else. A grade attached to a school-provisioned account also counts. The annual report clause is the one to watch, though. The first report is due on or before July 1, 2027, and operators are sorted into five tiers by monthly active users, from above 10 million down to under 500,000. An operator has to report how often it estimated a minor to be an adult, broken out for users under 13, 13 to 15, and 16 to 17. That goes past asking a company to check age and asks it to publish how often the check is wrong.

2

An April Order Bars Even an Investigation

While the rules were being written, something else was happening in court. xAI sued Colorado Attorney General Phil Weiser on April 9, in the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado, Civil Action No. 1:26-cv-01515-DDD-CYC. The complaint rested on the First Amendment, with dormant Commerce Clause, due process and equal protection claims alongside it. The statute it aimed at was not today's SB 26-189 but its predecessor, SB 24-205.

Norton Rose Fulbright's account of the complaint lays out the argument. Designing a model is itself expression, and the statute would force xAI to redesign its own model, Grok, around Colorado's idea of fairness. Against the duty to disclose bias mitigation work, the complaint claims a right not to speak. On due process it argues that the words making up "algorithmic discrimination" go undefined and invite arbitrary enforcement, and it calls the carve-out that exempts outputs meant to increase diversity a violation of equal protection.

On April 24 the U.S. Department of Justice intervened on equal protection grounds, after the acting attorney general certified the case as one of general public interest. Three days later, on April 27, Magistrate Judge Cyrus Y. Chung, acting on referral from Chief Judge Daniel D. Domenico, entered a minute order (ECF No. 24). The order states that the defendant "shall not initiate enforcement, including but not limited to the initiation of an investigation." The same order vacated the scheduling conference set for June 16 and held that "all case management and other deadlines are STAYED."

The clause setting the boundary of the bar points at a span of conduct rather than a date on the calendar. Enforcement is barred for violations "that occurred or may occur on or before 14 days after the date the Court issues a ruling." The ruling in question is the court's ruling on the preliminary injunction motion xAI is expected to file.

Laid out on one line, the stretch from the filing in April through the start of obligations in January 2027, and the open-ended span beyond it, looks like this.

The Freeze Is Not Just xAI's

An order protecting only the party that sued would be a smaller story. What the court granted, though, was not the joint motion as first submitted but the revised version filed on April 24 (ECF No. 22), and no company is named in it. The original motion (ECF No. 18) was denied as moot. What the operative sentence governs is not a company either, but a single act by the state attorney general. PPC Land reads the stay, for that reason, as reaching every company covered by the law rather than the plaintiff alone.

Stopping one official stops enforcement outright because of how this law is enforced. The Colorado AI Act carries no private right of action, and enforcement authority rests exclusively with the attorney general under section 6-1-1706(1) of the Colorado Revised Statutes. The revised joint motion the court approved cites that same provision, noting that the attorney general "has exclusive authority" to enforce the law. Where one person is the only one who can enforce, barring that person from starting leaves no route through. The same exclusive authority, as it happens, is what xAI pointed to in its due process claim about arbitrary enforcement.

The second blank around scope is how far the bar reaches across statutes. The order speaks of violations of SB 24-205 "or any legislation replacing or amending SB24-205 enacted during this legislative session." SB 26-189 is not named, but it repealed the earlier statute and took its place, so the November 22 report treats it as likely covered. The Chatbot Safety Act stands differently. It neither replaced nor amended SB 24-205, and the order does not mention it. Whether the stay reaches the chatbot provisions is a question the order does not address.

The single volume of rules adopted on November 19 supports both statutes. The order of April 27 attaches by name to only one of them. Obligations written in the same document may therefore sit under different enforcement conditions. No ruling resolving that mismatch has come from the court.

4

The 14-Day Clock Has Not Started Counting

The route out of the stay is written into the order. xAI is to file a preliminary injunction motion, and an amended complaint if necessary, "within 28 days after final adoption of rulemaking implementing SB24-205 or any legislation that may replace or amend SB24-205." Once the court rules on that motion, 14 more days bring the barred span to its end. The whole procedure hangs on one filing by the plaintiff.

When those 28 days begin is unclear. The order never defines "final adoption." Treating November 19 as the date puts the deadline at December 17, which is PPC Land's calculation. The Department of Law, however, wrote only that it had adopted the rules on November 19 and placed finality twenty days after publication. With no publication date set, the deadline has no date either.

Recent activity on the docket has nothing to do with the injunction. ECF No. 25 of October 2 and ECF No. 28 of November 16 are both motions to withdraw as counsel, and the latter is the most recent entry. Neither a preliminary injunction motion nor an amended complaint had arrived by November 16. On the rules side, the most recent step is the Department of Law's request on November 20 for a formal opinion from the attorney general.

Read against the text of the order, this delay does not end as a simple postponement. The bar runs not to a date but over violations "that occurred or may occur on or before" the day it names. If the filing slips far enough that the ruling lands in 2027, conduct after obligations begin on January 1 falls inside the same span. One available reading is that violations within that span never become investigable, even later. No court has confirmed that reading, and this far is the result of reading the order.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Story

A regulation existing and a regulation working are two different facts. Colorado shows the two coming apart, with numbers attached. The document companies would consult is finished at 76 pages and the calendar reads January 1, 2027, while the office that would hold anyone to that calendar cannot so much as open an investigation.

Reading this as a window in which preparation can wait misses something. A good deal of what the rules ask for cannot be produced later. Response deadlines of 30 or 45 days cannot be met by building the machinery after the request arrives, and the duty to retain human review for three years only holds if a record existed at the moment the review happened. Which data entered which decision, and who looked at it again, do not get reconstructed afterwards. A company that leaves no records during the frozen span will not be able to account for that span once enforcement returns.

Korea has a similar question sitting in a different place. The AI Framework Act took effect on January 22, 2026. The two notices that would spell out how operators of high-impact AI are to meet their duties and their safety obligations had not been issued as of October 14. That fact comes from the Federation of Korean Industries, which put 19 proposed improvements to the Ministry of Science and ICT on October 29, with the difficulty of knowing what to prepare given as the reason. Whether the two notices have since been issued is something this article could not confirm. In Colorado the method of compliance was completed first, at 76 pages, and enforcement stalled behind it; in Korea the statute stood first and the layer describing the method was empty. The blanks sit in different places, and the question companies face is the same one. Is this law working right now?

So a few things are worth leaving behind inside a company while a span like this runs.

  • The source behind a decision. The rules require a deployer answering a request for more information to name data sources where it knows them. Unless which data entered which automated decision is written down at the moment of the decision, nobody can produce those names within 15 days.
  • The trace of a review. Who looked again, when, and what they changed has to survive for the three-year retention duty to mean anything.
  • The clock on deadlines. A machine has to be counting the day a request arrived and the day it was answered before 15 days and 45 days become numbers anyone can operate.

None of the three can be built after enforcement returns. Where data came from and which decision it became has to be written down in the moment. That is the place Pebblous points at when it talks about AI-Ready Data.

The length of the rules, the deadlines by provision, the contents of a notice, the age-assurance methods, the adoption date and the signing dates of the two statutes come from PPC Land, writing on November 19, 2026. The case number, ECF numbers, the quoted language of the order and the state of the docket through November 16 follow the same outlet on November 22; this article did not open the court records directly and relied on that reporting.
