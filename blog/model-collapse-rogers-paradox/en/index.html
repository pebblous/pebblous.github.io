Executive Summary

AI models that keep training on text written by earlier models lose rare knowledge first. A paper posted to arXiv in November explains this effect, known as model collapse, with two theories from cultural evolution. Its sole author is Nam H. Le of the Department of Computer Science at the University of Vermont, and its title is "Who Pays for the Knowledge Models Copy?" Le argues that copying wears knowledge down even when the world stands still, and that when the world changes, even perfectly copied knowledge goes stale. The experiment code and per-seed result files are public on GitHub under the MIT license.

In the experiments where the world stays fixed, a neutral drift model of word frequencies predicted how many rare words survive without a single fitted parameter, and its error never exceeded 0.028 in any generation. In the experiments where the world changes, the home cities of 2,000 fictional people were reshuffled every generation. Faster change brought lower accuracy and more stale answers, and models that inherited the previous generation's weights lost accuracy four times as fast as models that restarted. The largest model has 125 million parameters, though, and no experiment adds an equal amount of real data as a comparison.

The paper ends on one closed-form result. Once people can ask a model instead of finding things out themselves, the model's accuracy at equilibrium settles at one minus the cost of finding out. Even if the world never changes, someone has to keep paying that cost. Sections 1 through 7 report what we checked in the paper and the public code. Section 8 is Pebblous's reading from the side of those who work with synthetic data.

1

University of Vermont Paper Reads Model Collapse Through Henrich and Rogers

Model collapse became widely known in 2024, when Ilia Shumailov and colleagues reported it in Nature. When a generative model is trained over and over on the output of the model before it, the tails of the distribution, meaning the things that appear rarely, disappear first. Le's paper looks at the same effect through two models from cultural evolution, the field that studies what happens when human groups pass knowledge from one generation to the next.

The first is the skill-loss model that anthropologist Joseph Henrich proposed in 2004. As the paper summarizes it in Section 2, a group learns a skill by watching the most skilled member of the previous generation. Copies fall short of the original on average and only occasionally surpass it. So in a small group, complex skills can vanish even when the environment does not change. Henrich applied the model to Aboriginal Tasmanians, who lost several toolmaking skills after they were isolated. Le maps the first cause of model collapse onto this picture: copying is itself lossy, even in a world that stays the same.

The second is the model anthropologist Alan Rogers published in 1988. In an environment where the best behavior changes with probability u each generation, individual learners pay a cost c to find out what works now. Social learners copy someone else and pay nothing, but they are right only if the person they copied was right and the environment has not changed in the meantime. Copying spreads through the population, yet once it has spread, the population as a whole is no better off than one made up entirely of individual learners. In the paper's words, "copying has been adopted without making anyone better off." That is Rogers' paradox.

Le ties the two models into a single chain. The abstract puts it this way.

"In a fixed world, copies are lossy, as in Henrich's account of skill loss in small populations. In a changing world, even perfect copies go stale, which is Rogers' paradox."

The chain has three dials. One is the share of newly verified human data in the training set. Another is copy fidelity σ, how much of the content survives each copy. The third is u, how fast the world changes.

Section 4 of the paper writes the chain as one equation. Take a single word or a single fact as the unit of knowledge, and let $q_t$ be the fraction of the lineage that holds it. The next generation can get the unit in two ways. Fresh human data can supply it directly, with probability φ. Or the synthetic part can pass it on with probability σ, provided the fact has not changed in between (probability 1−u). φ rises with the fresh-data share but is not equal to it, because a rare unit can be missing from the fresh data too.

$$q_{t+1} = \phi + (1-\phi)\,\sigma\,(1-u)\,q_t, \qquad q^* = \frac{\phi}{1-(1-\phi)\,\sigma\,(1-u)}$$

Both mechanisms sit inside this equation. With u=0 and σ<1, nothing changes and yet $q^*$ stays below 1; only fresh supply (φ>0) prevents total loss. That is Henrich's case. With σ=1 and u>0, the value drops even though copying is perfect, and the lost units are not missing but wrong. The author tracks the fraction holding values that were once right but are now wrong with a separate equation, and those are the stale answers counted in Section 4. That is Rogers' case.

Four Lineage Experiments, From Darwin and Melville Trigrams to OPT-125m

The paper tests the same chain on four different kinds of lineage. A lineage is a sequence in which the output of one generation's model becomes the training data for the next. The repository rogers-model-collapse has a separate script for each experiment.

The table below sorts the four lineages by the kind of world and the setup. The first three test copying loss in a world that stays fixed, and the last tests staleness in a world that changes. The models grow from simple word statistics up to a pretrained language model.

Lineage World Setup

OPT-125mFixedSame setup as Shumailov and colleagues. Fine-tuned on its own output from WikiText-2
OPT-125mChanging2,000 fictional people and 50 cities. Each person's home moves with probability u per generation

▲ Source: arXiv 2611.06022, Sections 5–6 and Appendix A

The trigram experiments measure rare-word survival. Leave out the 1,000 most frequent words in the original book; rare-word survival is the share of the remaining words still present in a generation's training data, scaled so that generation 0 equals 1. Seventy percent of each book's sentences served as the reservoir from which fresh human data was drawn, and 10% were held out for testing.

The changing-world experiment is built on invented facts. Two thousand people with random, pronounceable names each live in one of 50 cities. Because they do not exist, the pretrained OPT-125m knows nothing about them. Human-written text states each person's current home using 16 templates such as "X lives in Y." How often each person is mentioned follows a Zipf distribution, so a few appear hundreds of times and most appear about once. The model is given four prompts, including "X lives in," and the city it ranks most likely as the next word counts as correct if it is the current home and stale if it is a former home.

Three lineages with different training regimes went into this experiment. The restart lineage fine-tunes from the original pretrained weights every generation. The inherit lineage continues training from the weights the previous generation left behind. In both, 30% of the training data is newly written human text and the rest is the previous model's output. The third, the all-fresh lineage, is a baseline trained only on current human-written text.

3

Neutral Drift Model Predicts Rare-Word Survival to Within 0.028 With No Free Parameters

In the fixed world, the author's prediction tool is a neutral drift model of word frequencies. It assumes that how often each word appears in the next generation is drawn at random in proportion to its frequency in the current one. A word that appeared only once has a high chance of not being drawn at all next time, so rare words drop out first. Since the world does not change, the change rate is fixed at 0, and the training-set size and fresh-data share are values the experimenter sets. Not one parameter is tuned to the data.

The model matched rare-word survival in the trigram lineages for both books and every condition. According to Section 5, the maximum error at generation 12 is 0.007, and across all generations it is 0.028. The abstract rounds this to "within 0.03." In the same section the author reports that mixing in any fresh data at all stops the decline ("Any fresh fraction halts the decline"). In the condition that keeps all earlier generations' data, rare-word survival at generation 12 was 1.00 for both books.

The decline being predicted was not small either. Trigram models trained only on their own output lost four out of five rare words over 12 generations, and their perplexity on human text grew 7.7-fold on the Darwin book and 4.1-fold on the Melville book. In the character-level transformer, rare-word survival fell to 4% of its starting value. Mixing 30% fresh data into the same model stopped survival at 0.41 by generation 6.

The author revised the chain in v2. The one-parameter chain in v1 was off by as much as 0.17 during the stretch where the decline was under way. Its plateau values only looked right because two errors cancelled out, the author writes. v1 assumed that 10%, 30%, and 50% fresh data would supply rare words at 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50, but on the Darwin book the actual supply was 0.15, 0.40, and 0.60. That is why the equation in Section 1 keeps φ separate from the fresh-data share.

OPT-125m showed the same trend. The lineage trained repeatedly on its own output saw its perplexity on human-written text double from 47 to 102, and kept only 0.59 of its rare words. Perplexity measures how unfamiliar human text looks to the model; higher is worse.

The table below shows the conditions that mixed in fresh human data. With just 10%, perplexity stopped at 68 instead of climbing to 102, and higher shares brought the plateau closer to the starting value of 47. At generation 10, the gap in rare-word survival between the 30% fresh lineage and the own-output-only lineage was 0.23.

On OPT-125m the neutral drift prediction did not fit as well as on the trigrams. At the 30% and 50% points the errors were within 0.01 and 0.04, but elsewhere the model missed in both directions. On the first copy the lineage lost more than predicted (0.78 predicted, 0.75 actual), while the own-output-only lineage kept far more than predicted by generation 10 (0.33 predicted, 0.59 actual). The author concludes that something outside the training data is putting rare words back, and names the pretrained weights, which every generation starts from, as the likely source. In Henrich's terms, that is a source of innovation that never passes through the copying chain. The hypothesis leads into the restart-versus-inherit comparison in Section 4.

For the changing world, the chain fitted under fixed-world conditions was applied unchanged to conditions with a change rate u above 0. For mid-frequency facts, the mean absolute error was 0.023 for accuracy and 0.015 for the stale-answer rate; for rare facts it was 0.009 and 0.025. Frequent facts barely lost accuracy when the world was fixed, so the chain could not be fitted to them, the author writes.

Section 8 sorts synthetic-data filters into the same framework. The author classes filters that keep the common, such as likelihood or perplexity filters, as a conformist learning strategy. Rare content is by definition less like other text, so such filters cut diversity, the argument goes. In the experiments, a filter that kept a random half was no different from no filter at all. The likelihood-based filter sped up rare-word loss, and on the transformer it did worse than random selection. A filter that favored rare words kept more of them, but perplexity got worse faster.

4.1What Moved: Change Rate and Weight Inheritance

In the two lineages with 30% fresh data, accuracy fell and stale answers rose as the change rate went up. At u=0.4, the restart lineage named a former city for 8% of people and the inherit lineage did so for 21%. The accuracy slope per unit change rate is −0.87 for inherit and −0.21 for restart. The author writes that the inheriting lineage loses accuracy four times as fast.

The same inheritance helped when the world held still. At u=0, the inherit lineage reached 0.65 accuracy, more than three times the restart lineage's 0.19 and higher even than the 0.55 of the all-fresh lineage trained only on human text. The abstract sums it up: "Models that inherit their weights keep more in a static world" and carry more outdated facts in a changing one.

4.2What Did Not Move: The All-Fresh Lineage and World Swaps

The all-fresh lineage, trained only on current human-written text, held accuracy at 0.52 to 0.55 almost regardless of the change rate. In chain terms its fresh-data share is 100%, so no stale facts carry over from the previous generation.

Section 6.3 measures excess negative log-likelihood (NLL) on human text, using arXiv abstracts published after the pretraining cutoff as the test set. In late generations the restart lineage settled at 0.48 nats on rare words and 0.21 nats on common words. The inherit lineage climbed to 0.83 nats, 0.35 nats above restart. By contrast, swapping out the entire world every generation added only 0.04 nats of excess for restart and −0.09 nats for inherit. On this metric, whether a model inherits weights made a far larger difference than whether the world changes.

5

Le's Paper Has No Comparison That Adds an Equal Amount of Real Data

Le's paper has no experiment that adds an equal amount of real data on top of the synthetic data as a comparison. The design fixes the size of the training set each generation and varies the share of fresh human data within it from 0% to 100%. Raising the fresh-data share shrinks the synthetic portion by the same amount inside that fixed size. No experiment stacks real data on top; there are only experiments that replace synthetic data with real data.

Two conditions come closest. One is the all-fresh lineage in the changing world, where the same-size training set is filled entirely with human text. The other is the accumulate condition in the trigram experiments, which keeps every earlier generation's data so the training set grows each generation. Rare-word survival stayed at 1.00 under that condition, but because the original human text never leaves the training set, it cannot be read as a comparison that adds real data separately. What the paper establishes is a monotonic relationship between the fresh-data share and the size of the decline, and no more.

6

The Rogers Chain in Closed Form: Model Accuracy Settles at 1−c

Section 7 of the paper stops treating the fresh-data share in the experiments as a value handed in from outside and makes it the result of people's choices. People either look a fact up themselves (cost c) or ask the model (no cost). Let h be the share of people who look things up and s the share of model-written text in the training data; the fresh-data share entering training is then $f = (1-s)h$. People who ask the model are right as often as the model's accuracy a, and people who look things up get 1−c.

Finding the equilibrium is straightforward. At the fixed point of the Section 1 chain, model accuracy is set by the fresh-data share f, copy fidelity σ, and change rate u. As long as people switch strategies when one pays better, an interior equilibrium requires askers and lookers to come out even, so a* = 1−c. Setting the fixed-point accuracy to 1−c and solving for f gives the formula in Proposition 1.

$$a^* = \frac{(1-u)\,f}{1-(1-u)(1-f)\,\sigma}$$

Proposition 1 gives the following equilibrium.

$$a^* = 1 - c, \qquad f^* = \frac{(1-c)\,\bigl(1-(1-u)\sigma\bigr)}{(1-u)\,\bigl(1-(1-c)\sigma\bigr)}, \qquad h^* = \frac{f^*}{1-s}$$

The first equation is the paper's conclusion. At equilibrium, the model's accuracy equals what people who look things up get. If the model is more accurate than that, more people ask it, fewer look things up, fresh data dries up, and accuracy comes down. If the model is less accurate, the movement runs the other way. The abstract says "the model ends up exactly as reliable as finding out is costly." The equilibrium is interior only when s is smaller than 1−f*.

With perfect copying (σ=1), the formula reduces to $f^* = \dfrac{u(1-c)}{c(1-u)}$, so if the world does not change (u=0), no fresh data is needed. That is Rogers' original paradox. With lossy copying (σ<1), things are different. Setting u=0 leaves the following value.

$$f^*\big|_{u=0} = \frac{(1-c)(1-\sigma)}{1-(1-c)\sigma} > 0$$

The paper's Corollary 1 concludes: "Someone must keep paying to learn even when nothing changes." Plugging in numbers shows the scale. With a lookup cost c of 0.2 and copy fidelity σ of 0.9, f* is 0.08÷0.28, or about 0.29. Even in an unchanging world, about 29% of the training data has to be directly verified human data to hold the equilibrium. (This is a Pebblous worked example, not a value from the paper.)

Proposition 2 adds a third strategy, checking: people who take the model's answer and pay $c_v$ to verify it. Checkers come out ahead of individual learners only when the checking cost is below $c(1-c)$. If $c_v \ge c(1-c)$, checkers have no advantage and the system returns to the Proposition 1 equilibrium. Bringing in verifiers changes the equilibrium only when verification is cheap enough.

Here the author criticizes the existing remedies. The ones that work, namely fresh human data, verifiers, and curators, all bring information in from outside the loop. Yet "the theory of model collapse treats that supply as given," the paper argues.

What We Confirmed in Le's Paper, What the Author Claims, and the Limits He States

What we confirmed in the paper text (v2) and the GitHub repository differs in kind from what the author puts forward as interpretation. The table keeps them apart.

Type Content
ConfirmedThe figures in Sections 2–4 (rare-word error 0.028, perplexity 47→102, Table 2, slopes −0.87 and −0.21, excess NLL), the chain equation (Eq. 1) and the formulas in Propositions 1 and 2, MIT-licensed code
Author's claimsThe framing that model collapse is explained by two mechanisms, Henrich's skill loss and Rogers' paradox; the classification of likelihood-based filters as a conformist learning strategy; the criticism that existing remedies treat the outside supply of information as given
Our interpretationSection 8, and the worked example with c=0.2, σ=0.9 in Section 6

The author lists his own limits in Section 9. The largest language model has 125 million parameters. Rare-word survival is measured on the training data, and a metric on the model itself was added only for OPT-125m. The fact world is kept simple: one attribute per person, uniform change, and fixed templates. Some experiments have only three or four seeds, which leaves rank-based tests with little power. The ecosystem model in Section 7 assumes facts are independent of one another, and its predictions were not tested with real users. The filter experiments cover only conformist filters; filters that select on performance and filters that correct against the world were not tested directly.

8

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Paper

Pebblous has long held that synthetic data should be aimed precisely where it is needed and that the results must be validated against real data. Le's paper puts a price on that validation and writes it as a formula. For teams running synthetic-data pipelines, the paper suggests three things to check.

  • • Who pays the cost of first-hand checking? Field labelers, domain experts, and the people who collect real measurements are the ones paying it. If their work is replaced by reviewing model output, h in Proposition 1 shrinks.
  • • Do your quality filters keep the common? Section 3 reports that filtering synthetic data by perplexity or likelihood drops rare cases first. The more a pipeline deals with defect data or minority classes, the more this applies.
  • • Do you keep training from the previous model's weights? In Section 4, inheritance helped when the world held still and left the most stale answers when it changed. If field conditions shift in your data, the stale-answer rate of continually trained models is worth measuring on its own.

Editor's Note

The closed form in Section 6 rests on the assumptions that facts are independent and that people choose between looking up and asking purely on cost. The author also notes that the ecosystem model was not tested with real users, and the largest model in the experiments has 125 million parameters. Still, the paper pulls the outside supply of information that every remedy relies on into the open as something to be calculated. Because the per-seed result files are in the repository, Table 2 and the figures can be reproduced without retraining.
