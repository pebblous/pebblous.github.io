법정에서 피해자가 자기를 쏜 사람을 용서한다고 말했다. 그는 그 자리에 오기 4년 전에 숨진 사람이었다.

애리조나 항소법원 제1부가 지난 9월 30일 그 양형을 파기했다. 크리스토퍼 펠키는 2021년 챈들러의 한 교차로에서 보복운전 끝에 총에 맞아 숨졌다. 유족은 양형 심리에서 그를 AI로 되살린 영상을 틀었다.

1심 판사는 그 영상을 보고 10년 6개월을 선고했다. 검찰이 구한 것보다 1년 긴 형이었다. 판사는 그 자리에서 그 AI를 사랑했다고 말했고, 화면 속 용서가 펠키의 사람됨을 보여 준다고 밝혔다. 과실치사 유죄는 그대로 남고 형량만 다시 정해진다.

영상에는 성격이 다른 두 겹이 들어 있었다. 아래쪽은 생전에 찍힌 영상과 사진이고, 위쪽은 여동생이 쓴 대본과 AI가 만든 목소리다. 펠키는 누구를 용서한다는 말을 한 적이 없다. 항소법원은 이 영상이 "피해자가 무슨 말을 했을지에 대한 가족의 믿음과, 피해자 자신의 목소리 및 견해 사이에 있던 해석의 거리를 지운다"고 적었다.

항소법원이 영상 전체를 내친 것은 아니다. 섞여 있던 생전의 실제 녹화분은 양형 심리에 들어와도 된다고 보았다. 법원이 그은 선은 매체가 아니라 출처에 있다. 유족은 이것이 AI로 만든 영상이라고 법정에서 설명했고, 판사도 그것을 AI라고 불렀다. 그 공개가 아무것도 막지 못했다.

파일에 "AI로 만들었다"를 붙이는 일과, 어느 문장이 기록인지 가르는 일은 다른 일이다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

A victim told an Arizona courtroom that he forgave the man who shot him. He had been dead for four years.

On September 30 the Arizona Court of Appeals, Division One, vacated the sentence that followed. Christopher Pelkey was shot and killed after a road rage encounter at an intersection in Chandler in 2021, and at the sentencing hearing his family played a video that brought him back with AI.

The trial judge watched it and imposed ten years and six months, a year longer than prosecutors had asked for. He said in open court that he loved that AI, and that the forgiveness on screen showed who Pelkey was. The manslaughter conviction stands, and only the sentence goes back to be decided again.

The video held two layers of different kinds. Underneath was footage and photographs taken while Pelkey was alive. On top was a script his sister wrote, read out in a voice the AI produced. Pelkey never said he forgave anyone. The video, the appeals court wrote, "erases the interpretive distance between the family's belief of what the victim would have said and the victim's own voice and opinions."

The court did not throw the whole video out. The real footage mixed into it may come back at resentencing. The line it drew runs along provenance rather than medium. The family had told the courtroom the video was made with AI and the judge called it AI, and that disclosure stopped nothing.

Labelling a file as AI-generated and marking which sentence is a record and which one was invented are two different jobs.

## Facebook (EN)

"In another life, we probably could've been friends."

In May of last year, in a courtroom in Arizona, a man who had been shot and killed said that on screen to the man who shot him.

Christopher Pelkey died in 2021, at an intersection in Chandler. The face and the voice on screen came from footage and photographs taken while he was alive. The sentences he spoke were written by his sister, Stacey Wales.

She had been gathering written statements from family and friends for the sentencing hearing, forty-nine of them. The writing does not seem to have filled the place where her brother had been.

Judge Todd Lang watched the video and said he loved that AI. He said the forgiveness on screen showed who Pelkey was, and he chose a term a year longer than prosecutors had asked for.

On September 30, the Arizona Court of Appeals vacated that sentence.

One line from the opinion stays with me.

The video "erases the interpretive distance between the family's belief of what the victim would have said and the victim's own voice and opinions."

Interpretive distance.

When a family stands in court and says "he would have put it this way," everyone in the room can see that it is a guess. The judge looks at that open space and adjusts how much the words weigh.

The video filled it in.

The part worth noticing is that the court did not bar the video outright. The real footage of Pelkey mixed into it can come back at resentencing. The line the court drew ran along provenance, not medium.

The video already carried an explanation that it was made with AI. The judge himself called it AI. And still, nothing inside the frame separated what had been recorded from what had been written for him.

Disclosing and distinguishing are not the same act.
