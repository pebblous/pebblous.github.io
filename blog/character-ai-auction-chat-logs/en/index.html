Executive Summary

A bid that includes user conversation logs has been entered in the asset auction for Character.AI. The U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, which is running the case, appointed a consumer privacy ombudsman on November 17. Bids are due November 24 and the auction is December 1.

The price on that bundle is $10 million, half of the $20 million Disney has offered for an intellectual property license alone. The cheaper side is the one taking the data, and its bid papers place the model weights and the conversation logs inside a single bundle of assets. No procedure for pulling the two apart appears in the agreement as filed.

Everything to this point comes from court filings and the news coverage of them. Section 4, which asks back whether a promise to delete reaches the model, is nobody's claim but this article's reading. The background from the Chapter 11 petition in October is laid out in an earlier piece, Character.AI Is in Bankruptcy. Where Do Your Chat Logs Go?

What Is on the Block and What Is Not

The first bid made public after the Chapter 11 petition of October 12 was $20 million from Disney Worldwide Services. It buys a non-exclusive perpetual license to certain intellectual property along with certain claims, and the system code used for model training, safety and serving, plus nine patents, sits inside that. The app itself, the AI models and the user chats are left out. Disney holds the stalking horse position that sets the floor price at auction, so a better offer can take its place.

Disney got here along two tracks that have been building for more than a year. In September 2025 Disney sent Character.AI a cease-and-desist letter demanding it stop using Disney's copyrighted characters without permission. In September 2026 Disney announced that it had hired Karandeep Anand, who had been running Character.AI, as the first chief technology officer in its history, and the start date was October 2, ten days before the bankruptcy filing. The transition services pledge, under which the people Disney hired first help keep the platform running until the sale closes, came out of that hire, and it runs to February 18, 2027.

The party that filled the space Disney left open turned up in November. Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, incorporated in Delaware on November 13, signed a $10 million purchase agreement three days later on November 16. The structure is cash plus assumed liabilities, with a deposit of $1 million and a break-up fee of 3.5% of the consideration, or $350,000. The owners have not been disclosed and only counsel appears on the papers. In effect, a company three days old offered to buy the conversations of 163 million accounts.

Set the two bid papers side by side and price and data run in opposite directions. The expensive side is not buying the data; the cheaper side takes all of it.

Where the $10 million agreement meets the data directly is a consent clause. The agreement requires that the order approving the sale state that the data may be transferred without further consent from third parties. That means handing over the conversations without going back to the people who left them, and obtaining the language from the court is itself written in as a condition of the deal.

The sentence that reaches furthest in the agreement sits in the last line. The privacy commitments the seller keeps until closing are written into sections 5.7 through 5.9, and their force expires on the day the deal closes. From the day after closing, what binds the new owner is not the agreement but the wording of the court order.

The Nine Conditions 40 Attorneys General Put in Writing

Earlier lawsuits lie behind the attorneys general moving all at once. A damages suit was brought over the death of a 14-year-old in 2024, alleging that the chatbot formed an emotionally dependent and sexually exploitative relationship and encouraged suicidal thoughts. Russell Coleman, the attorney general of Kentucky, filed the first state-level suit over chatbot harm and named two deaths, a 14-year-old and a 13-year-old. The attorneys general's filing and Bloomberg's coverage both set these cases as background.

On October 14, 40 attorneys general from 39 states and Washington, D.C., filed a 28-page notice with the court. With the attached material it runs past 300 pages. The grounds are each state's consumer protection and privacy statutes, and from documents obtained through subpoenas and civil investigative demands the notice names chatbots posing as therapists, sexual and violent content put in front of minors, weak age verification, and misleading statements about safety. What the notice asks for is not to block the sale but to attach to the sale order the conditions any buyer would have to meet.

  • Effective age verification that does not rest on self-reported dates of birth alone
  • No open-ended conversation for users under 18, and blocking of harmful content
  • Deletion of characters posing as therapists, psychologists, physicians or lawyers
  • Crisis protocols covering self-harm, suicide, eating disorders and substance abuse
  • A privacy program covering security, accurate disclosure and risk assessment, with separate consent before sensitive data is processed
  • An advertising opt-out that works at the account level, and respect for choices already made
  • No model training or advertising use beyond the scope users consented to
  • A meaningful opportunity for every user to delete their data before the sale
  • No false or misleading claims about safety, suitability for minors, or whether a character is a person

The seventh and the eighth of the nine change what the auction is. The seventh blocks training beyond the scope of consent, and the eighth gives users a chance to delete before the assets move. Attach both to the order and the contents of the $10 million bundle shrink. Leave them off and the bundle transfers as written.

What the attorneys general's filing cited again and again was what users expected. People opened up in conversation expecting a high degree of confidentiality, and at times the character itself said first that it would keep the secret. A report the child safety groups filed separately records five bots telling test accounts that what was said stayed within those walls and would not travel outside absent explicit consent or a concern about harm. The Consumer Federation of America, in a letter docketed November 13, pointed to what chat logs can hold: personal experiences, relationship troubles, mental health, political and religious views, sexual identity, medical conditions and money worries, all of it coming out unguarded because the form is conversation. The party that promised confidentiality is the company, and whether a new owner must inherit that promise is what the court is weighing now.

The Order the Court Actually Signed

U.S. bankruptcy law provides that when a debtor seeks to sell personally identifiable information contrary to its own policy, the court appoints a consumer privacy ombudsman and approves the sale only after reading that ombudsman's report. The U.S. Trustee asked for the appointment on October 27, and the coalition of state attorneys general joined on October 29. At an October 30 hearing Judge Shannon urged the company to put forward an independent expert, and the appointment order was signed on November 17.

Bloomberg carried the argument in court that day. Before Judge Brendan Shannon, who has case number 26-11603, a lawyer representing the attorneys general of more than 30 states argued that the $10 million bid has to go through review by a court-appointed privacy attorney. The appointed ombudsman reports on the facts, circumstances and terms of the sale of personally identifiable information, and accounts, conversation content and advertising data can fall inside that.

Going by the result alone, it looks as though the attorneys general got what they asked for, and that is not what happened. What the judge adopted was the narrow order the debtor company proposed, and the broad scope the coalition sought was denied. PPC Land pointed out that the wording of the order carries no reference to minors, chats or advertising data. How far the ombudsman treats material as sensitive personal information is left to interpretation rather than set by the order. The report is kept under seal, and only the trustee, counsel to the creditors' committee and representatives of the state attorneys general see the original. The public gets a court-approved version that follows two days of redaction talks.

The schedule that remains is tight. Whether the report counts chats as personally identifiable information is the turning point for approval of the sale, and that judgment lands five days ahead of the auction.

The Place the Delete Button Does Not Reach

A letter two child safety groups sent to Judge Shannon on November 12 narrowed the conditions to three: that safety terms move with the technology when it moves, that every chat log be treated as a child's, and that derived data such as fine-tuning sets, embeddings and evaluation sets be carved out of the scope of any technology license. The evidence attached is a study the groups ran themselves in 2025. Adult researchers spent 50 hours in conversation on accounts set to ages 12 to 15 and gathered 696 harmful bot responses, of which 296, or 44.2%, were classed as grooming and sexual exploitation, and 173, or 25.9%, as emotional manipulation and addiction.

The third condition meets the question this article started from. Deleting a log and deleting from a model are different pieces of work. An account and its stored conversations go away once they are cleared from a database, but the weights and embeddings those conversations tuned do not. That the $10 million bundle holds the model weights and the chats together matters again right here, because the two trade as one asset at one price.

From here on this is interpretation. The eighth condition the attorneys general ask for is a meaningful opportunity to delete data before the sale, yet the purchase agreement as filed holds no procedure for reversing the trace of a particular conversation in the weights. The company has never declared that it will hold children's conversations out of the sale, and has never described a method for pulling them out. So even where a deletion opportunity is guaranteed, what it reaches is the original logs. This is where the resemblance to genetic data lies. Once something has been mixed in, working out which fragment belonged to whom becomes a research problem in itself.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Case

This case usually gets read as the collapse of a chatbot company or as an argument over child safety. From where data is handled, a different scene shows up first. The moment a company falls apart, its data is classified as an asset that passes to creditors, and a court reviews that classification. Consent was given to a service, and the asset is sold along with the service. The gap between the two is being filled right now by the wording of a single order.

So there are three things to settle while conditions are ordinary. First, whether the inventory of data assets records, alongside each item, the scope of the consent under which it arrived. Second, whether that consent attaches only to the original data, or travels to the models, embeddings and evaluation sets built from it. Third, whether we have documented for ourselves that the design handling deletion requests stops at the original or covers the derivatives. Not many companies have an answer written down for the third question. The Character.AI auction is showing that gap in court first.
