## LinkedIn (EN)

A file's hash is the same number in every country. What that file is said to contain is not.

Researchers at Greece's FORTH Institute of Computer Science, working with several units of the Hellenic Police, published PreventCSA@EU on arXiv this month. It is the semantic model behind an annotated hash database the two are building with EU Internal Security Fund support, and it takes on the point where cross-border cooperation stops being a detection problem. When a label crosses an institutional boundary and the receiving side cannot place it in one of its own categories, nothing is automated. Someone opens the file again.

The design decision worth noting is the one the authors declined to make. They aligned with INHOPE's Universal Classification Schema at the level of concept definitions rather than by establishing structural equivalence between the two models. Instead of copying where each label sits, they confirmed only that both point at the same thing and built their own hierarchy. Several labels filed under people or investigative information in the source schema now sit under a class for visual depiction, because that is what they describe.

The paper states its own limits. INHOPE's schema is access-restricted, so not one aligned label name appears in the text, and the authors acknowledge this may narrow independent verification. The EU Centre database the ontology is built to connect to does not legally exist yet, with trilogue talks slipping to September after a fifth round closed without agreement.

The sequence is the argument. Rather than wait for the laws to converge, match the vocabulary so the systems can talk while the laws still differ.

## Facebook (EN)

There is a moment I keep picturing. Someone at a desk, a file that arrived from an agency in another country, and a label attached to it that will not sit cleanly in any box on their own screen.

The hash matches. It always does. A hash is the same number wherever it is computed.

The name is what fails to travel.

The same material can be plainly criminal in one country and a judgment call in the next. When that happens nothing is automated. A person opens the file again.

What stayed with me in the PreventCSA@EU paper, written at Greece's FORTH institute with the Hellenic Police, was the method of alignment rather than the model itself. They matched an international schema at the level of what each concept means, and deliberately not by making the two structures identical. Instead of copying where each label sits, they confirmed the two were pointing at the same thing and built their own hierarchy underneath.

Interpol has been working in the same grain for years. It sets aside only the material that would be illegal in every country with a relevant law, calls that Baseline, and shares that portion first. 'Partial agreement' seems the right name for this. You do not wait until everything lines up. You let the part that already lines up begin to move.

The scene Pebblous keeps meeting in data quality work is not a field left empty. It is the same thing going by two names.

"How many of the labels we exchange with our partners have been confirmed to mean the same thing?"

When names diverge, enforcement is not the only thing that stops. Measuring what is happening, and how much of it, becomes hard first. I read the paper as an argument that something still has to happen before the control list gets written.
