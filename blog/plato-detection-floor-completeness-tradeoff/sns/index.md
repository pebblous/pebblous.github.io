유럽우주국의 외계행성 탐사 위성 PLATO는 아직 발사되지 않았다. 그런데 9월 4일 arXiv에 올라온 논문 한 편이, PLATO의 M왜성 표본을 겨냥해 손본 탐색 파이프라인이 후보로 받아 줄 최저 신호세기를 어디에 긋느냐에 따라 치를 대가를 미리 재 두었다. 저자는 컨소시엄 바깥의 독립 연구자 한 사람이다.

측정 설계가 단순하다. 모의 광도곡선을 돌리면서, 행성을 심은 것마다 같은 별 같은 잡음에 행성만 빼놓은 쌍둥이를 하나씩 만들었다. 정답이 없다고 보증된 데이터라, 파이프라인이 거기서 무언가를 찾아내면 그것은 정의상 헛것이다. 그 쌍둥이가 465개다.

케플러는 이 두 수치를 미션이 끝난 뒤에야 쟀다. 발사 전에 고정한 선의 근거가 실제 데이터가 아니라 잡음에 대한 이론적 가정이었고, 그 가정이 어긋났기 때문이다.

페블러스가 데이터 품질을 진단하며 가장 자주 받는 질문도 기준을 몇으로 잡느냐다. 답 대신 곡선을 만들어 두자고 말하는 이유가 여기에 있다. 통과 기준을 정하는 자리에는 어디나 같은 환율이 걸려 있고, 갈리는 것은 그 환율을 손에 쥐고 선을 긋느냐다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Raise a detection floor by two points and the share of empty stars that still hand back a planet candidate drops from 79% to 0.6%.

PLATO, the European Space Agency's next exoplanet telescope, has not launched. A paper posted to arXiv on September 4 has already priced what moving that line will cost. Its author is a single independent researcher working outside the PLATO consortium.

The design is plain. Across the campaign, every simulated light curve with a planet in it was paired with a twin built from the same star and the same noise, minus the planet. That half is guaranteed to contain nothing, so whatever the pipeline reports there is a phantom by definition. There were 465 such twins.

Nothing about that is free. Over the same range where the phantoms thin out, the share of injected planets the pipeline hands back falls from 61% to 51%, and the author settles on 7.5 as the operating floor for discovery.

Kepler measured the same pair only after its mission ended. Its threshold had been fixed before launch on a theoretical noise model, and the model turned out to be wrong.

The question we field most often in data quality work is where to set the cutoff. The reason we offer a curve instead of a number is this one. Every acceptance rule carries the same exchange rate; what differs is whether anyone has measured it before drawing the line.

## Facebook (EN)

"So what number should we set the threshold to?"

It is the question we get most often when we go through someone's data quality, and the honest answer is almost always that it depends on the data. Which is an awkward thing to say out loud.

A paper posted to arXiv a few days ago answers that question with something other than a number.

It hands over a curve.

PLATO, the European Space Agency's next exoplanet telescope, has not launched. An independent researcher living in France took a transit search pipeline tuned for the PLATO M-dwarf sample, planted simulated planets in it, and ran the whole thing early.

The part of the design that stayed with me was the twins. For every light curve with a planet in it, there is a companion built from the same star and the same noise, with the planet left out. That half is guaranteed to hold nothing. Whatever the pipeline finds there is a phantom, by construction.

Set the weakest acceptable signal at 6 and 79% of those 465 empty stars still return a candidate. Move it to 8 and three of them do. Over that same stretch, the share of real planted planets recovered slides from 61% to 51%.

So what the paper leaves behind is not a threshold. It is closer to an exchange rate between phantoms and misses, written out so that any line you draw comes with the price of standing there.

Kepler learned its own rate afterwards. The line was fixed before launch on an assumption about noise rather than on the data, and only after four years of light curves could anyone count what that line had let through.

"What is our threshold actually resting on right now?"

Anomaly sensitivity. The bar for rejecting a labeled batch. Deduplication. PII detection. Every place we decide what passes carries the same exchange rate, and far fewer teams have measured theirs than you would expect. That is why we try to hand over a curve alongside a diagnosis rather than a single cutoff.

Where to draw the line stays yours. Whether you are holding the table that prices the move seems like the question that comes first.
