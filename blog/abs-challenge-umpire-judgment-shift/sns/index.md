메이저리그가 2026시즌에 들인 자동 볼·스트라이크 판정은 모든 공을 보지 않는다. 심판이 먼저 판정하고, 선수가 그 자리에서 이의를 걸어야만 기계가 그 공 하나를 다시 판정한다.

카이스트·연세대 공동연구팀이 이 제도 아래에서 무엇이 달라졌는지를 열두 시즌 치 투구 판정 411만 건으로 분석했다. 연구팀은 심판이 볼과 스트라이크를 가르는 자리와, 그 갈림이 얼마나 또렷한지를 따로 쟀다. 올해 움직인 것은 자리뿐이었다. 또렷함은 앞선 열한 시즌의 추세를 그대로 이어 갔다. 애매한 공을 더 잘 구분하게 된 것이 아니라 선을 긋는 위치가 기계 쪽으로 당겨졌다는 뜻이다.

정정 하나가 돌아왔을 때의 반응은 짧았다. 판정이 뒤집힌 직후 심판은 지적받은 그 경계만 정정 방향으로 손봤고, 나머지 세 경계로는 번지지 않았다. 같은 경기를 넘기면 그 흔적이 잡히지 않았다. 검수대에 오른 공 자체도 드물었다. 이의를 걸 수 있었던 기회 가운데 실제 요청은 5.03%였고, 기계가 봤다면 거의 확실히 뒤집었을 공조차 상당수가 그냥 넘어갔다. 선수들은 공의 정확한 좌표가 아니라 그 순간 눈에 보이는 단서에 기대어 요청 여부를 정했다.

오심의 총량은 줄었지만 종류는 갈렸다. 여섯 구종 모두 전체 오심률이 떨어졌는데, 그 감소는 거의 전부 존 밖 공을 스트라이크로 부르는 쪽에서 나왔다. 반대로 존 안 공을 볼로 부르는 오심은 체인지업에서 36.1% 늘었다. 선을 안쪽으로 당기면 따라오는 교환이다.

사람이 1차로 판단하고 AI가 일부만 골라 되짚는 구조는 데이터 라벨링과 품질 검수 현장의 표준 설계와 같은 모양이다. 검수를 붙이면 라벨이 정확해진다고들 하는데, 이 기록이 보여 준 것은 검수받지 않은 자리에서도 판정하는 사람의 기준선이 함께 움직였다는 사실이다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Major League Baseball's automated ball-strike system, introduced this season, does not look at every pitch. The umpire calls it first, and the machine rules again only when a player challenges on the spot.

A team from KAIST and Yonsei asked what changed under that arrangement, using 4.11 million pitch calls across twelve seasons. The researchers measured two things separately: where an umpire divides ball from strike, and how sharply that division is drawn. Only the placement moved in 2026. The sharpness stayed on the trajectory the previous eleven seasons had set. Umpires did not get better at telling borderline pitches apart. They moved the line toward the machine.

The response to any single correction was brief. After an overturn, umpires adjusted the reviewed edge in the corrected direction and left the other three alone. By the opening of their next game, the adjustment was no longer detectable. Few pitches reached review at all: players used 5.03% of the chances available to them, and many calls the machine would almost certainly have reversed went unchallenged. Their decisions tracked what was visible at the moment of the call rather than the ball's exact coordinates.

Total miscalls fell, but the mix shifted. All six pitch types saw their error rate drop, and nearly all of that came from pitches outside the zone no longer being called strikes. Inside the zone, changeups moved the other way, with wrong ball calls up 36.1%. Pull a boundary inward and this is the trade that follows.

A human ruling first while AI reviews a selected slice is the standard design of data labeling and quality assurance work. Adding reviewers is supposed to make labels more accurate. What this record shows is that the person doing the labeling moved too, including on the work no reviewer ever saw.

## Facebook (EN)

A scoreboard in Detroit put one pitch on display.

Six tenths of an inch outside the zone. Call overturned.

The hundreds of other pitches thrown in that ballpark that day never got the screen.

Major League Baseball's automated ball-strike system moves only when a player asks it to. The umpire rules on every taken pitch; the machine arrives when called. If the arrangement needs a name, review that only comes when called is close enough.

What a team from KAIST and Yonsei asked, with 4.11 million calls from twelve seasons in hand, was whether review that arrives that rarely changes the person being reviewed. They measured two things: where an umpire divides ball from strike, and how sharply that division is drawn. Only the placement moved this season. The sharpness kept to the path of the previous eleven.

Not better sight. A different place to put the line. That sentence has stayed with me well past the ballpark.

A person labels first, some fraction of the work goes to a review queue, and on whatever reaches the queue the reviewer has the last word. That is the shape of the pipelines we look at every day.

What lands in that queue is not the item furthest from the truth. It is the low confidence score, the item a worker flagged, the class someone decided to watch. The players sorted their pitches the same way: not by the ball's exact coordinates but by what they could see in the moment, and they used 5.03% of the chances they had.

"What picks the items that reach review in your pipeline, and when did you last count the errors that rule leaves behind?"

This is where our habit at Pebblous comes from, of asking teams to keep the conditions a label was made under and the trail of its review, not just the label's accuracy. The study itself was only possible because the public records carried a column marking which pitches had been overturned. Without that column, nobody could have asked whether the umpires' baseline had moved.

Whatever adjustment followed a correction did not survive the game it happened in.

If we want the person to change, one correction is evidently not enough. How often it should come back, and in what form, is a question we have not answered either.
