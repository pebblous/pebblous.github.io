Executive Summary

What happens if you collect fewer demonstration videos for a robot and put the saving into a larger model instead? A result that takes the question head-on is now in. It is a technical report named ScaleVLA, posted to arXiv on 16 November 2026. The authors swapped only the backbone that handles the robot's seeing and reading, from 2 billion parameters up to 552 billion, and left the action-generating module and the training conditions identical.

The number that stands out is 87.8%. That is the success rate the 552-billion backbone reached when each task was given only ten demonstrations, and it sits above the 85.6% the 2-billion backbone reached with fifty. The comparison came out of a setting where every model received the same number of training steps, and the authors note in the same report that the smaller backbone keeps improving when trained longer.

Sections 1 through 5 follow the design and the numbers the report discloses. Section 6, which reads the result as a budget question for teams collecting robot data, is this article's own interpretation.

1

What Goes In, What Comes Out

The ScaleVLA backbone is DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017, a 552-billion-parameter vision-language model that ScaleVLA freezes rather than trains. The more telling detail is that only half the model ever runs. The backbone has 20 encoder layers and 20 decoder layers, and the decoder that produces text is never called at all. ScaleVLA does not think in words. It takes only the intermediate representation the encoder leaves behind after reading the scene and the instruction.

There are three inputs. Camera images and the human-written instruction go into the backbone. The robot's own joint-state readings bypass the backbone and feed straight into the action module. Each token pulled from the backbone carries 5,120 dimensions, and image tokens are handled separately from the rest.

The action module is trained from scratch. Representations from the backbone pass through an adapter that compresses them to 2,048 dimensions, enter a 32-layer diffusion transformer, and come out as several upcoming motion steps bundled together. The bundle holds continuous values such as joint angles, not symbols or tokens. On LIBERO tasks the policy generates 16 steps at a time, executes 8 of them, and then replans. How many steps go into one chunk varies by task: 8 for the kitchen-environment tasks, 30 for the bimanual dexterous tasks, 16 on the real robot. State and action values are normalised against the 1st and 99th percentiles of the training data before they go in.

The generation method is flow matching, which starts from noise and converges on a motion over four passes. One device keeps that affordable. Across those four passes the giant backbone runs only once, at the start, and the representation it produced is reused through all four. Running a 552-billion-parameter model four times would not leave a speed usable for robot control.

Converting 552 billion straight into a cost figure overstates it. This backbone keeps several sets of experts and wakes only some of them for each input, a mixture-of-experts design, so the parameters that actually take part in computing one token are far fewer than the total. How many exactly is written differently in different outside sources, so this article does not fix a number for it. The dimensionality of the action space and the control frequency in hertz are also absent from the report.

Every Action Was Learned From Scratch

One premise matters a great deal in this experiment: no model pre-trained on robot actions was used. Most robot policies released these days start from weights pre-trained on large robot datasets, whereas the action side of ScaleVLA begins empty. What the report compares, then, is not how much robot experience a policy has, but how much the backbone's representational power helps when there is no robot experience at all.

The training data differs per benchmark. Four are simulated and the last is a physical robot. The two sides cost very different amounts per additional episode. In simulation the same environment can be cloned to yield tens or hundreds of episodes per task, but the 303 real episodes were stacked one at a time by a person teleoperating the robot, and 132 of those were recorded again for the insertion phase alone. The budget argument that returns in Section 6 rests on that asymmetry.

LIBERO All demonstrations across four suites; the sample-efficiency run used only 5, 10, 25 or 50 per task Simulation
LIBERO-PRO Policies trained on LIBERO were evaluated as they were, with no retraining Simulation (altered conditions)
RoboCasa 24 tasks, with 30 or 100 generated demonstrations per task Simulation
DexJoCo 5 bimanual dexterous tasks, 100 human demonstrations per task Simulation

The compute is noted too: 64 H200 GPUs at a global batch size of 640, with 5,000 steps for LIBERO, 20,000 for DexJoCo and 40,000 for the real-robot policy. That step count is identical across every condition in which the backbone size was varied. A clue that returns in Section 4 sits right there. Holding the steps fixed also means the comparison does not contain the answer to how far a smaller model gets when it trains longer.

3

Scores on Four Simulated Benchmarks

LIBERO comes first. It is a collection of manipulation tasks split into four suites, trained on the full set of demonstrations and scored by success rate. Scaling the backbone does little here. The 2-billion backbone already averages 91.2%, and the 552-billion backbone reaches 96.1%. On the easy suites of picking up and moving objects, even the small backbone climbs into the 98% range, so the gap nearly vanishes. The gap opens on the Long suite, where a task has to be carried through several stages: 77.8% for the 2-billion backbone against 90.2% for the 552-billion one.

Backbone Goal Long Four-suite average
Qwen3-VL 2B 90.4% 77.8% 91.2%
Cosmos-Reason2 8B 88.6% 87.2% 93.7%
Cosmos-Reason2 32B 95.2% 83.8% 94.0%
ScaleVLA 552B 96.8% 90.2% 96.1%

▲ LIBERO success rates. The average is the arithmetic mean over the Spatial, Object, Goal and Long suites. | Source: arXiv:2611.21607

Every value in the table above was produced by this report itself, running each backbone with the same action head and the same training conditions. Alongside them the report also lists figures published by other papers: 94.2% for π0 on the four-suite average, 83.9% for CoT-VLA, 76.5% for OpenVLA. The training setups differ, so putting them on one line and measuring is not sound, though the rough position is readable. The 96.1% of the 552-billion backbone sits under 2 percentage points above the best-known prior policy, and the 91.2% of the 2-billion backbone sits below it.

The gap widens sharply on LIBERO-PRO. It takes the same tasks, changes the background or the objects, and asks the policy to perform without any retraining. Here every policy collapses. Models that were posting scores in the 90s drop into the 20s and 30s. Averaged across the three conditions, the 552-billion backbone only manages 45.1%, but that is distinctly above 31.3%, the best average any small backbone posted. How much robustness survives a change of conditions varies with backbone size, and that is the most consistent signal in the report.

Strip the average away and look at the individual conditions, though, and the ordering is not always the same. On one of the three conditions the 32-billion backbone scored 32.4% against 31.2% for the 552-billion one. Scaling the backbone lifts the average without flipping every single condition along with it. It is safer to read backbone size as one of several axes that shape the score.

On RoboCasa, the kitchen-environment task set, the gap stands out most when demonstrations are scarce. Trained with 30 demonstrations per task, the small backbones cluster between 22% and 26% while the 552-billion backbone hits 30.7%. Raise the count to 100 and the small backbones land between 43% and 46%, the 552-billion one at 54.9%. On DexJoCo, the set of fine bimanual tasks, it scored 42.1% when only object positions were randomised and 24.4% when the whole environment was shaken. Both are the highest among the models compared.

The DexJoCo table also carries published figures for prior methods: 16.0% for DP-T, a diffusion-policy variant, and 10.1% for π0.5. Measured against those, even the smallest 2-billion backbone is nearly double at 31.9%. Scaling the backbone from 2 billion to 552 billion bought 10.2 percentage points, and adopting this architecture opened a wider gap than that. Skimming the numbers by backbone size alone hides this part.

Some tasks stayed unsolved no matter the scale. On the DexJoCo condition that randomises everything about the environment, the assembly task left every policy at or below 1.3%, and the 552-billion backbone was no exception. Scaling the backbone raises the overall average, and that is a counterexample to the idea that a particular task's failure disappears with it.

4

Where Ten Demos Beat Fifty

For anyone on the data-collection side, the most interesting experiment is elsewhere: a table that holds the task fixed, varies the number of demonstrations across 5, 10, 25 and 50, and measures success rate at each backbone size. The figures below are for the LIBERO Goal suite. Only two things move in this table, the demonstrations per task and the backbone size. The architecture of the action module and the number of training steps were matched across every cell.

The pair worth looking at is the second row against the fourth. The 552-billion backbone given only ten demonstrations posts 87.8%, while the 2-billion backbone given fifty stops at 85.6%. It won on a fifth of the data. On the Long suite, where a task must be carried through several stages, the direction holds as well. There the 552-billion backbone on ten demonstrations reaches 75.0% against 82.4% for the 2-billion backbone on fifty, so no reversal happens, yet at five demonstrations the spread is widest at 62.2% against 24.0%.

Two places call for care in reading this result. One is a phrase in the body of the report. The authors write that the large backbone needs a fifth of the demonstrations to pass the small backbone's baseline, and read alone that sentence leaves it unclear a fifth of which. The table shows that the 77.4% the 552-billion backbone reaches on five demonstrations does not even match the 85.2% the 2-billion backbone reaches on 25. The reversal happens at ten demonstrations, and ten is a fifth of fifty.

The other is the training budget. The authors measured separately under the five-demonstration condition, stretching training steps from 2,000 to 40,000, and reported that the small backbone keeps improving on the long-horizon suite from 10.4% all the way to 55.2%. Over the same range the 552-billion backbone is recorded as rising from 46.4% to 71.8%. So all the comparisons above are results under an equal step budget for everyone, not a comparison that ran each model as far as it could go. The authors flag this as a limitation themselves.

5

The Real Robot and the Cost of Inference

There is exactly one test on a physical robot. A dual-arm robot picks up a charger and plugs it into a power strip: the right arm grips the charger and hands it to the left, and the left aligns it with the socket, inserts it and lets go. One camera sits overhead and two at the wrists. The team ran 10 trials in distribution and 15 under perturbation, moving the charger by up to 16cm, rotating it by up to 30 degrees, or relocating the power strip. Success was judged by a person watching rather than by an automatic sensor, and the authors list the narrow evaluation scope as a limitation.

Policy In distribution (10 trials) Perturbed (15 trials) Median time to finish
Cosmos-Reason2 2B + same action head 4 5 147.1s
π0.5 + same action head 8 7 132.0s
ScaleVLA 552B 9 8 114.4s

▲ The charger-insertion task. This is the only physical task reported. | Source: arXiv:2611.21607

The jump from 4 to 9 holds only if the 2-billion backbone is the baseline. π0.5, the closest comparison among released robot policies, succeeded 8 times under the same conditions, so the difference there is one trial. The effect of scaling the backbone is large against a small model and small against a well-built prior policy. The failure modes separate a little more clearly. The 2-billion backbone left too little clearance between the two arms and triggered the safety stop several times, and ScaleVLA triggered none.

Speed has to be read along two separate tracks. While running the charger task, ScaleVLA took 384 milliseconds to produce one action chunk, the slowest of the three. Yet the total time to finish the task was the shortest. Isolate the insertion phase and its median is 58.1 seconds, shorter than π0.5's 81.2 and the 2-billion backbone's 85.9. The authors write that it is 13% faster to completion than π0.5 and 28% faster through the insertion phase, and explain it as a slow reaction finishing earlier because it wastes fewer motions. That explanation is the authors' reading, not a measurement.

Inference cost has its own measurement table. It was taken in the kitchen-task environment rather than on the charger task, on a server with 8 H200 GPUs, sending one request at a time over 3,000 measurements, so it cannot be set directly against the 384 milliseconds above. This measurement ran on vLLM, and the authors state that they did not break it down component by component against the different execution path used for evaluation.

The same table carries the mid-sized backbones, and that part of the cost curve is fairly flat. The 8-billion backbone comes in at 124.8 milliseconds and the 32-billion at 140.7, not far off the 114.7 of the 2-billion one. All three ran on a single GPU. The one stretch where the time jumps is the 552-billion side. Isolating the backbone's own share, it climbs slowly from 44.7 milliseconds to 68.4 and then doubles to 140.7 at 552 billion. That is the same number as the 32-billion backbone's 140.7 just above, but that one is the full latency and this one is the backbone's share alone.

The sentence the authors pull out of this table is that 276 times the parameters costs only 1.9 times the latency. The number itself holds. Two things written in the same table are missing from that sentence, though. The 2-billion backbone ran on 1 GPU and ScaleVLA used 4. And ScaleVLA processed 568 tokens, more than double the 212 of the smaller models. Why it takes in more is not broken down anywhere in the report. The authors do write in the body that the hardware cost is higher.

6

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Report

Robot data is data a person makes by hand, sitting in front of equipment. One demonstration consumes human time, robot uptime and floor space, which makes the number of episodes to collect a budget line. That is why the question of whether a bigger model means fewer demonstrations is a question about money rather than a technical debate.

Reading this report's answer as permission to collect less data would be stretching it. The place where ten demonstrations beat fifty is one suite of simulated tasks, and on the suite next to it no reversal occurred. The physical test is 25 trials on a single task. Scaling the backbone costs four GPUs at inference. And every comparison sits under the same training-step budget.

What has changed instead is the scale on which the trade is weighed. Collecting more demonstrations used to be almost the only route to another percentage point of performance. What this report shows is that moving the same budget onto the backbone actually works, and that this option carries a price tag of its own in GPUs. Which of the two routes is cheaper will come out differently from task to task.

So that nothing here arrives blended together, the contents of this article are separated into three kinds.

Kind Content
Verified The figures in Sections 1 through 5 were checked against the body of the report published on arXiv. The architecture, the training-data composition, the success rates on the four benchmarks, the outcome of the 25 physical trials and the latency measurements all belong here.
The authors' claims The conclusion that a large vision-language model can serve as the representational foundation of a robot policy, the explanation that a slow reaction finishes sooner because it wastes fewer motions, and the phrase about needing a fifth of the demonstrations are the authors' own reading. The last of those has a blurred basis of comparison, which Section 4 addresses separately.
Our interpretation Reading this as a shift in the budget balance between data and model is this article's interpretation. The report never compares budgets or costs; it only records the fact of four GPUs.

A team accumulating robot data can take this report and ask three questions of it.

  • Is our task stuck because there are too few demonstrations, or because it falls apart when conditions change? If it is the former, scaling the backbone is now an option; if the latter, what matters more is that 45.1% was the ceiling on LIBERO-PRO.
  • Are the demonstrations we are collecting now teaching the backbone something it already knows? Recognising scenes and objects is work the backbone can take over, but how this particular robot body moves lives only in the demonstrations.
  • Is this a site that can keep four GPUs attached to inference at all times? Unlike a lab score, that condition is the first one a deployment runs into.

One more thing remains from the data-quality side. If fewer demonstrations will do, the quality of each one that remains matters more rather than less. Set this next to Robot Demonstration Data Keeps the Detours Operators Make, which covered the finding that a path an operator wandered through transfers almost intact into the learned motion, and a direction appears. That piece looks at quality on the collection side; this one looks at size on the model side. Put together, they say that the more a design leans on ten demonstrations, the cleaner those ten have to be. If the VLA architecture itself is new to you, What Is a VLA (Vision-Language-Action) Model? lays out the concepts. For how several teams solved the same structure differently, there is Three Teams, Three Robot Brains. This article holds the structure fixed and changes only the size, so its axis differs from that comparison.
