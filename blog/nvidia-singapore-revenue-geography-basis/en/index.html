Executive Summary

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission is examining how Nvidia accounts for its revenue. When Reuters reported it exclusively on 20 November, Nvidia shares fell more than 13% in a single session and about $490 billion of market value went with them. What the reporting points to is one paragraph in a footnote filed roughly a year earlier.

What Nvidia touched is the rule that fills a single field called region. Regional revenue had been counted by the address where a customer receives its invoice, and the company moved to the location of the customer's headquarters. Singapore, which carried $10.2 billion and 22% of total revenue in the quarter before, was absent from the table in the quarter after. No revenue moved; the map did. The change itself sat in the footnote, and even Michael Burry, its sharpest critic, wrote that he has no evidence of a misstatement.

Sections 1 through 4 set out what the quarterly filings and the reporting establish. Section 5, which reads the episode as a question about how a data field is defined and how that definition is recorded, is this article's own interpretation.

1

$490 Billion of Market Value Gone in One Day

Reuters reported exclusively on 20 November that the SEC's enforcement division is looking into Nvidia's accounting. It cited two people familiar with the matter and said the question is whether the company's disclosures misled investors. That the SEC was investigating Nvidia at all had been reported three days earlier by The Wall Street Journal. What was new that day is the line saying the investigation's focus is accounting.

The market moved the same day. Nvidia shares fell more than 13% and the company's market value dropped from $3.52 trillion to the $3.03 trillion range. About $490 billion went in a session, the second largest single-day loss on record. Nvidia also slipped to fourth by market value, overtaken by Alphabet.

The decline did not begin that day. Nvidia shares had fallen for seven consecutive weeks through 20 November, losing 46% over that stretch. Measured against the October high, $2.6 trillion to $2.7 trillion had evaporated.

The accounting inquiry landed while Nvidia was already working through several crises at once. Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive and from the board on 11 November over a personal matter, and chief financial officer Colette Kress took over as interim chief executive. On 16 November the California attorney general issued a subpoena asking whether advanced chips had reached China. At the 17 November earnings release, quarterly revenue of $115.8 billion beat expectations, and the share price still did not turn.

Of these events the accounting inquiry is the one of a different kind. The rest happened in November, but the accounting treatment the inquiry is said to focus on had already been filed roughly a year earlier. A fact that was read late became the event.

2

From Billing Address to Headquarters, One Field Is Redefined

In the quarter running from May to July 2025, the second quarter of Nvidia's fiscal 2026, regional revenue was counted by the address at which the customer receives its invoice. Singapore stands at $10.2 billion in that table, 22% of total revenue. The figure is 81% higher than the same quarter a year earlier, and $19.173 billion across the six months to date.

Beside those numbers Nvidia had attached a caveat of its own. The footnote reads: "Customers use Singapore to centralize invoicing while our products are almost always shipped elsewhere." It goes on: "Over 99% of controlled Data Center compute revenue billed to Singapore was for orders from U.S.-based customers." The Singapore line marks where the invoice arrived rather than where the chips were installed, and Nvidia was the first to say so.

The rule changes from the following quarter. The filing for the quarter from August to October 2025 reads: "Revenue by geographic area is based upon the location of the customers' headquarters. The end customer and shipping location may be different from our customers' headquarters location." The reason comes in one sentence: "We believe changing to revenue based upon the location of our customers' headquarters provides a better representation of the geographic profile of our revenue." Prior quarters were recast on the same basis.

A single transaction carries several addresses. There is the place the invoice is sent, the place the product is shipped, the end customer that actually uses it, and the headquarters of the company that bought it. Nvidia's new footnote states plainly that these can differ from one another. The address that enters the regional revenue table is one of the four. Any of them answers the question of where a sale happened, but unless a reader knows which one was picked, the table has no settled meaning.

The result shows up in the table at once. The Singapore line that had carried 22% of total revenue through the previous quarter is gone, absorbed into the Other item. Across fiscal 2025 as a whole, Singapore revenue on the billing basis was $23.68 billion, about 18% of the total, and under the new rule that amount scattered into the United States and Other.

Nvidia did not say separately which lines received it. FourWeekMBA, reconstructing the two tables side by side, puts 59.7% of the net $27.19 billion that left Singapore and Other into the U.S. line. That allocation is an outside estimate and appears nowhere in the filings.

The totals were left alone. This is not a restatement of the financial statements but a change to how revenue is divided by region, so the quarterly revenue figures themselves are identical under both rules. In the one quarter FourWeekMBA lined up before and after the recast, total revenue of $35.01 billion was the same on both sides and only the U.S. share moved, from 42% to 56%.

The share booked outside the United States moved with it. On the new basis, revenue from outside the United States was 31% in the third quarter of fiscal 2026 and 34% across the nine months. A year earlier the same periods came to 44% and 41%. Two different changes are mixed into that comparison. Nothing published separates the part that follows from the change of rule from the part that follows from an actual change in the customer base.

3

The Footnote Was There, and It Was Read a Year Later

Read this far, nothing was hidden. The change was written into the quarterly footnote along with the reason for it. No one has argued so far that a disclosure obligation was breached.

The question is when that footnote was read. About a year sits between the date the rule changed and the date the market treated it as an event. Through that year Nvidia's regional revenue table was filed each quarter on the new basis, and investors formed their judgments on it.

The recast reached back into the old figures too, which means two tables now cover the same period: the one filed first and the one recalculated later. The summer 2025 filing carries the Singapore line with an amount against it, and the table the autumn filing recalculated on the new basis has no such line. Both are figures Nvidia filed and both are correct. Which rule produced them is not written in the table; it is in the footnote. Numbers get quoted with the table lifted out on its own, and what those numbers count stays behind in the footnote.

The person who made the most of that gap is Michael Burry. Writing in his paid newsletter, he disclosed that he holds put options on Nvidia and then called the new rule "a much less geographically precise methodology." His point is that a headquarters address is a worse clue than a billing address for anyone trying to follow where the chips physically end up.

Burry raised a second point. Many people remember that China's share of revenue fell after export controls arrived, and few noticed that Singapore's share grew quietly over the same stretch and then vanished in one go. He suspects the change obscures how much revenue is entangled with China.

Burry also wrote that he has no evidence of an outright misstatement. The issue is not whether something is false but when it was said and how thickly it was explained. The quarter right after Singapore climbed to 22% of total revenue is the quarter in which the rule changed, in a single footnote paragraph, with no separate notice.

4

One Field, Two Kinds of Exposure

The property that a Singapore billing address is not the actual destination does not stop at financial disclosure. The same property turns up on the export-control side.

Megaspeed, the semiconductor distributor named in allegations that Nvidia chips reached China, is reported to have bought more than $4 billion of chips and equipment from Nvidia, and it has been investigated over claims that it routed around U.S. export controls on China. Singapore's authorities began looking into it in February 2025. The subpoena the California attorney general sent on 16 November also asks whether advanced chips flowed into China. Neither matter is settled, and both remain allegations under investigation.

Nvidia has answered those allegations by saying that more than 99% of data centre revenue billed to Singapore came from orders placed by U.S.-headquartered customers. It is the same argument the company gave for moving the accounting basis to headquarters location.

The same field works in two directions. In financial disclosure it is the ground for reclassification, because a billing address is not a real location and a headquarters address is held to be more accurate. In an export-control investigation it is the gap in the trail, because a billing address alone does not tell anyone where a chip went. One field definition produced two kinds of exposure at the same time.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Case

Read only as a story about share prices and regulators, this episode leaves nothing behind for people who work with data. Read another way, it is a case in which the definition of one classification column changed. No revenue moved, and the only thing that differed was which address fills the field called region. A country left the table on that basis and another country's share rose by 14 percentage points.

The same column sits in any organisation's data warehouse. It is country in the customer table, region in the orders table, and revenue by region on the dashboard. Ask what fills those fields and the answers rarely converge on one thing. It might be a billing address, a shipping address, a corporate headquarters or the IP address a user connected from. More often, different sources arrive carrying different rules mixed together.

What the Nvidia case shows is not that definitions can change. It is what has to remain once they do. Nvidia at least recorded that it had changed the rule and why, and recalculated prior quarters on the same basis. That is why analysis comparing the two bases is possible now, and why this article could put the numbers side by side at all. Internal data rarely retains that much.

Anyone who owns a classification column has three things worth checking.

  • Is it written down somewhere what fills the region field? If the table description stops at the word region, the column has no definition.
  • Has the definition ever changed, and is the date of the change on record? Without a schema change history, nobody knows from which point a different yardstick was in use.
  • When the rule changed, was historical data recalculated, and was the value before the recalculation kept as well? With only one of the two, last year's report and this year's cannot be compared on the same yardstick.

For anyone working with AI the question gets heavier by one layer. An analyst reading a table can ask the owner what region means when something looks off, and a model that trains on or queries the same table has nobody to ask. It works from the column name and the values. Put figures from two years ago and figures from this year on one chart without knowing where the definition changed, and the model starts explaining a change that never happened. This is why AI-Ready Data means more than clean values. What the value counts, and since when it has been counted that way, has to travel with it.

Whatever the Nvidia inquiry concludes, one thing is already established. The revenue was left exactly where it was, the definition of the field that counts it changed, and a country left the books. How your own region column was filled and from when, and how many days it takes to find the answer, is the measure of an organisation's metadata.

Editor's Note

Singapore revenue of $10.2 billion, the 22% share and the verbatim footnote on billing were read directly in the quarterly report (Form 10-Q) Nvidia filed for the period ended 27 July 2025. The wording of the switch to headquarters location and the share of revenue booked outside the United States come from the quarterly report for the period ended 26 October 2025. For 20 November, outlets put the share price fall anywhere from the low teens to 14% and the market value loss between $490 billion and $494 billion, and the more conservative figures are used here. Total revenue of $35.01 billion and the move in the U.S. share from 42% to 56% come from FourWeekMBA's reconstruction and are not a comparison table Nvidia itself filed. The allocation putting 59.7% of the net $27.19 billion that left Singapore and Other into the U.S. line in fiscal 2025 comes from the same analysis. Michael Burry's criticism is taken from reporting that quotes his paid newsletter, in which he disclosed that he holds put options on Nvidia. The passages on Megaspeed and Jensen Huang's resignation describe what has been reported and are not settled fact. The metadata reading in section 5 is this article's own judgment and appears in no public source.
