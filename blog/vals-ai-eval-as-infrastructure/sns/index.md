## LinkedIn (EN)

A company that grades AI models, not one that builds them, has been valued at $400 million.

Vals AI closed a $40 million Series A led by a16z on August 13. It sells neither models nor the data to train them. It sells exams built from the actual work of lawyers, financial analysts and software engineers, along with the machinery that grades the answers against an expert standard.

The price follows a collapse of trust in public benchmarks. Researchers at ETH Zurich and Stanford examined 60 widely used language model benchmarks and found the top models were no longer distinguishable in 29 of them. The surprise sits in the remedy. The private test sets the industry had been offering as the cure did not stop saturation. What held up was expert-written questions, plus the discipline of retiring an exam once every model aces it.

This market has a shadow. Moody's failed badly in 2008, in part because of its commercial ties to the issuers it rated, and any grader that grows large enough will face the same question about its own independence.

At Pebblous we keep meeting the quieter version of this in data quality work: agreeing on what counts as the right answer takes longer than swapping the model. What has been priced here is not a hidden exam. It is the work of writing a new one every time the models improve.

## Facebook (EN)

In the meeting where a team decides which model to adopt, what usually ends up on the screen is a single leaderboard.

You can see that the gap between the top three is somewhere past the decimal point, and you still have to pick one of them.

A study published this year says that decimal point had stopped telling you anything.

Researchers at ETH Zurich and Stanford looked at 60 widely used language model benchmarks and found that in 29 of them the scores of the best models had collapsed into measurement noise.

Exams age too. Not a single question has to leak: labs polishing their models against a known metric is enough to push the top scores together.

What stayed with me was what came next.

The industry's standing answer to contamination has been to keep the questions private. In this study, privacy did not stop saturation. Age and size were far stronger predictors.

What held up was questions written by hand by experts, and the practice of retiring an exam once it saturates and replacing it with a harder one.

A few days ago Vals AI raised $40 million at a $400 million valuation. It converts real work in law, finance and software into exams and grades the answers against an expert standard.

So what got a price was not a piece of content sold once, but work that has to be redone every time the models get better.

"Forget the vendor leaderboard: do you have an exam built from your own work?"

This is close to what we keep meeting in data quality work at Pebblous. Agreeing on what counts as the right answer takes longer than swapping the model, and scores piled up without that agreement are hard to reuse later.

Any organization bringing agents inside will eventually get around to writing its own exam. Far fewer have decided when to retire it.

When was the exam your team uses written? That is the question I find myself starting with now.
