미국 연방자동차운송안전청이 자율주행 트럭 회사 오로라에 2031년까지 반사 삼각대를 세우지 않아도 된다는 면제를 내줬다. 멈춘 대형 트럭 뒤에 사람이 내려 삼각대 세 개를 세우라는 규정 대신, 운전실 지붕에 단 호박색 경광등이 그 자리를 맡는다. 오로라만의 허가는 아니다. 레벨 4 자율주행 시스템을 운영하는 회사가 당국에 미리 서면으로 알리면 같은 면제를 쓸 수 있다.

근거는 석 달짜리 임시 허가 기간의 운행 기록이다. 트럭 34대가 50만 마일 넘게 달리는 동안 경광등의 결함이나 오작동은 한 건도 보고되지 않았다.

반대 의견을 낸 쪽은 같은 기록에서 다른 숫자를 셌다. 그 기간에 경광등이 실제로 켜져 있던 시간은 모두 합쳐 10시간이 채 되지 않고, 그나마 예정된 정차 중에 켠 것이어서 비상 상황의 성능을 보여 주지 못한다는 지적이었다.

결정문의 논리는 경광등이 삼각대보다 낫다는 입증이 아니다. 오로라 연구에서 뒤차가 반응하기 시작한 평균 거리는 경광등 135.71m, 삼각대 133.24m로 차이가 2.47m에 그친다. 대신 문서에는 이런 문장이 그대로 적혀 있다. "FMCSA는 반사 삼각대가 운전자 반응 시간을 유의미하게 개선한다는 데이터 역시 갖고 있지 않다." 삼각대가 낫다고 볼 근거가 없으니 경광등을 막을 근거도 없다는 쪽으로 서 있는 결정이다.

면제의 대가로 오로라는 경광등 관련 사고를 닷새 안에 보고하고, 해마다 오작동의 빈도와 날짜, 도로 종류, 차량 구성을 적은 연례 보고서를 내야 한다. 항목만 보면 앞으로 그 공백을 메울 자료는 이 보고서들뿐이다. 그런데 같은 결정문에 사업자가 영업비밀로 지정한 정보는 법이 허용하는 한 공개하지 않는다는 조항이 함께 들어 있고, 이번 승인의 근거가 된 임시 허가 기간 보고서도 실제로 그렇게 제출돼 공개 요구가 거절됐다.

사람의 자리를 자동화로 바꿀 때 같은 수준의 안전을 증명하는 일은 결국 무엇을 기록하기로 정했느냐에 달린다. 페블러스가 데이터 품질을 볼 때 값 자체만이 아니라 그 값이 만들어진 조건과 기록의 행선지를 함께 남기라고 말하는 이유도 같은 자리에 있다. 적지 않기로 한 것은 5년 뒤에도 모르는 채로 남는다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

The U.S. Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration has excused Aurora's driverless trucks from setting out reflective warning triangles until 2031. Where the rule requires a driver to climb down and place three triangles behind a stopped truck, an amber beacon on the roof of the cab now stands in. The permission is not Aurora's alone: any operator of a Level 4 automated driving system can use it after giving the agency written notice.

The record behind the decision covers a three-month temporary authorization. Across more than 500,000 miles run by 34 trucks, no beacon defect or malfunction was reported.

Commenters opposing the exemption counted a different number from the same record. The beacons were actually lit for under ten hours in total, and those hours came during planned stops, which they argued says nothing about how the device performs in an emergency.

The agency's reasoning is not that beacons outperform triangles. In Aurora's on-road study, following drivers began reacting at an average of 135.71 m with beacons against 133.24 m with triangles, a gap of 2.47 m. What the document says instead is this: "FMCSA similarly lacks data showing that warning triangles significantly improve driver reaction time." The decision rests on the absence of evidence for the incumbent device rather than on evidence for the new one.

In exchange, Aurora must report any crash involving the beacons within five days and file an annual report listing malfunction counts with their dates, road types and vehicle configurations. On paper those filings are the only thing that will close the gap. The same decision also carries a clause withholding information a carrier designates as confidential business information, and the temporary-period reports this approval rests on were filed exactly that way, with a request to release them refused.

Proving that automation is as safe as the person it replaced comes down to what a team decided to record before the swap. That is the same reason Pebblous asks teams to keep the conditions a data point was produced under, and where its record goes, alongside the value itself. Whatever nobody agreed to write down stays unknown five years from now.

## Facebook (EN)

When a heavy truck stops on the shoulder of a U.S. highway, the driver has to get out.

The rule gives them ten minutes to place three reflective triangles at set distances front and back. Those few minutes of walking beside moving traffic are what the regulation asks for.

Aurora's driverless trucks have no one to get out.

In a decision published in the Federal Register on 9 October, the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration found that an amber beacon flashing on the roof of the cab is enough to stand in for those triangles. The exemption runs five years, to 2031.

What stayed with me after reading the decision was not the outcome but a sentence written into the same document.

"FMCSA similarly lacks data showing that warning triangles significantly improve driver reaction time."

The record supporting the new device is under ten hours of actual illumination across three months. The record supporting the device that has been in the rulebook far longer is not in the agency's hands at all. This was not a finding that one is safer than the other. It was a decision made while both remained unknown. A comparison without evidence, if it needs a name.

The agency did not leave the gap untended. In exchange for the exemption, Aurora must report any crash involving the beacons within five days and file yearly on how often they malfunctioned, when, where, on what kind of road, in what vehicle configuration. On paper, those filings are the only thing that will ever fill the blank.

And in the same document sits a clause withholding whatever a carrier designates as confidential business information, as far as the law allows. The temporary-period reports this approval rests on were filed under exactly that designation, and the request to release them was turned down. What anyone outside can check are the totals: 34 trucks, 500,000 miles, zero malfunctions.

"Who reads the records a company hands over in exchange for being excused from a rule?"

This is where our habit at Pebblous comes from, of asking teams to keep the conditions a data point was produced under, and where its record goes, rather than the accuracy of the value on its own. What a team decides to write down is what it will be able to know five years later.

When a machine takes over work a person used to do, the claim that it is just as safe can only be settled by records accumulating somewhere. If they accumulate where no one can read them, I am not sure what any of us will know in five years that we do not know now.
