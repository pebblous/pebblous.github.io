## LinkedIn (EN)

A NASA team has published a field guide to its own broken pixels.

SPHEREx, the all-sky spectral survey launched in March 2025, finished the first year of its observing plan, and its team posted a catalogue of eight low-level electrical artifacts found in the flight data. The word the paper uses is bestiary.

Ground testing had anticipated three of the eight. Four appeared only once the instrument reached orbit. One of them turns a reverse-biased pixel into a faint emitter whose light travels sideways through the detector material like a fiber, and in an image of Saturn its effects reached beyond 270 pixels. No laboratory setup had ever created that condition.

What makes the paper unusual is that it records where the masking fails with the same care it records the masking. Saturn moves across the sky, so it is absent from the star catalogue the source masks are built from, and the mask does not cover the full extent of the artifact. Image persistence has no answer yet and is deferred to a follow-up paper.

Most pipelines delete outliers quietly and keep only the result. The data Pebblous runs into most often is not data full of defects, but data where nobody can say what was removed or why.

## Facebook (EN)

When we drop an outlier from a pipeline, we rarely write down why it was strange.

The count goes into a log. The judgment stays in the head of whoever made it.

When that person moves on, the judgment goes with them.

I kept returning to that thought while reading a SPHEREx paper posted on arXiv a few days ago. NASA's all-sky spectral survey finished the first year of its observing plan, and from that flight data the team pulled out eight low-level electrical defects and gave each one a name. Charge blooming. Snowballs. Crosstalk. The word in the title is bestiary.

What held me was not the count. Ground testing had anticipated only three of them. Four appeared for the first time after launch, and one of those turns out to be light trapped inside the detector material, traveling sideways like a fiber, reaching more than 270 pixels away in an image of Saturn. No one had ever built that condition into a lab test.

And then there is the part that kept me reading.

The paper records where each recipe fails with the same care it records the recipe. Saturn moves, so it is not in the star catalogue the masks are built from, and the mask does not cover the whole artifact. Persistence has no answer yet, so it is handed to a follow-up paper.

"How far can I trust this data?"

To that question, from whoever inherits the data, the team answered with a list of gaps rather than a claim about their own pipeline. Cleaning a defect and recording one seem to be different kinds of work. Cleaned data ends with the team that cleaned it. A defect with a name travels to the next instrument flying the same detectors.

I have been asking what our own bestiary looks like.
