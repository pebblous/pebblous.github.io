## LinkedIn (EN)

Italy's Legislative Decree 179/2026 took effect on October 23, and a dismissal decided by automation alone is now null and void. There is no transition period.

Article 41 draws its line in a single sentence. Decisions on entering into, managing and terminating an employment relationship belong to a natural person exercising effective and autonomous power. Everything from hiring to dismissal sits inside that line. A clause in the onboarding paperwork consenting to automated assessment carries no weight.

The provision goes further next to that prohibition. A worker may ask for an explanation covering whether and to what extent AI influenced the decision, which main factors and parameters were considered, and what the human intervention contributed. None of the three asks how the system works today. All three ask what was in place on the day the decision was taken.

One square is left empty. Searching and pre-selecting applications is not a final decision, the statute says, and it adds that this holds even where the screening is what stops an application from advancing. By the time a person begins choosing, the pool has already been narrowed. The EU AI Act's high-risk obligations reach that stage on December 2, 2027.

If you run a personnel or performance system, the thing to check is not model accuracy but retention design. Without the input values from that day a year ago, the as-of date of those inputs, the version of the model and the criteria, and a log of what the reviewer changed, there is nothing to answer any of the three items with. ## Facebook (EN)

Picture someone holding a dismissal letter and asking why it was them.

In Italy, since October 23, that question has to be answered.

Article 41 of Legislative Decree 179/2026. Decisions on hiring, discipline and dismissal cannot rest on automated processing alone, and a dismissal taken in breach of that is null and void. A signature on a consent form in the onboarding pack changes nothing.

The demanding half of the provision turned out not to be the prohibition. Sitting next to it is a right to an explanation.

A worker may ask three things. Whether AI influenced the decision, and how much. Which main factors and parameters were considered. What the human intervention contributed.

All three point to the same place. Not the system as it runs today, but the day the decision was taken.

A written explanation can be produced at any time. The state of things on that day cannot.

"As of what date were the values that went into the model that morning?"

If that question arrives a year later, the people who can reach the answer do not sit in the legal department. If all that was kept is a pointer to the source table, the numbers from that day are gone the moment the table refreshes. If all the log holds is that someone clicked approve, the third item is empty too.

The statute also leaves one square blank. Searching and pre-selecting applications is not a final decision, and the text adds that this holds even where the screening is what stops an application from advancing.

That blank square is where the right to an explanation goes dark. By the time a person starts choosing, the pool has already narrowed, and the people cut there often never learn an algorithm was involved.
