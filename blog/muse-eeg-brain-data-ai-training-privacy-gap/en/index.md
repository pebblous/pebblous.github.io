# The Muse Sleep Headband Already Trains an AI on Users

_A yearlong Science investigation — Interaxon trained a brain model on about 50,000 hours of Muse users_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> The recordings from the EEG band worn to bed have gone into the training of an AI model. The device is Muse, the sleep-and-meditation headband built by the Canadian company Interaxon. It is not the Meta AI agent that carries the same name; this one is a piece of hardware whose forehead electrodes measure the electrical signals of the brain.

> The finding comes from a yearlong investigation by the journal Science, reported with the Pulitzer Center's AI Accountability Network and published on 4–5 November 2026. Reading the privacy policies of 57 consumer EEG companies worldwide, the reporters found nine that had publicly said they were building large AI models of the brain, and not one of them had written that fact into its own policy. Interaxon amended its policy only after confirming to Science that it had trained a model on roughly 50,000 hours of user EEG.

> Sections 1 through 4 report what the Science investigation and the coverage of it establish. Section 5 is this article's own reading of what the episode means for anyone who handles training data.

## Nine Companies Build Brain Models, and Not One Policy Said So

The Science investigation was carried out over a year by Jonathan Moens, a freelance investigative reporter, working with the Pulitzer Center's AI Accountability Network. Its subject was the companies selling consumer EEG devices, the kind that measure the brain's electrical signals at the scalp. Fifty-seven of them were selected worldwide; the reporters read each privacy policy line by line and interviewed companies, experts and people who actually wear the devices.

The documents themselves were uneven. Four of the 57 had nothing that could be called a privacy policy. Eleven had one, but it covered only the information of website visitors and said nothing about the EEG the device and its app collect. Of the 42 that remained, roughly two-thirds stated plainly that they gather brain data.

The routes out of the company were wider than that. Every company with a policy but one permits sharing in de-identified or aggregated form, and only six have ruled out selling brain data. More than two-thirds state that if an acquisition or merger changes the owner, the data can change hands with it, without asking the user. Science added that at this stage, actually putting brain data up for sale still looks like an uncomfortable move for most of these companies.

Which box was empty is the finding of the investigation. Nine of the 57 had publicly said they were building large AI models of the brain, and at the outset not one had written that into its privacy policy. The model work itself was not hidden; some companies had announced it through press releases and other channels. It was absent only from the document a user reads and agrees to when signing up.
