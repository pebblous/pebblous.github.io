## LinkedIn (EN)

A paper posted to arXiv last month spent no new telescope time at all.

Andrew Gould of Ohio State University left the ten years of microlensing records held by KMTNet, run by the Korea Astronomy and Space Science Institute, exactly where they are. He then calculated how precisely those records alone could measure the Einstein radius of a black hole drifting through the Galaxy on its own. The answer is 0.5 milliarcseconds.

That figure holds only across the roughly 12 square degrees watched most intensively. Over the 28 square degrees beyond it, the error doubles. What splits the precision is not the object being measured. The same black hole crossing an equally bright star yields a different limit depending on how often that patch of sky was photographed. An observing plan drawn up a decade ago is setting today's measurement floor.

Gould states the condition plainly. Ground-based observation still suffers a great deal of systematics, and the numbers hold only on the assumption that those systematics can be controlled. The reprocessing needed to select candidates has been completed for a single year, 2023, out of ten. No black hole has been found yet.

KMTNet was built to hunt exoplanets. Those records could become material for an entirely different question because the per-field cadence and the original images survived alongside the results. ## Facebook (EN)

There is a photograph of a crane lifting a telescope dome onto a Chilean mountainside in 2014.

The people standing there were not looking for black holes. They were looking for Earth-mass planets in the habitable zone.

Twelve years later a different researcher opened the same records. On top of them he laid a calculation: how heavy is a black hole drifting alone through the Galaxy. Not one new image was taken.

What held me up while writing was not the figure of 0.5 milliarcseconds. It was that the precision splits by a factor of two inside a single database. Not because the objects differ, but because of how often that patch of sky was photographed.

"If conditions set a decade ago decide today's measurement floor, whose question will today's conditions answer twelve years from now?"

I am not sure yet whether to call this the residual value of data. What had to survive in this case, though, is clear enough. A record of how often each field was observed. A time series thick enough to absorb a year that went missing entirely. And an original that can be processed again from the beginning, not just a finished product. Those three are the questions Pebblous keeps running into whenever the subject is AI-Ready Data.

The paper has not found a single black hole. Only the calculation is finished. And everything that went into it had been quietly accumulating for ten years.
