Executive Summary

This article reads a paper posted to arXiv on October 22, 2026, titled "Verus-Insight: Towards Explainable Artifact Detection in AI-Generated Videos." Fourteen authors put their names to it, among them Peking University and the Kling video generation team at Kuaishou, and the subject is a detector that separates AI-generated video from the real thing. This article is less interested in the detection technique than in the way the paper handed out its grades.

Each detector's result was written into two columns. Did it call real or fake correctly, and did it also point at the flaws a person had already marked in the video? VideoVeritas, an existing detector, took the first column at 91.12, the highest of any specialized detector in the comparison. Its second column reads 8.47. Under a score that counts verdicts alone, a model that looks at almost nothing and still answers correctly rises to the top.

Sections 1 through 3 follow what is written in the paper. Section 4, which lays this result over the place where data quality gets checked, is this article's own.

1

Does a Right Answer Mean the Model Found the Flaw?

Detectors for AI-generated video have long been judged by a single number, balanced accuracy. It takes the share of real videos called real and the share of fake videos called fake, works the two out separately and averages them, so that a lopsided mix of clips cannot inflate the result. Models are easy to line up against each other that way, and most detection papers rank their field by it.

The Verus-Insight paper set a second column beside it. That column is called Finding F1, and it measures whether a model also pointed at the places a person watching the video had marked as wrong. Put the two side by side and the ranking moves.

Model Type Verdict score

The two columns do not come from the same evaluation set. The verdict score is balanced accuracy on a set called RewardData, and the evidence score can only come out of material where a person marked each flaw by hand. Carrying the name of the column along with the number is the safer habit. Even so, read one model's row across and a fact stays put. VideoVeritas, the specialized detector with the highest verdict score, sits only just above the general-purpose models on evidence.

2

What Finding F1 Measures

People make the answer key behind the evidence score. An annotator watches a video, finds a spot that looks off, writes down a category and a description, and marks where on the screen and roughly when it shows up. A finger that gains a joint for one frame, a shadow that drifts away from the object casting it. There are 18 categories in all.

Those marks are collected in an evaluation set called Verus-Bench. It holds 192 videos, half of them filmed and half of them generated, 96 each. Attached to them are 326 flaws that people found and wrote down. The evidence score is graded against those 326.

Grading pairs the marks with whatever the model pointed out. The pairing runs on meaning rather than on wording, and the share of marked flaws the model found is folded together with the share of the model's remarks that landed on a real flaw to give an F1 value. A video carries somewhere between one and five flaws, so pointing anywhere and collecting credit is not an option.

A verdict score comes from answering the question, is this fake. An evidence score comes from answering the question, where is it wrong. The first question can be guessed at and half the guesses land. The second one leaves nothing to guess with.

In its opening figure the paper laid out two ways an existing detector comes apart. It answers real or fake wrongly, or it answers correctly while the flaw it offers as evidence sits somewhere else. One video with a mangled face, shown to four models, puts both of those in one place.

Model Verdict How it handled the flaw
BusterX++ Correct Failed to find the central flaw
Skyra Wrong (called real) Never inspected the stretch of time where the problem sits
VideoVeritas Wrong (called real) Looked at the spot but read it as ordinary styling
Verus-Insight Correct Inspected the region and recovered the flaw a person had marked

The verdict and the handling of the flaw are carried over directly from the paper's Figure 2.

The row that catches the eye here is BusterX++. It got the real-or-fake verdict right but failed to find the central flaw. On a scorecard with only a verdict column, that model is written down as "correct" in exactly the same shape as Verus-Insight, which actually recovered the flaw. VideoVeritas got the verdict itself wrong on this clip. The reason was not that it failed to reach the spot, but that it reached the spot and judged it to be fine. Both missed the flaw, but the way they missed it, and the score each one paid for it, are not the same.

3

How Did the Paper Make Evidence Count?

Verus-Insight does not say real or fake the moment a video arrives. It first sets out what to check and where, then goes item by item to see whether the flaw is actually there, and only at the end gathers what it confirmed into a verdict. With the verdict resting on a record of checks, the order is hard to run backward, settling the answer first and attaching an explanation afterward.

Training was arranged to teach that order. The model was first drilled on following regions through a video and drawing up the checks to run, and then on reproducing the whole stretch from description to checks to verification result to final call. Both of those stages are supervised learning, where it works from answers people had written out in advance.

That was apparently not enough. For the last stage of training the researchers mixed the evidence score straight into the reward. When the model is tuned with reinforcement learning, the reward does not stop at whether the verdict was right; on a generated video it is multiplied by how far the evidence lined up with the flaws people had marked. Inventing a flaw that is not there in a real video costs points. The shortcut of answering correctly and leaving the evidence blank was closed off while the model was still learning.

The material was gathered in the same direction. There are 8,411 training instances that people worked on, carrying 36,309 rubric checks, of which 11,745 confirm a flaw and 24,359 confirm that nothing is wrong. The clean side outnumbers the other by more than two to one. Train a model only to go find something odd and it will start producing flaws in footage that has none.

Set against the backbone, the result is sharp. Qwen3-VL-8B, the model Verus-Insight is built on, stayed at 3.38 on evidence while working the problem alone, and a model of the same size came out at 35.42 after going through this process. Against 18.08 from GPT-5.5, the best figure on the proprietary side, that is close to double.

The figure that remains deserves the same look. A score of 35.42 is only the highest in this comparison. On a scale where the model's flaws and the people's marks overlapping completely would read 100, it stands a little past one in three. On Verus-Bench, the set that carries the human annotations, the verdict score is 89.06, the same value GPT-5.5 reached. The contribution here sits closer to a scale that asks whether evidence was read at all than to a model that reads evidence well.

4

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Paper

Video detection is not what Pebblous does. Still, the hole this paper opened has the shape of one that turns up daily wherever data quality is handled. A data validation pipeline also tends to be reported as a single pass rate. The line that reads "97% passed" is tidy, and nothing inside that number says which column and which pattern the decision rested on.

What 91.12 and 8.47 say together is that the two can move apart. A correct verdict carries no guarantee that the checking was done, and a score that counts verdicts alone keeps no record of the difference. Three things are worth settling whenever a scorecard for an in-house model or validation tool arrives.

  • Whether a passing verdict comes with evidence a person can open. It only counts as evidence once it goes down to which record and which value tripped the check
  • Whether an answer key exists to check that evidence against. Something like the 326 annotations in Verus-Bench, a set marked by people first, has to be there even in small form before evidence can be graded
  • Whether the accuracy of the evidence enters the criteria for choosing a model. A metric that lands in a report and never reaches a decision does not survive long

The third is the part most worth taking from this paper. The researchers did not stop at measuring the evidence score; they put it into the training reward. Where the measuring and the choosing sit apart, a metric stays decorative.

Editor's Note

Grading evidence requires that people mark the answers first. What it cost to attach 326 flaws to 192 videos is not written in the paper, and without that work the Finding F1 column would never have existed. That the starting point for a new metric sat on the annotation side rather than the model side is worth noting.
