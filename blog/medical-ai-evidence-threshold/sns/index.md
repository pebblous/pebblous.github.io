사내 AI 지침을 한 번 배포했다면, 지금 그것을 안다고 답할 사람이 몇 %인지 세어 보는 것이 가장 싼 점검입니다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Weekly AI use among US doctors has roughly doubled in two years, from 38% to close to three in four. The share of clinicians who know how their own hospital handles AI moved from 21% to 27%.

Both figures come from a Wolters Kluwer survey of 355 US doctors and nurses, fielded by Ipsos in March. The Financial Times reported on September 20 that clinicians are pushing back on medical AI, and the pushback is not a demand to stop.

Skepticism concentrates past diagnosis and image reading. Finding something in a scan has a verifiable answer, which is how it travels into journals as sensitivity and specificity. Drafting the clinical note and suggesting a course of treatment has no answer key, and 44% of doctors already let AI draft their notes.

Deskilling ranks among the greatest AI risks for 74% of clinicians. Meanwhile 77% say they check AI answers against trusted sources. That check is a personal habit rather than an institutional rule, and it is the first thing to slip on a busy day.

Inside the 27% the ground narrows further. Among clinicians who knew their organization's policies, 35% also knew the guidelines for checking the accuracy of AI output. Across the whole clinician sample that works out to about one in ten.

This is why AI-Ready Data at Pebblous never points only at training data. What a tool cleared, and who measured that value when, has to sit somewhere the organization can still find it later.

If your company sent out AI guidance once, the cheapest audit available is to count how many people would now say they know it.

## Facebook (EN)

In an exam room I find myself waiting for the doctor to take their hands off the keyboard.

That wait is getting shorter, apparently. An AI now writes the first draft of the note.

A survey puts 44% of US doctors in that position already. Wolters Kluwer commissioned it from Ipsos, which polled 355 doctors and nurses in March.

On September 20 the Financial Times laid the clinicians' pushback on top of those numbers.

The pushback was not what I expected. Nobody is asking to stop.

What clinicians are asking about is the evidence threshold.

An AI that finds disease in a scan has a verifiable answer behind it, which is how sensitivity and specificity carried it through journals and review.

An AI that drafts the note, answers patient messages and suggests a course of treatment has no answer key. Clinicians do not even agree on what a good clinical note looks like.

The tools share a name and crossed very different thresholds.

The quietest number in the survey is 27%: the share of clinicians who say they know how their own organization handles AI.

While that figure climbed six points from 21%, the share of doctors reaching for AI every week doubled.

Go one layer inside the 27% and the ground narrows again. Among those clinicians, 35% also knew the guidelines for checking accuracy, which is about one in ten across everyone.

Deskilling ranks among the greatest AI risks for 74% of clinicians, and 77% said they check AI answers against trusted sources.

The line is being held by individual hands rather than by an institution.

"What did this tool clear to get here, and who is holding that evidence now?"

Those are the two questions Pebblous starts with on data quality. The tools have improved; if knowledge about them does not circulate inside the organization, what remains is a records problem rather than a performance problem.

None of this is particular to medicine. The same shape sits in hiring screens and contract review. What differs in the exam room is that the outcome translates straight into somebody's body.

Sending the guidance out leaves a trace in the organization's records. Knowing it does not.

Without counting, there is no telling where your own number falls between 21% and 27%.
