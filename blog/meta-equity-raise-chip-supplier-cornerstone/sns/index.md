## LinkedIn (EN)

Meta is selling $97.5 billion of stock to pay for AI facilities, and the three cornerstone buyers named in the filing all sell Meta its chips.

The October 26 filing lists Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom as cornerstone investors. A day later Nvidia put its figure at $3 billion and AMD at $1 billion; Broadcom has not given one. Both chipmakers announced expanded deployments at Meta the same day.

Money moved in two directions inside a single week. Meta pays the two companies for silicon, and the two companies pay Meta for shares. One camp calls the arrangement circular financing; the other calls it vendor financing.

The labels differ, the evidence does not. Investment amounts and deal sizes alike were written down by the parties to the transaction. The measures that would settle the question from outside, utilization and power draw, appear nowhere in the announcement.

## Facebook (EN)

I read the same three company names twice in one week.

Once on the list of firms Meta buys chips from. Once on the list of firms buying Meta's stock.

Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom.

They are the cornerstone investors Meta named in the filing announcing a $97.5 billion equity raise for AI facilities, and all three sell Meta chips and systems. The next day Nvidia said it would put in $3 billion and AMD $1 billion. Each announcement set out expanded deployments at Meta alongside the investment figure.

Critics of the arrangement call it "circular financing." Those who push back call it "vendor financing." The labels point to opposite conclusions. Yet the material both sides lay on the desk is identical: the investment amounts and the size of the supply deals were written down and released by the companies doing the deal.

Sorting the figures in this story by where they came from left me with three layers. Values priced from outside or already audited. Values written down by a party to the deal. And a layer with nothing in it. Most of the amounts that got the biggest headlines were sitting in the middle one.

What belongs in the top layer is utilization, power draw, revenue per token. That layer was empty.

So I started calling these "numbers nobody answers for."

"When this value turns out to be wrong, who answers?"

Behind a figure in a financial statement stand auditors and regulators. Behind a figure in a press release stands the company that wrote it. The two can sit in the same table, and the table will not tell them apart.

Pebblous sets a figure against the quality of AI training data with DataClinic. In data the same question arrives first. When a value a sensor recorded and a value a person typed in share one row, is there a column saying who answers if it is wrong?

Without that column a model trusts them equally. What an outsider can see, for now, is the amounts the parties sent each other.
