## LinkedIn (EN)

A Grok user in Illinois sued the company behind the chatbot on October 7, and the objection is not to the selfie that went up but to the distances calculated from it between eyes, nose and mouth.

Cesar Padilla had asked for something usable as a headshot and a few restyled images came back. The complaint says Grok automatically located and scanned that face along the way, extracting the proportions and spatial relationships among facial landmarks without written consent. The Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act, the law behind the claim, charges $1,000 for a negligent violation and $5,000 for one that is willful or reckless.

Read the statute alone and the claim looks like it should fail, since it expressly excludes photographs from the biometric identifiers it regulates. Courts have nonetheless read the photograph apart from the face geometry obtained by scanning it. The line falls on the scan rather than on the image.

None of which means Padilla wins. A suit brought against X Corp under the same law was dismissed in 2024, after the plaintiff failed to plead specifically that the image hashes at issue were scans of face geometry capable of identifying a person. All three suits now running against Grok are still at the pleading stage.

For anyone whose work is data, the story is less about Musk's AI company drawing another lawsuit. Landmark coordinates sit in memory for a few seconds and then they are gone, which is why most data inventories carry no name for them even though the code plainly does. ## Facebook (EN)

There was a week when I needed a headshot and had no time to sit for one, so I handed a selfie to a tool and let it do the work.

Erase the background, straighten the collar, move the face to the middle. A few seconds, and I closed the tab as soon as the image came back.

What got computed in those few seconds never crossed my mind.

Cesar Padilla, who lives in Illinois, did the same thing. A selfie went up to Grok with a request for something usable as a headshot, and a few restyled images came back.

The complaint filed in federal court on October 7 does not object to those images.

It objects to the numbers that passed through in between. How far the eyes sit from the nose, the mouth, the jawline, and in what proportion.

What to call those numbers is the whole of this case. I have started calling them the middle measurements.

They are not a photograph, so they never reach a retention list. They are not an output, so nothing puts them on a screen. They sit in memory for the few seconds a request takes and then they are gone.

Which is why "we do not build a face database" is usually a true sentence.

The Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act, written in 2008, expressly excludes photographs from the biometric identifiers it regulates. Courts have nonetheless read the photograph apart from the face geometry obtained by scanning it.

The line falls on the scan rather than on the image.

"Is a value that exists only in the middle and then disappears data we hold, or not?"
