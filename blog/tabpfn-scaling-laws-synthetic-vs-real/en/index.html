Executive Summary

Some models take a whole table at once and answer on the spot. There is no step where you retrain for each new dataset: feed the table in, and one forward pass returns the prediction. The TabPFN family works that way, and it is pretrained without a single real table. It learns only from the fake tables a generator invents. A paper posted to arXiv on November 18 measures from scratch how much that model improves when you make it bigger. It is the result of training 6,120 models of varying size and setup across roughly 80,500 GPU-hours.

On the fake-table side the formula held. A curve fitted on runs between 1013 and 1016 FLOPs predicted a validation loss of 0.769 at 1017 FLOPs, and the measured value was 0.770. Carry the same formula onto real data, though, and it slips. Negative log-likelihood was predicted at 0.289 and measured at 0.309. The miss is 0.020, and it runs one way only. The formula expected the model to be better than it turned out to be.

Sections 1 through 6 follow what the paper states and what we confirmed in the primary source. Section 7, which asks how anyone working with synthetic data should take this result, is this article's own reading.

1

A Model That Never Sees a Real Table

For a long time there was one way to work with tabular data. You train a model on the data you have, and when the data changes you train again. The TabPFN family reverses that order. Bundle the training rows and the rows you want predicted into one block, hand it to the model, and the model reads the pattern inside and hands back an answer. There is no training step. TabPFNv2, published in Nature in 2025, lifted this approach to something usable on small tables, and the name tabular foundation model stuck to it afterwards.

What makes the family unusual is its pretraining data. It does not collect real tables. A generator builds millions of tables by rule, and that is all the model ever sees. Measured by how far it leans on synthetic data, this is the furthest out of any model family in use today. So applying a scaling law to TabPFN raises one extra question. When you make it bigger, is it the fake-table score that improves, or the real-table score?

What gets measured is not TabPFNv2 itself but nanoTabPFN, open code that reimplements the architecture in simpler form. The pretraining tables are classification problems from the MLP generator in TabICLv1, and training and evaluation were run as one pipeline with TFM-Playground, a tool that already existed. The setup is built so a grid can be filled densely at sizes a research budget can afford. The authors also wrote down why they chose it: when the work began, few tabular foundation models had their training pipeline open as well. What the authors released this time is the scaling-law measurements and the code that redraws the figures. The smallest model holds 1,826 parameters and the largest 553,482, across twenty sizes.

Who did the measuring matters for reading the result. The seven authors list the University of Freiburg, TU Delft, the ELLIS Institute Tübingen, and Prior Labs. Prior Labs is the company that turns TabPFN into a product, and SAP acquired it in May 2026. Frank Hutter, named on both TabPFN papers, is a co-author. A report that the scaling law breaks on real data is coming from the side that builds and sells the model.

Eight conditions were varied across the grid. Alongside the training and architecture knobs, one entry stands out: the size of a single pretraining table is part of the search. How many rows and columns a table may hold sits on the same line as model size and learning rate.

The Formula Was Built on One Range and Tested on Another

The most common weakness in scaling-law work is using the data that built the formula to argue the formula fits. This paper split those two apart from the start. Runs at four budgets from 1013 to 1016 FLOPs, 6,075 of them, built the formula. The 45 runs between 1016.25 and 1017 FLOPs were held out, never seen during fitting, and used only to check whether the prediction lands.

Compute budget 10¹³ 10¹⁴ 10¹⁵ 10¹⁶ 10¹⁷
Training runs 4,050 1,651 288 86 45

▲ The first four columns, 6,075 runs, built the formula; the 45 runs in the 10¹⁷ column were used only to check extrapolation. Source: arXiv 2611.24214, Table 2

How those 45 runs were chosen has to be read alongside the number. They were not drawn from the grid above. They are configurations picked around what the formula prescribed, and they are not bound to the values in Table 1. So the question this test answers is not whether the formula holds for any configuration scaled up to 1017 FLOPs. It is whether a configuration chosen the way the formula says lands where the formula said it would. That is close to how a scaling law actually gets used, but it leaves standing the fact that the test subject was picked by the test instrument.

The fitting procedure runs like this. At each compute budget, only the best-scoring runs are kept, which traces a frontier. That line is not made of runs that wasted compute on badly chosen settings; it is the best that budget could buy. The following function is fitted to that line.

L(C) = A · C−α + L0

C is compute and L is loss. For a bounded metric such as ROC AUC, L is one minus the metric.

Why that subtraction is there shows up in earlier work. Scaling for tabular foundation models has been looked at before. TabDPT fitted a power law between model size and real pretraining data, LimiX looked at scale on the synthetic side, and MegaTabICL worked out model size and training length for a given compute. The point the authors single out, pointing at LimiX, is the shape of the function. A formula fitted without constraints can predict a value above 1 for a metric like ROC AUC that cannot exceed 1. Taking one minus the metric as the loss and holding the floor term L0 positive keeps the prediction improving with compute without passing the metric's ceiling.

Fitting minimises a Huber loss in log space, with 105 starting points each run through and the best one kept. How compute should be split between model size and data size was derived separately by least squares in log-log space, following the first approach Hoffmann and colleagues used in the Chinchilla paper, as the authors state.

Scores were measured three ways. Synthetic validation loss is measured on synthetic tables held out of training: 2,048 small tables of 500 to 1,000 rows and 5 to 50 columns, and 1,024 medium tables of 1,000 to 3,000 rows and 50 to 150 columns. Each group is averaged, and the two averages are then averaged again with equal weight into one number. The counts differ two to one, 2,048 against 1,024, but they carry the same weight in the score. Real-data scores were measured as negative log-likelihood and ROC AUC on 21 datasets drawn from the TabArena benchmark under a cut of 500 columns and 10,000 rows. That cut is the same range TabPFNv2 was designed to handle.

It helps to fix how differently these three behave before going further. Synthetic validation loss shows how well the model does inside the world it learned. Real-data NLL asks how close the probabilities it emits are to the true distribution. ROC AUC sets the probability values aside and asks only whether the ranking is right. As the sections below show, the three give different answers about where the next unit of compute should go.

The Synthetic Side Held, the Real Side Drifted

All three metrics fit the scaling law cleanly. The coefficients of determination run from 0.986 to 0.997. On the numbers alone you could say the TabPFNv2 family draws a predictable curve like any other model family. The trouble starts when that formula is carried out past the range it was fitted on.

Metric Measured at 10¹⁷ Predicted at 10¹⁷ Fit R² Mean extrapolation error
Synthetic validation loss 0.770 0.769 0.997 0.001
Real-data NLL 0.309 0.289 0.986 0.011
Real-data ROC AUC 0.877 0.873 0.991 0.004

▲ The fit uses the frontier up to 10¹⁶ FLOPs, and extrapolation was checked at four budgets from 1016.25 to 10¹⁷. Source: arXiv 2611.24214, Table 3

Synthetic validation loss came in at 0.770 against a prediction of 0.769. Ten times the compute further out, and the match holds to three decimal places. Judging on that metric alone, you could reasonably say the formula is good enough to plan an investment around.

Real-data NLL came in at 0.309 against a prediction of 0.289. That is 0.020 in absolute terms and a little over 6% of the measured value. It is a small-looking number, and two things make it hard to wave off. First, the miss runs one way. The formula expected the model to do better than it did. Second, the gap showed up as something that widens with compute. It stays invisible inside the fitting range and opens once the formula steps outside it.

ROC AUC came in at 0.877 against 0.873, a smaller miss than NLL. That metric, though, swings hardest somewhere else, which the sections below take up.

4

What Moved With Compute and What Did Not

The value of a scaling law is not in hitting a number. It is in deciding where the next budget goes. Make the model bigger, or push more data through it? Here the three metrics do not give the same answer.

4.1One Side Says Grow the Model, the Other Says Grow the Table

Synthetic validation loss asks for more parameters. Real-data scores rise further when the synthetic tables used for pretraining are made larger. For the same compute, one metric points at the model and the other points at the data. Allocate a budget on synthetic loss alone and you tilt toward model size, and nothing in this experiment guarantees that choice is the best one on real tables.

The authors do not tell anyone to pick one. Measured either way, the basic signal that bigger is better comes out the same, so what splits is not the signal but the prescription. Their recommendation is to measure both sides, let the synthetic formula hold the optimistic end and the real-data formula hold the conservative end, and run large-budget training somewhere between them.

Not every axis separated them. On how fast the rows and columns of the pretraining tables should grow with compute, synthetic validation loss and real-data NLL gave similar prescriptions. The two metrics have places where they split and places where they agree. The one that spoke differently was ROC AUC, which asked for rows and columns to grow faster.

Some things did not move with compute at all. The authors fitted a trend against compute for each of the eight varied conditions and the values derived from them, and two shape ratios showed no trend: embedding size divided by number of layers, and rows divided by columns within one table. As the budget grows, whether the model goes deeper or wider, and whether the table goes longer or wider, does not tip one way. Weight decay got no trend line at all, because the values sitting on the frontier include zero, as the paper notes. Parameter count and the number of cells the model saw, on the other hand, fit well at R² of 0.84 and above, and the only pairing that fell short was ROC AUC against parameter count.

4.2Table Size Is a Scaling Variable

The claim pushed hardest is that the size of the pretraining table must not be held fixed. Dataset size, the authors write, belongs on the scaling ladder. The comparison behind that is plain. A narrow setting that pinned tables to 4 columns and 100 rows, and a wide setting that pinned them to 16 columns and 400 rows, both failed to keep up with the version that grew table size along with compute. Setting it wide is not enough; the table has to grow as the budget grows. The wide setting named here, 16 columns and 400 rows, is the largest within this comparison. The maximum of the full search space sits elsewhere: Table 1 allows columns up to 16 and rows up to 1,200.

This runs against a common choice on the synthetic-data side. Generator settings are usually fixed once and then used to stamp out data. The volume grows, but the shape of a single table stays. If the result holds, adding volume and enlarging one table are different acts, and the second one weighs more on real-data scores.

4.3Where One Power Law Broke

The scaling law did not keep the same shape under every condition. In the comparison above, under the setting with the largest tables (16 columns, 400 rows), the ROC AUC frontier stayed flat for a stretch, climbed sharply at one point, and flattened again after. Neither a single power law nor a single logistic could cover both stretches. The authors compared several forms, a broken power law among them, and reported three: one logistic, a power law plus a logistic decay, and the sum of two logistics.

They chose the last, and fit error alone is not the reason. Root-mean-square error against the 19 frontier observations was lowest for the two logistics at 0.0038, while the backtest error, fitting on the early part and predicting the rest, was better for the power law with decay at 0.004. The decision turned somewhere else. The power law with decay pushes the curve of this deliberately restricted setting up to 0.030 above the overall curve chosen with no restriction at all. A prediction in which the narrowed option beats the unnarrowed one is hard to accept. For the two logistics that figure was −0.002, staying under the overall curve. The form was chosen not on how well it fits but on whether it makes sense outside the fit. It was used in that comparison only.

That the functional form had to change is itself a result. Scaling laws are usually built by assuming a power law and solving for the coefficients. Change the conditions, and that assumption broke. Same model, same generator, one metric apart, and the curve took a different shape. It is why a report that the formula fit well should be met with a question about which condition it fit under, and in what shape.

5

How Many Evaluation Datasets Make a Result Trustworthy

The part closest to day-to-day practice is not the scaling law but the evaluation. The question the authors ask is simple. Does a configuration picked on a handful of evaluation datasets do just as well on datasets that were not used to pick it?

The check starts by hiding one of the 21 datasets and drawing n from the remaining 20. The configuration with the best average score on those n is picked at each compute budget, and that configuration is then scored on the hidden one. The draw is repeated 20 times, and the hidden dataset rotates through all 21. The gap between the score at selection time and the score on the held-out dataset is the selection bias, and that gap is compared against the residual of the scaling-law fit itself. Only once it falls below the residual can the gap be called buried in measurement noise.

The answer differed by metric. NLL dropped below the residual at 8 datasets. ROC AUC took 20. Same model, same experiment, same pool of datasets, and the point at which it becomes trustworthy sits two and a half times apart. It reads as a sign that a metric watching only rank is pushed around by between-dataset variance more than a metric watching probability values.

One more thing belongs with that 20. Twenty is also the largest value this experiment could test. With one of 21 hidden and n drawn from the remaining 20, the ceiling for n is 20, and the values the authors actually tested were 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 16 and 20. The ROC AUC result is better read as having only just reached the mark at the end of the range than as a statement that 20 datasets are enough. How many more it would have taken cannot be known inside this design.

Where this result meets the numbers in Section 3 is what matters. The coefficient of determination for synthetic validation loss was 0.997. The curve sat almost perfectly on the data. Yet when few evaluation datasets are used, the configuration that perfectly fitted curve points to is not that good on outside data. That a fit went well and that its conclusion can be trusted are two separate statements, and that is why the word reliable is in the title.

6

The Comparison This Paper Leaves Out

There is one comparison anyone working with synthetic data wants most. Is it better to add more synthetic tables, or to add the same amount of real ones? The paper does not have it. The 21 real datasets were used for evaluation from beginning to end, and not one of them entered pretraining.

The closest result is the prescription in Section 4.1. Real-data scores rose further when the synthetic pretraining tables were made larger. But that too is a comparison inside synthetic data. What happens once real tables are mixed in is not something this experiment answers. Reading it as evidence that growing synthetic tables beats adding real ones puts words in the paper that are not there.

The scope is narrow as well. One architecture, nanoTabPFN. One generator, the MLP prior from TabICLv1. Classification only. Even the largest model holds a little over 550,000 parameters, far smaller than the TabPFNv2 that actually ships. Whether the coefficients found here carry over to larger models is not something the paper shows. The authors themselves left putting the generator into the function as a variable to future work.

7

Why Pebblous Is Watching This

Organisations working with synthetic data almost all end up standing in the same place. More data can be made, but whether making more actually helped is hard to confirm. Confirming it needs real data, and if real data had been plentiful there would have been less reason to make synthetic data in the first place. This paper is a rare case of putting numbers on that awkward place.

What is worth looking at is not the size of the miss but its shape. The synthetic curve was nearly perfect. A coefficient of determination of 0.997 and an extrapolation error of 0.001 are a good fit by any standard. That good fit read real-data scores as 0.020 better than they were. The intuition that a clean synthetic metric means the real side is probably fine breaks right here. Synthetic loss measures how well the generator was imitated, and how closely that generator resembles reality has to be measured somewhere else.

The evaluation result is more direct. A configuration picked on fewer than eight datasets reports inflated performance by that very act of picking. Report in ROC AUC and the number is twenty. How many validation datasets a model's performance is confirmed on is a number most organisations could stand to count once, and counting usually turns up fewer.

When a scorecard arrives for a model trained or evaluated on synthetic data, three things are worth checking.

  • • Which side of the data produced that score. If it is a loss measured on generator-made data, it is a number about how well the generator was copied. In this paper the distance between the two sides showed up as a fit of 0.997 and a 0.020 NLL underestimate
  • • How many real datasets confirmed it. Too few, and the number leans good. In this experiment the point where that lean is buried in measurement noise was 8 datasets for NLL and 20 for ROC AUC
  • • Whether the volume grew or a single instance grew. What moved real-data scores was the rows and columns of the pretraining tables. Fixing the generator settings and adding volume alone never touches that axis

Editor's Note

What stayed with me through the paper is how much room Section 4.3 takes up in the body. The selection-bias experiment has no direct bearing on the scaling law, and the paper stands without it. The authors put it in anyway and set the word reliable in the title. Their own formula fits at 0.997, and they still wrote down that the number alone is not enough. That said, their own evaluation set is also 21 datasets, barely clearing its own ROC AUC bar of 20. Running the same experiment with a different generator, on the released code and tools, is still out there to do.
