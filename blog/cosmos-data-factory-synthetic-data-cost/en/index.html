Executive Summary

NVIDIA has released Cosmos Data Factory, a toolkit for generating synthetic training data for robots and self-driving cars, and says it produces autonomous-driving scenes about 45 times more cheaply than collecting them on the road. According to the announcement on NVIDIA's developer blog on November 18, the toolkit consists of five blueprints, which are finished end-to-end workflows, and six skills, which are the shared building blocks. NVIDIA says it cuts the time needed to assemble a dataset by up to 60 times.

The 45x figure compares a GPU compute cost of $2.66 per synthetic clip with $125 for road collection. But the method NVIDIA itself describes is to divide a daily server cost of $960 by daily output. The autonomous-driving blueprint produces 130 clips a day, which works out to $7.38 per clip. Compared on that basis, the saving is a little over 17x. Three other blueprints in the same announcement come back to $960 a day under this method; only autonomous driving does not. NVIDIA notes that the figures cover compute alone and leave out data preparation and human review.

The only performance evidence in the announcement is a case from ANYbotics, which makes industrial inspection robots. In the original source, a post by Encord, NVIDIA's figure of recall improved by up to 24% comes from scenes where a new object appears. The 1,400x return that NVIDIA pairs with it comes from liquid-spill scenes, where recall rose 8%. The case used synthetic clips to calibrate the decision threshold of an existing anomaly detector, and false alarms on normal scenes rose as well. Nowhere does the announcement compare a model trained on synthetic data with one trained on real data. Sections 1 to 5 set out verified facts and the companies' claims; section 6 is Pebblous's interpretation.

NVIDIA Releases Cosmos Data Factory With Five Blueprints and Six Skills

On November 18, 2026, NVIDIA published a post on its developer blog titled "Build Training-Ready Physical AI Datasets with Two Prompts in NVIDIA Cosmos." The author is Nakul Arora, a director of product management for NVIDIA Metropolis. As the title says, the announcement's central claim is that two prompts are enough to produce a training dataset for physical AI. Physical AI refers to AI that moves and makes decisions in real space, such as robots, self-driving cars and factory inspection cameras.

Cosmos Data Factory has two layers. The lower layer holds six skills: Auto Labeling, AnomalyGen (anomaly scene generation), Augmentation, Curation and Retrieval, Simulation, and Orchestration. The upper layer holds five blueprints, each of which strings skills together to carry one job from start to finish: autonomous-driving dataset generation, image attribute augmentation, video data augmentation, event video generation, and defect image generation. All five blueprints run on top of the Orchestration skill.

The code is public in the GitHub repository NVIDIA/cosmos-data-factory. The code is licensed under Apache-2.0 and the documentation and skill content under CC-BY-4.0, and the repository states that it is not accepting outside contributions for now. It lists the number of GPUs each blueprint needs: the autonomous-driving blueprint needs four with local models and does not support connecting to an external model server. The defect image blueprint was built around images of PCBs, metal and glass parts. Cosmos Evaluator, which checks generated video for physical accuracy, object consistency and hallucination, runs alongside by default.

The announcement gives a daily output and a unit price for each blueprint. NVIDIA's figures are as follows.

Each row of the table is compared against something different. Autonomous driving is set against the cost of a road collection team, the two augmentation blueprints against the average price of purchased data, and defect images against time rather than money. Event video generation has a unit price and nothing to compare it with. The numbers in the multiplier column come from different baselines, so they cannot be lined up and ranked against one another.

Image attribute augmentation ~42,649 pairs $0.023 per pair Purchased data, $0.25 average ~10x

The announcement also includes four customer stories. Siemens aims to cut the manual work of building automated optical inspection programs by up to 85% and to reduce misjudgments by 40%. Volkswagen expects to cut repeated physical test drives by up to 90% and its AI regression-testing cycle by up to 85%. Centific says it shipped in under two weeks with Cosmos Curator what would have taken six months to build in-house. NVIDIA labels the Siemens figures with "Targeting" and the Volkswagen figures with "(expected)," so they cannot be read as measured results. The only measured result is the fourth, from ANYbotics, which section 4 covers separately.

Cosmos Data Factory's 60x Comes From Defect Images, Its 45x From Driving

The two headline numbers of NVIDIA's announcement are up to 60x faster and 45x cheaper. They come from different blueprints. The 60x is a case in which the defect image blueprint cut dataset assembly from two months to one day. The 45x comes from the clip price of the autonomous-driving blueprint.

The other blueprints report far smaller multipliers. Image attribute augmentation comes in at about 10x and video data augmentation at about 7x, and event video generation offers no comparison at all. The defect image blueprint that produced the 60x, for its part, discloses neither daily output nor unit price. The headline pairs the single largest number from each of two of the five blueprints.

The announcement also leaves open whether the two terms of the 45x share a unit. The synthetic side is the compute cost of one clip; the road side is what a collection team spends to gather one scenario. The announcement does not say how many training clips one road scenario yields, or whether one synthetic clip does the work of one road scenario. Nor does it say under what conditions the $125 collection-team cost was estimated.

NVIDIA's $2.66 per Clip Becomes $7.38 Under Its Own Method

NVIDIA explains its pricing in a single sentence. It measured daily output on a cloud server with eight RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs, priced the server at about $40 an hour or $960 a day, and estimated cost per asset by dividing the daily cost by daily output.

Running that method backwards for each blueprint gives split results. Daily output multiplied by unit price should equal the daily cost. For image attribute augmentation, 42,649 pairs at $0.023 comes to about $981. For video data augmentation, 305 clips at $3.15 comes to about $961. For event video generation, 384 clips at $2.50 is $960. All three match the server's daily cost. For image attribute augmentation, the announcement even states "$960/day" in the same line.

Only the autonomous-driving blueprint fails to match. 130 clips at $2.66 comes to about $346, a little over a third of the $960 daily server cost. The chart below recomputes the daily cost of four blueprints from the announced figures.

Applying NVIDIA's stated method to the autonomous-driving blueprint as written, $960 a day divided by 130 clips gives about $7.38 per clip. Against $125 for road collection, that is about 17x. The GitHub repository says the blueprint needs four GPUs; even assuming it used only half the server, the result is $480 a day, about $3.69 per clip and about 34x. Neither calculation yields $2.66 or 45x. The $346 that $2.66 times 130 clips implies corresponds to running the server for roughly 8.6 hours, but the announcement does not explain whether this one blueprint used a different basis.

NVIDIA itself states what the price leaves out. The announcement contains this sentence.

Note that these figures cover compute only; source data preparation, setup, agent use, storage, and human review may add costs.

NVIDIA Technical Blog, November 18, 2026

The $125 on the road side is the cost of running a collection team. A cost that includes human labor and a cost that excludes it end up in a single ratio. Before a synthetic clip can be used for training, someone has to check what was generated, and that review cost is not in the per-clip price.

4

ANYbotics' 24% Recall Gain, Checked Against Encord's Own Table

The performance evidence in NVIDIA's announcement is the ANYbotics case, which links to Encord as its source. NVIDIA did not measure it. It restates a post that the data platform company Encord published on October 21, 2026, under the name of its research group, Encord Data Labs. Encord's post says the results come from applying the event video generation capability to ANYbotics' industrial site images before the feature's official launch at GTC Berlin 2026. A figure caption dates the evaluation to September 25, 2026.

4.1Where ANYbotics Used the Synthetic Clips

ANYbotics' four-legged inspection robot, ANYmal, works in oil and gas facilities, power plants and factories, places where routine human patrols are dangerous or remote. On each patrol the robot returns to the same inspection points and takes photos, and a change detection model compares each photo with the usual distribution of photos for that point, flagging an anomaly when it falls outside. According to Encord, a well-run site has so few anomalies that a robot can patrol for months without meeting one, so the threshold for what counts as an anomaly is often set with no real anomaly examples at all.

The Encord team produced more than 1,500 clips across nine change types from 410 reference images. ANYbotics extracted several frames from each clip and used them to calibrate the decision threshold of its change detection model. The model was not trained from scratch on synthetic data; an existing model's baseline was adjusted with synthetic anomaly scenes.

4.2The Recall Table

The evaluation compared a threshold set from normal photos alone with a threshold set from normal photos plus synthetic frames, scoring both against real anomaly cases at the same inspection points. The metric is recall: how many of the real anomalies the model caught rather than missed.

Change type Inspection points Normal photos only With synthetic clips Relative change
Object appearance 23 0.54 0.67 +24%
Liquid spill 9 0.47 0.50 +8%
Overall 25 0.50 0.62 +24%

▲ Mean recall on real anomaly cases. Relative change as labeled in Encord's Figure 2. ANYbotics evaluation, September 25, 2026. Source: Encord Data Labs

The 24% is a relative change. Recall for object appearance rose from 0.54 to 0.67, a gain of 13 percentage points, and overall recall rose from 0.50 to 0.62, a gain of 12 points. Liquid-spill recall rose from 0.47 to 0.50, or 3 points. Even with the synthetic clips added, overall recall stands at 0.62, so the model still misses close to four in ten real anomalies. Scoring used 25 inspection points in total, nine of them for liquid spills.

The summary at the top of Encord's post says liquid-spill detection improved by 10%. The body uses a different metric for that. It says detection accuracy rose 10% for both object appearance and liquid spills, while recall rose 24% and 8% respectively. The summary picked recall for object appearance and accuracy for liquid spills. The post also discloses how many examples went into calibration. For object appearance, 525 synthetic examples were added to 237 real anomalies, an increase of 221%. For liquid spills, 92 synthetic examples were added to 205 real ones, an increase of 45%.

4.3The Limits ANYbotics Reported

Encord's post also records side effects. Calibrating with synthetic anomaly scenes raised the rate of false alarms on normal scenes, and some change types did not improve meaningfully. The Encord team hypothesized that augmenting normal photos with changes in lighting, angle and blur could narrow the gap, but published no test of that idea.

Encord reports ANYbotics' assessment in these words.

ANYbotics assessed that real anomalies remain stronger calibration data where they exist, but since they are sparse and unknown in advance, synthetic data gives a meaningful head start on change types with few or no real examples.

Encord Data Labs, October 21, 2026

NVIDIA's 24% and 1,400x Belong to Different Anomaly Types in Encord's Post

NVIDIA's announcement packs the ANYbotics case into one sentence: synthetic data raised recall on real anomalies by up to 24%, with an estimated 1,400x return on data generation cost at offshore oil and gas facilities. In Encord's original post, the two numbers are attached to different change types.

Encord calculated a return only for liquid spills. The synthetic clips that raised liquid-spill recall by 8% cost $55 to produce, about 60 cents a clip. ANYbotics then valued catching 8% more liquid spills by site type: $22,800 a year at a chemical plant, or 415 times the generation cost; $31,400 at a hydropower plant, 571 times; and $78,500 at an offshore oil and gas facility, 1,427 times. These are ANYbotics' estimates of avoided equipment damage, downtime, safety incidents and environmental fines, converted from Swiss francs to dollars. For object appearance, where recall rose 24%, neither a generation cost nor a value was published.

In its own summary, Encord picked the most conservative figure, the chemical plant, and described the value as more than $22,000 per site a year, over 400 times the generation cost. NVIDIA's announcement chose the largest of the three sites, the offshore facility at 1,427x, and placed it next to the 24% for object appearance and the overall score instead of the 8% for liquid spills.

The cost basis differs from NVIDIA's too. Encord's figure caption says the $55 is compute cost plus an allocated share of setup cost. NVIDIA's blueprint prices cover compute alone. The same event video generation capability is priced at about 60 cents a clip in the Encord case and $2.50 a clip in NVIDIA's announcement. Clip length and cost scope are not disclosed, so the two prices cannot be compared directly.

The timing also stands out. The ANYbotics evaluation ran on September 25, Encord published on October 21, and NVIDIA announced on November 18. The only measured case in NVIDIA's announcement is an evaluation that ended nearly two months earlier.

Why Pebblous Is Watching This

With the Cosmos Data Factory announcement, synthetic data has started to carry a per-clip price. A price list makes buying decisions easier, but what it covers is only the cost of a GPU turning out clips. Whether those clips fill the scenes our model actually lacks, and whether they break something else in the process, is not part of the price. Cosmos Evaluator, which NVIDIA attaches by default, checks whether a clip is physically plausible; it does not measure whether the clip filled a gap in the model.

The ANYbotics case shows what that measurement should look like. It scored the model before and after adding synthetic clips against real anomalies at the same inspection points, and it reported the unfavorable result, the rise in false alarms, alongside the rest. The gain varied by change type and was only 3 percentage points for liquid spills. The value of precisely targeted synthetic data is set only after measurements like these.

Anyone bringing in a synthetic data generation tool should check three things.

  • • Can the unit price be recomputed for your own server setup? If daily output and server cost are both published, multiply them. For the Cosmos Data Factory autonomous-driving blueprint, the product does not match the server's daily cost
  • • Has the human time for review been budgeted separately? Putting a compute-only price and a collection cost that includes people into one ratio inflates the multiplier
  • • Are there results scored on real data? Ask for the model with synthetic clips re-measured on real cases, including the metrics that got worse, such as false alarms

Editor's Note

$7.38 and 17x are Pebblous calculations from the server cost and daily output given in NVIDIA's announcement. NVIDIA may have used a different server configuration or running time for the autonomous-driving blueprint alone, in which case $2.66 could be correct. Neither the announcement nor the GitHub repository explains this, so we could not confirm it. The ANYbotics figures come from the table and text of Encord's original post; the underlying evaluation data has not been released.
