Executive Summary

The Federal Communications Commission has given SpaceX authority to operate 1 million satellites in Starmind, its orbital data center constellation. The order issued by the FCC's Space Bureau on November 25 calls it the first authorization of a space data center constellation in U.S. history. Six orbital shells sit between 500 and 1,002 km, and the grant goes null and void if the first satellite is not in orbit and operating by November 2033 or if the full million is not deployed by November 2040.

Three petitions to deny, from Amazon's Kuiper Systems, from Viasat and from the American Astronomical Society, were all denied. The order carries its own section headed with issues beyond the Commission's authority, and the effect on optical astronomy is disposed of there. The reasons given are that the Commission's rules do not address the protection of optical astronomy and that SpaceX's stated commitment to coordinate with the National Science Foundation is enough. Of the eleven conditions attached to the authorization, not one protects astronomical observation.

The cost that was set aside already exists in numbers. A paper released in October simulated a million-satellite orbital data center constellation against a year of observing at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory and found that on some nights up to 80% of the pointings carried a satellite streak. Sections 1 through 4 follow what the order and the paper say. Section 5, which asks who writes this cost into which ledger, is this article's own reading.

1

The grant covers radio and nothing else

The authorization at the front of the order states its scope in a single line: radio frequency authority for backup and emergency telemetry, tracking and command for a constellation of 1 million satellites operated as data centers. The link Starmind actually works over is an optical one with Starlink satellites, and radio is used only when that optical link drops. The assigned bands are 18.8–19.3 GHz for the downlink and 28.6–29.1 GHz for the uplink, on an unprotected, non-interference basis.

So this grant has a slightly different shape from a blanket permission to launch a million satellites. The handle the Commission holds in law is radio. The size of the constellation, the brightness of the satellites and the waste heat they radiate do not catch on that handle. The phrase "beyond the Commission's authority" that appears in Section 2 comes from here.

There are six shells. Each is about 50 km wide, and the inclinations split between roughly 30 degrees and sun-synchronous. A sun-synchronous orbit is set so that a satellite passes over the same point at the same local time every day, often tracking the line where the sun rises and sets. That property returns in Section 3.

There are four milestone conditions. One satellite must be in orbit and operating by November 25, 2033, 10% must be deployed by November 25, 2035, 50% by November 25, 2038, and the remainder by November 25, 2040. Missing any one of them makes the authorization null and void. SpaceX's original application asked for a waiver of these deployment milestones outright, then changed the request in October to apply the new schedule the Commission adopted this July, and that is what it got. The license term runs 15 years from the day the company certifies that the first Starmind satellite is in place. The launch vehicle is Starship, designed for full reuse, and space.com reports that the first launch is targeted for the third quarter of 2027.

2

Why were all three petitions denied?

SpaceX filed the application on January 30, and the Commission put it on public notice on February 4. On March 6, three petitions to deny arrived on the same day, from Amazon's Kuiper Systems, from the satellite operator Viasat and from the American Astronomical Society. Viasat raised radio interference, orbital debris and whether SpaceX has the capacity to run several constellations at once. Amazon added its opposition to the deployment milestone waiver. The astronomers raised brightness, radio interference and waste heat. SpaceX answered all three together ten days later, and the ordering clauses at the end of the order deny all three.

The interference arguments were decided on the merits. The Commission found it unlikely that the optical links would drop across all million satellites at once, and considered the matter resolved by conditions requiring unprotected, non-interference operation and coordination with federal agencies. One argument on the radio side was set aside rather than decided. The Square Kilometre Array Observatory wrote that the surfaces of a million satellites would themselves reflect radio emissions back and interfere with radio telescopes, and the Commission answered that the comment was not directed at the radio operations SpaceX had applied for and that no technical basis was offered for the reflection claim. That is the only place in the order where it asks anyone to come back with evidence.

Debris split by altitude, and that outcome is Section 4. The remaining arguments moved into a section headed with issues beyond the FCC's authority. That section runs in three branches.

2.1Environmental review: space is outside U.S. territory

On October 1 the Commission adopted an order revising its rules under the environmental policy act. That order concluded that space operations licensed by the FCC are extraterritorial activity and therefore not a major federal action, and that a spectrum license does not create federal control over or responsibility for those operations. So no environmental review is required here either. Even if there were jurisdiction, the order adds, the environmental effects of rocket launches are already the Federal Aviation Administration's to examine, and the atmospheric effects of satellites burning up on reentry are speculation with no support in the record.

2.2Optical astronomy: the rules have no such item

Optical astronomy is settled in two sentences. One is that SpaceX stated in its application and supplements that it will coordinate with the National Science Foundation, which sufficiently addresses the concerns in the record. The other is that the Commission's rules do not address the protection of optical astronomy. The second sentence is carried over word for word from the Reflect Orbital decision this July. The request from the astronomers and others to attach additional conditions was refused. On the society's other argument, that the waste heat from a million satellites would raise the infrared background of the night sky, the order wrote that this is an effect not arising from radio operations and therefore outside the scope.

Comments worried about orbital congestion were settled the same way. The Commission's rules set no cap on the number of satellites sharing similar orbital parameters, and the Space Bureau wrote that it would not create what amounts to a new rule in an individual licensing proceeding. Comments doubting whether orbital data centers are technically feasible, whether they fall short of ground data centers in efficiency and whether they make economic sense were turned aside as asking the government to substitute its own judgment for the business case.

The astronomical society said in a statement the next day that it was disheartened and dismayed. Repeating its earlier argument that decades of federally funded astronomical facilities lose their value and that researchers and the public lose the ability to see the night sky, it pointed out that no formal obligation to coordinate with the National Science Foundation was written into this authorization. Open the authorization and that reading holds. The eleven conditions cover frequency interference, coordination with federal agencies, the deferred debris finding, the ITU filing, semiannual reporting, four granted waivers, the deployment milestones and the license term. Astronomical observation appears in none of them.

The Commission's answer sits in a footnote rather than in a condition. The order states that it coordinated this authorization through the National Telecommunications and Information Administration with NASA, NOAA and the federal astronomy agencies, so the comments asking for formal consultation with the National Science Foundation and others are already satisfied. The two statements do not contradict each other. The consultation happened once, before the grant, and what the society says is missing is an obligation that would survive after it.

Take the sentences of the dismissal apart and they do not say there is no such harm. They come closer to saying this is not the place to weigh that harm. The two look alike in result and differ in kind. If it were the first, accumulating data would eventually overturn it. If it is the second, no amount of data opens the question inside this proceeding. And where that place is, the order says nowhere.

3

The dismissed cost has already been measured

On October 22, a month before the grant, a paper that put numbers on what orbital data centers do to ground observation went up on arXiv. It is titled "The Impact of Orbital Data Centers on the NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory," and five authors wrote it. The Rubin Observatory, built in Chile by the National Science Foundation and the Department of Energy, has just begun a survey that photographs the whole sky over and over for ten years. Because the method is to image the same sky many times and catch what changed, the share of exposures that are ruined converts directly into lost science.

The authors assumed roughly a million satellites placed in two low-Earth regions and two sun-synchronous regions, following the most recently filed application, and ran a full year of the observatory's own observing schedule through it. The pipeline works in six steps: orbit propagation, field-of-view matching, observer-frame geometry, a brightness model, a surface brightness calculation and a pixel loss estimate.

Brightness does not gather at one value but breaks into several peaks. When the solar panels enter an angle that throws sunlight back like a mirror, the satellites brighten to magnitude 2 to 4, and in ordinary diffuse reflection they sit around magnitude 6. A smaller magnitude means brighter, so the first figure is the level of a bright star visible to the naked eye. The paper also notes that satellites in sun-synchronous orbits produce a disproportionate share of the contamination because of the illumination geometry. Three of the six shells in this authorization carry sun-synchronous inclinations.

The paper counted brightness by occurrence as well. In the one-year simulation, about 9 million sunlit streaks crossing the field of view were brighter than magnitude 12.5, and 83.9% of those were brighter than magnitude 7. Magnitude 7 is the limit the International Astronomical Union recommends for satellite brightness. More than 229,000 streaks came in brighter than magnitude 2. They did not cross a limit because no limit existed. A recommendation already exists, written as a number, and there is no box on the licensing side to copy that number into.

The conditions have to travel with the numbers. What the paper simulated were two low-inclination groups and two sun-synchronous groups spanning 686 to 1,002 km, which is exactly the four shells above 600 km among the six authorized here. The two lower shells at 550–585 km are not in the model. Satellite brightness is not a published design value either but a modeled one, and that model assumed 400 kW per satellite and 110 m² per radiator face. The wider the solar panels, the more reflection, so that assumption drives the result heavily. It is a preprint that has not been peer reviewed, and the paper itself states that it does not treat radio astronomy, infrared observation or orbital debris. The finding that widespread contamination continues year-round even at 10% of the plan, though, does not lean much on the scale assumption.

The order declined to address optical astronomy without mentioning this paper, and that silence is not a rebuttal. It follows from treating the subject as something it was never going to look at.

4

The four shells above 600 km still await review

The cover of the order reads granted in part, deferred in part. What was deferred is debris disposal. The Commission found SpaceX's debris mitigation plan compliant with the rules only for satellites operating below 600 km. As the table in Section 1 shows, four of the six shells sit above 600 km, and before deploying satellites at those altitudes SpaceX has to file a separate modification application carrying a final plan that answers every element of the rule. The altitude band the paper in Section 3 simulated is these four shells.

The FCC press release issued the same day states in one line that the system also operates consistent with the Commission's orbital debris rules. Open the order and the range that finding covers is below 600 km.

The deferred waiver request reads like this. The Commission's rules require satellites that finish their mission below 2,000 km to be disposed of through atmospheric reentry. SpaceX asked to be allowed to push satellites operated above 600 km into an Earth disposal orbit or a heliocentric orbit instead. The Commission put the decision off, saying SpaceX had not provided enough information to judge whether that alternative disposal method meets the rules. To operate satellites destined for disposal by any route other than reentry, the company has to submit a modification application and a fresh waiver justification. The operating sequence SpaceX described meshes with this boundary: deploy first at the lower altitudes and test that the satellites work, then raise them to the higher altitudes where they stay longer, and design satellites that burn up completely in the atmosphere, guided down over open ocean when the mission ends. The Commission found that these materials and commitments resolve the debris concerns in the record.

In their place came a semiannual reporting obligation. Reports are due every January 1 and July 1, covering the preceding six months. There are five boxes, a through e, but the first one asks two things, so there are six counts to make: how many conjunction events occurred, how many of those led to a maneuver or to coordination with another operator, how many satellites were taken out of operation or had further deployment held, how many reentered the atmosphere, the total deployed and the orbital parameters at the end of the period, and how many satellites communicated with ground stations in the Ka band. There is no box here for brightness or observational interference.

5

Why Pebblous is watching this

Moving compute into orbit solves two things that are hard to solve on the ground. The sunlight does not stop, and there is no fight with residents over a site. Those are the two advantages Musk has named for computing in space. In exchange, noise rises in the observational data that ground telescopes collect. It is a trade that cuts the quality of the data feeding science in order to get the data feeding AI.

What this decision exposed is not the trade but the ledger. The business side of the ledger has numbers in it. Space.com reports that SpaceX put its long-term total addressable market at $28.5 trillion, with enterprise AI applications accounting for close to $23 trillion of that. The cost on the other side has numbers too: 20% of exposures, 5 to 10% of pixels. Both sides have numbers, and there is no box in any ledger to write the second one into.

A cost with no owner gets counted as zero. A regulator manages only the items it has decided to count, and that list of items is the ledger. In this authorization the list is the six counts in the semiannual report. Conjunction events and reentries are counted. How much brighter the sky has become is not. So if someone sets out to tally the loss ten years from now, there is no official record to consult. Filling that gap rests, for the moment, on voluntary calculations by researchers like the ones behind the Rubin Observatory paper.

It does not end with this case either. The last paragraph of the FCC press release states that other orbital data center applications remain under review and that the Commission will act on them promptly under the same process. The dismissal reasoning used here is not a judgment applied once to one company. It is a frame that carries into the decisions that follow.

This structure transfers straight into a company. A new pipeline one team ships cutting the dataset quality another team depends on is a common situation. Three things are worth checking.

  • • Is there a metric that measures the loss? With no metric, the loss is tallied as something that did not happen. Absent and uncounted have the same shape in a report
  • • Is the side that measures the same organization as the side that carries the cost? If they differ, the side measuring has no authority to change anything, and the side deciding has no reason to look at the number
  • • Which items go into the regular report? Quality that is not a reporting item is not a managed thing. It survives as an anecdote

Editor's Note

The subtitle of the FCC press release says Starmind satellites will support America's booming super intelligence economy. Chairman Carr's statement uses SI, super intelligence, rather than AI. The body of the order renders the same thing as American leadership in artificial intelligence. Between the two words used for one decision, the scientific loss that has already been calculated made it into neither sentence. This article was written from the FCC order and press release and from the body of the paper, all read directly. The original statement from the American Astronomical Society could not be opened because access was blocked, and its wording is carried only as far as press reports confirm. Several news reports describe the power and compute specification of a single satellite, but none of that is confirmed in a primary document and none of it is written here. The 400 kW in Section 3 is not a design value stated by SpaceX but an assumption the paper made in order to calculate brightness.
