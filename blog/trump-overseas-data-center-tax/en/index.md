# Trump

_At a White House briefing on November 20 the president said he would not allow US companies to build data centers abroad, and added that he had not yet told his own staff_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> Trump says he will not let American companies build data centers in other countries. At a [White House press conference on November 20](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/20/trump-data-centers-ai-companies-tax.html), he said that any company insisting on building abroad would face "a tremendous tax," and gave as his reason that China would end up taking those facilities. He named one project outright: the site Google is building in Finland.

> Nothing that would actually make it a tax followed. No rate, no start date, no statute to rest it on. He also mentioned that he had not told his own staff about it beforehand. Under the US Constitution the power to lay and collect taxes belongs to Congress, and a president cannot create a new tax by himself. Shares in Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon all closed lower the same day regardless.

> Sections 1 through 3 set out what the reporting confirms. Section 4, which asks what changes once the location of a data center is decided by tax rather than by engineering, is this article's own reading.

## The Tax Threat Surfaced First at a White House Briefing

Back from a trip to Asia, Trump turned to data centers at a Friday press conference at the White House. He said he would not allow American companies to build them in other countries, and that anyone who went ahead anyway would pay for it. His words were: "We're going to tax them tremendously. If they're going to do that, we want them to build here."

The reason he gave was China. Facilities built abroad, in his telling, end up in Chinese hands sooner or later. He reached for semiconductors as the parallel: once production moved offshore, the United States began to fall behind.

He also named a project. Google announced in September that it would spend 13 billion euros on AI infrastructure in Finland, its largest single investment in Europe. Trump said the company was looking to build one in Finland right now, and that he would not let it happen.

> [!callout]
> The line that says most about the nature of the remarks is one he added himself. "I haven't even said that to my people. I'm saying it now, because you're here." This was not a plan worked through inside the government. He said it out loud for the first time standing at the podium.

What about sites inside the country? At the same event he was warm toward those, with a condition attached. Sites that draw heavily on the grid must not push up electricity bills for the people living nearby, and operators should bring their own power. Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle all run data centers abroad, and none of them commented in the hours after the remarks.

## Who Actually Holds the Power to Tax

The largest gap in the remarks is authority. How much, from when, and on what legal ground are all missing. Bloomberg Law wrote that Trump "didn't specify how he would carry out such a threat, or what legal authority the administration would have to impose it." The outlets that covered the briefing, CNBC and ABC Australia among them, could not fill that space either.

In the United States a president does not make taxes. Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution gives Congress the power to lay and collect taxes. A president cannot invent a new levy and impose it alone, and the exception arises only where Congress has handed a specific authority to the executive by statute. Every fight over tariffs in recent years has turned on the limits of that delegation. Trump did not say whether he intends to ask Congress to legislate or to reach for an authority the executive already holds.

One more distinction is worth drawing. He said he would not allow it and that he would tax it in the same breath, but in law these are different acts. The first blocks private investment abroad outright; the second raises the cost until the choice changes. The statute each would rest on, and the bar each would have to clear, are not the same. Which of the two does he mean? The briefing did not go that far.

Whether it would reach back to projects already announced is also unknown. Microsoft committed 25 billion dollars to Australia in April and Amazon 20 billion in June. Whether sites already under construction fall within scope changes the weight of these remarks entirely.

What the same kind of authority looks like when it is actually used had appeared the day before. On November 19 the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security published [a rule covering remote access to advanced chips](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/us-commerce-department-adopts-rule-restricting-remote-access-to-advanced-ai-chips) and made it effective that same day. Sending advanced AI chips to an overseas data center that supplies computing power above a set size to companies headquartered in China or Macau now requires a license first. The size threshold is written down as 12 million aggregate Total Processing Performance, and facilities that already serve such customers were given until February 17, 2027 to obtain one. Data centers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil were placed in a separate license category.

The starting point is the same worry voiced in the briefing room the next day: that China reaches American computing power through data centers abroad. So what is different here? This one carries a rule name, an effective date and numbers. Export control is an authority Congress handed to the executive, so there was somewhere to point.

## Words Alone, and the Share Prices Moved

It was a remark rather than a policy, and the market still responded the same day. Alphabet closed down 2.7 percent, Microsoft 2.8 percent and Amazon 4.1 percent, and data center REITs slid with them. Pinning those numbers on the remarks alone is difficult. The whole market was falling that week, and the S&P 500 had shed around 9 percent since November 4. One outlet reported that the move in the hour or two right after the comments came to less than 0.3 percent.

Even so, a single sentence with no rate and no start date was treated as a discount factor against investment plans worth hundreds of billions of dollars. A threat that has not been fixed still gets a price.

Why now? The answer sits in what has been happening inside the United States. Complaints that data centers draw enormous amounts of electricity and push up local bills have been piling up all year. At the November 3 midterms, 41 local ballot measures on data centers went to a vote across 10 states, and in 40 of them voters chose to limit development. In a CNBC survey, 64 percent of registered voters said they opposed an AI data center going up within 10 miles of their home, against 13 percent in favor.

That mood is already moving into legislation. On November 19, the day before the briefing, the Senate passed [an energy permitting bill](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/20/g-s1-154579/senate-bipartisan-energy-bill) by 62 votes to 33. In exchange for faster approval of grid construction, it instructs the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to rewrite rate rules so that data centers carry the transmission costs they themselves create. Votes came from both parties, and the bill has gone to the House.

The two attitudes on display in the briefing are the two halves of that mood. Build at home and do not push the electricity bill onto residents; build abroad and pay a tax. Keeping the data centers inside the United States without taking the local backlash is what both demands amount to, and both came out in one sitting.

## Why Pebblous Is Watching This

Where to put a data center used to be an engineering question. The farther a user sits from the facility, the longer a request takes to travel, and traffic crossing a continent carries a fee. Building close to the people who use it was the default. That calculation is written in milliseconds and dollars, so everyone arrives at the same answer.

A second force was already in the room. Some data has to sit physically inside the country it belongs to. Europe's privacy rules are the familiar case, and finance, healthcare and the public sector attach similar requirements country by country. This force points the same way: it pulls the facility toward the people whose data it holds.

Trump's remarks are a third force, pulling the other way. While performance and local law say build inside the country the users are in, a tax threatened at home says do not build outside the United States. Unlike the other two, this force comes with no numbers.
