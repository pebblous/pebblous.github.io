Executive Summary

A biped humanoid left an office lobby, crossed a street and stopped outside a store in the next building, 96.6 metres away, acting on spoken instructions and nothing else. The walk is reported in X-Nav, a technical report posted to arXiv by a team led by AgiBot X-Lab, and the robot that did the walking is the Lingxi X2. No map was drawn in advance, and no route was fixed.

The measured numbers, though, belong to a copy of that same building rather than to the walk. The team scanned the lobby where the robot would work, rebuilt it in three dimensions, and gathered 15,602 extra segments covering what the policy should do when it drifts off the path and how it should settle onto a stopping spot. After training on those segments, success across 100 runs inside the copy went from 40% to 80%. The same training pushed the share of runs that ended within one metre of the target down from 28% to 20%.

Sections 1 through 5 follow the design and the numbers the report discloses. Section 6, which reads the result as a question about collecting data site by site, is this article's own interpretation.

1

A Spoken Sentence Is All It Gets

The run begins in an office lobby. At 9.1 metres an operator speaks a new destination, a convenience store in the building next door. The robot turns, passes through a glass door at the 34.2-metre mark and continues outdoors, then waits 24 seconds between 63.7 and 65.5 metres for vehicles and pedestrians to clear. The storefront sign comes into view at 85.9 metres, and at 96.6 metres the robot stops in front of the entrance. The whole thing takes 3 minutes 24 seconds, 34.2 metres of it indoors and 62.4 metres outdoors.

There is a yardstick for that distance. An episode in the public navigation benchmarks usually runs 10 to 15 metres, so this walk is several times longer. Length is not the only thing that stretched. Indoor vision, where a wall sits a few metres ahead, turns into evening sunlight and buildings tens of metres away the moment the robot clears the glass door, and the same model keeps walking through the change with nothing swapped in.

What walked that route was not the full X-Nav stack but one model inside it. The planner that breaks instructions apart was left out, and MELIOR-A ran on its own.

A second run over the same ground, this time with the planner attached, also appears in the report. The operator first asks for a bottle of water from the vending machine, and shortly after the robot sets off the instruction changes: go outside, find the Lawson, buy a coffee. Out the door, turn right, second building, the operator adds. Neither instruction walks the robot through the route step by step. The planner breaks the words into small jobs such as changing the destination, finding the side door and turning right outside, and MELIOR-N picks the next place to stand for each job.

Three moving obstacles, two stationary ones and two narrow gaps sat on that route. The moving ones were a cart crossing the lobby, a cleaning robot and a pedestrian crossing the street. On the stationary side was another robot parked beside a kiosk, and the last gap was between a signboard and a traffic cone. The robot reached the store with no map and no predefined route. What the report does not give for this run is any figure for distance or time.

No success rate attaches to the 96.6-metre walk. It is a single demonstration reported in figures and prose, and the report never says how many times the route was attempted or how many of those attempts worked. Every number of the 40%-and-80% kind that appears later in this article comes from the simulated tests in section 5. The two results point at the same site, but they are different experiments.

The robot is the Lingxi X2. Its height, weight and joint count are absent from the report itself; material published by the manufacturer describes a biped humanoid roughly 1.3 metres tall. Weight and degrees of freedom differ between outside sources, so this article leaves them open. One thing is settled: inference does not run on the robot. A GPU off-board receives the video, computes the next action and sends it back over the network.

The authors describe the walk as the first time long-horizon, language-instructed navigation from indoors to outdoors has been carried out on a real humanoid. That is their own assessment, and no record of another team verifying it on the same terms turned up.

2

Walking Without a Map

X-Nav neither builds a map nor borrows one. The robot carries several sensors. The head-mounted RGB camera runs at 640×480 with a 79-degree horizontal field of view, and there is a downward-tilted RGB-D camera, a LiDAR unit and leg odometry besides. Yet the model that decides where to go receives exactly one input, the video from the head camera. The other sensors belong to separate layers that measure distance once a target is close and steer around obstacles.

The absence of added hardware is worth recording too. Putting a navigation model on a real robot usually means bolting on one more camera, and the report cites the Intel RealSense D455 as the example. X-Nav walks on the two cameras the Lingxi X2 already carries. The head camera serves the model that chooses the route, and the downward depth camera takes over in front of a target.

What it means to work without a map shows up in the comparison table. Among the recent systems the report places in that table, those that cover large sites mostly lean on something prepared in advance. They follow a route handed to them, or they query a three-dimensional memory built up on earlier passes through the space. X-Nav sits in the row where that column is empty.

The model picking the route is called MELIOR. It takes the instruction, the current frame and up to seven frames selected from the past, and returns what to do next. There are two variants. MELIOR-A, built on Qwen3-VL-8B, emits up to four primitive actions at a time, and there are only three kinds of action: move forward 0.25 metres, turn 15 degrees left or right, and stop. MELIOR-N, built on Qwen3.5-4B, returns a single navigation goal instead of actions. It can specify that goal four ways, by pointing at a visible location, by giving a bearing and a distance, by rotating in place, or by stopping.

Within one mission the jobs handed to the robot fall into two kinds. One is a route, a long sentence of the cross-the-lobby-then-out-the-glass-door-and-turn-right sort, and that side belongs to MELIOR. The other is a target, a thing called by name: the vending machine, the blue sofa, the store entrance. The report gives a numerical reason for not handing both to one model. End-to-end models reach the neighbourhood of a destination more often than they come to rest on it, and the gap between those two metrics in the benchmark table further down is the evidence. On top of that, when an object is small or occluded or seen from a new angle, there is no way to inspect what the model currently believes its target to be. That is why the last few metres were pulled out into a job of their own.

Approaching a named object belongs to MELIOR-X. LocateAnything-3B proposes candidates, Qwen3-VL-2B verifies them, SAM3 segments the object, and LiDAR points are filtered into that region to yield range and bearing. Close to the target the system switches to the depth camera. Above all of this sits one more layer, X-Navarch. Its planner breaks the spoken instruction into small jobs and reviews, each time the robot halts, whether what has been done so far is right. Below it, a goal adapter compensates for however long the computation took, and an avoidance layer handles stationary and moving objects separately.

The report draws these parts as three nested loops. The innermost repeats look-at-the-video and walk until one job is finished. The middle one is the planner issuing jobs one at a time and inspecting the result at each handover. The outermost is the deployment loop: scan the site, fit the model to that site, measure it in closed loop inside the scanned copy, then put it on the real robot. Sections 4 and 5 are the story of that outer loop.

The training bill is recorded as well. On NVIDIA H20 GPUs, MELIOR-A took roughly 4,450 hours and MELIOR-N roughly 3,115. Those figures cover the stage described in section 3, where general ability is built from general data. The site adaptation in section 4 is separate, and far cheaper.

General Ability From 6.31 Million Samples

General ability comes from the big end of the numbers. Training for navigation used 6,310,664 samples. Add 1,650,547 samples of video understanding and 590,667 of spatial reasoning and the total training set reaches 8,551,878, of which navigation accounts for 73.8%. Because the robot has to make sense of a scene and a sentence at once, more than a quarter of what it was fed lies outside navigation proper.

The 6.31 million come from four sources. R2R and RxR are public navigation benchmarks; the other two the authors generated themselves in their own simulator. The pile was not filled from public data alone.

Whether 6.31 million is a large number also has a yardstick. In the same table the report lists Qwen-RobotNav, trained on 15.6 million samples, and NavGPT-3, which added a planner on top of 19.3 million records. Another model used 2.4 million simulated trajectories plus reinforcement learning. Measured by volume alone, X-Nav is not the heaviest feeder in the room.

One branch never reaches that table. The report divides data collection into three routes: public scenes, the site replica that section 4 describes, and real robots. On a walking robot the head camera shakes with each step, the step commanded and the step actually taken diverge, and the pose where the robot comes to rest differs from the one it was told to hold. To capture that difference the team recorded video and motion directly on the Lingxi X2, and built the setup so several robots could collect in the same site at once. The targets were things like bins, coffee machines and vending machines. How many samples this route produced, and how much of it went into the training totals above, the report does not say.

Where the resulting model lands is shown by the benchmark table in section 5. The short version is that it climbs to a seat among comparable models and stops there. Carrying out an instruction end to end inside one real building is not something those 6.31 million samples solved. The next section is how that gap was filled.

4

Cloning the One Building It Works In

The method is to lift the exact space where the robot will stand. A handheld scanner is walked around the lobby, recording images, point clouds and the pose each capture was taken from. Visible appearance is rebuilt from that record with 3D Gaussian splatting, while depth and walkable floor are attached separately as a mesh and an occupancy layer. A person then curates the object graph by hand, identifying 35 named objects, and places a goal pose for each target somewhere with enough clearance for the robot to stand.

Inside the cloned lobby the team builds 500 tasks. A baseline planner lays out collision-free paths, and no single task is longer than 35 metres. Three hundred of them follow instructions literally around the vending machine, 100 go to a target named outright, and 100 leave the robot to work out which object it needs. The 500 are split 400 for training, 50 for validation and 50 for testing. Cut from the baseline trajectories, the training set comes to 32,404 segments.

So far this amounts to a pile of model answers. What comes next is the part of the report most worth dwelling on. The policy trained up to this point is set running inside the cloned space. Whenever it wanders off the path or settles in the wrong spot, the baseline planner annotates what it should have done at that point. The procedure collects recoveries from mistaken positions rather than more correct behaviour, and it follows a method proposed in 2011. The segments gathered this way come to 9,601 for getting back onto the path and 6,001 for refining where to stop, 15,602 in total. Added to the baseline segments, the training set reaches 48,006.

The training cost is small. The body of MELIOR-A's weights stays frozen, and only a rank-64 LoRA is trained on top. One segment pairs the current frame plus the seven before it with the next four actions. This is a different order of magnitude from the thousands of GPU-hours in section 2. The ability built from general data is left alone, and what is needed in this one building is laid over it in a thin layer.

Nor is it a procedure that runs once. The planning layer logs every mission, sends failed episodes back as material to rerun in the replica, and sends unscanned areas back as material to scan. Failures in the field become the next collection list.

The report also describes a setting with the baseline trajectories scaled up to 2,500. That yields 240,030 segments, 78,010 of them corrections. The mixing ratio is the same in both settings: 67.5% baseline trajectories, 20% recovery segments, 12.5% stop refinement. The text states that site adaptation used the 2,500 setting, yet the 100-run closed-loop results in the next section come from the 500 setting. Running the same test on the larger setting is not reported.

Success Doubles, Precise Stopping Goes the Other Way

The test ran 100 times inside the cloned lobby, 50 on the validation split and 50 on the test split, closed loop, meaning the robot was left to move under its own decisions to the end. Two policies were compared: one trained on the baseline trajectories alone, and one given the 15,602 correction segments on top.

▲ The 100-run closed-loop test inside the cloned lobby. The collision counts appear in the prose rather than in the table. | Source: arXiv:2611.17598

The largest movement is on the route side. Runs that used to end an average of 9.63 metres from the target now end 3.36 metres away, and runs that finished in sustained collision fell from 52 to 3. Two rows moved the other way, both near the bottom. Stopping within one metre dropped from 28% to 20%, and stopping within half a metre from 13% to 3%. Even with 6,001 segments devoted to refining where to stop, precise stopping got worse, and the authors record the point as a limitation.

The fitted model did not stay inside the replica. The report shows it back in the same lobby on the real robot, crossing open floor, passing a bank of lockers and reaching the vending machine. Measuring in the cloned space and sending the result back to the real site is the outer loop described in section 2. This deployment, too, is shown as one run, with no repeat count given.

5.1What the Table Does Not Say

What these numbers measure has to be pinned down first. Tasks in the cloned space never exceed 35 metres, an order of magnitude away from the 96.6-metre walk in section 1. The report also states outright that the scenes and targets used in training are the same ones used in evaluation. So the table cannot be read as the model having improved across the board. It measures how well the model has been fitted to this one site, and the authors describe it that way themselves.

When the tasks were built, they fell into three kinds. Three hundred follow an instruction literally. In 100 the target is named outright, and in the last 100 the robot has to infer which object is meant. The 100-run results, however, are not broken out by kind. There is one aggregate and nothing else. Which kind failed more often, and what situations the collisions clustered in, are not given as cases either. The aggregate drop from 52 to 3 is everything the report says about collisions. The only axis left for comparison is baseline-only against baseline-plus-corrections.

There is also a naming inconsistency to watch. The introduction and the results section take 40% to 80% at the 3-metre threshold as the headline, while the conclusion takes 34% to 78%, the version that requires the robot to stop. The same improvement is being called by two different rulers, so anyone quoting a doubled success rate should say which ruler. Every figure called a success rate in this article uses the 3-metre threshold.

5.2Where It Stands on Public Benchmarks

The general ability built from the data in section 3 was measured separately, on the unseen-environment splits of R2R-CE and RxR-CE. The report sorts the comparison into two groups. One holds models that use a single forward-facing camera; the other holds models that take panoramic input, call on map tools, or were trained at far larger scale. MELIOR belongs to the first group.

On R2R-CE, MELIOR-N leads its group. Its 70.5% success and 65.1% path-weighted score are the highest among models working from a single forward camera, and its average distance from the target, 3.34 metres, is the smallest. One figure runs the other way: the share of runs that merely reached the neighbourhood of the target is 73.6%, trailing LightNav-0's 73.7% by a tenth of a point. Move to RxR-CE and the order turns over. The same model manages 60.9%, behind LightNav-0 at 73.6% and CorrectNav at 69.3%. That is the multilingual split, where each instruction runs far longer, so the safer reading is that following long sentences is not yet where this model leads. The higher numbers from NavGPT-3 belong to another weight class, using panoramic input and map tools, and the report itself separates that group as a reference.

6

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Report

What draws the eye in this report is the data rather than the robot. A model fed 6.31 million samples and placed near the top of the public benchmarks reached the neighbourhood of its goal four times out of ten in the one building it was meant to work in. What closed that gap was not more general data but the 15,602 segments collected inside the cloned lobby. The ratio between the two numbers is better than 400 to 1.

Look closer and what closed the gap was a kind of data, not an amount of it. The 15,602 segments are not more successful runs. They cover only two things: getting back on track after leaving it, and choosing where to come to rest. General data holds almost none of that, because people demonstrate the correct answer. This is why Pebblous talks about aiming when it talks about synthetic data. Data that adds more of what a model already handles and data built only where the model breaks are worth different amounts, even at the same ten thousand records.

Aiming does not rescue everything, though. Under the same training, stopping accurately within one metre went down. What you aim at decides which metrics rise and which fall, and that is the most useful warning this report leaves behind. Collecting data with aim puts a weight on one side of the scale; it does not lift the scale.

How much to mix in is also unsettled. The first item the authors list as future work is a study that varies the volume of data, the number of scenes, and the ratio between correction data and real-robot data. What this report shows is one recipe, 67.5 to 20 to 12.5, producing one result at one site, and whether that recipe is the best one the authors leave open as well.

So that none of this gets read as one undifferentiated claim, here is what this article rests on, split three ways.

A team trying to put a model into a site can take this report and ask three questions of their own setup.

  • Is there a record, segment by segment, of where our model gets stuck? The 15,602 correction segments are data you can only build if you already know the failure points.
  • Can we build a replica that reproduces those places? The replica in this report came from one scanner and a reconstruction pipeline, and it included a person labelling 35 objects by hand.
  • Are we measuring what falls while the metric we aimed at rises? Had the one-metre threshold not been measured separately, this report would have been remembered only as a story about a doubled success rate.

Set beside the previous article in this series, ScaleVLA: Does a Bigger Robot Brain Need Fewer Demo Videos?, the axes separate. That one looked at growing the model to spend less on data; this one looks at leaving the model alone and laying site data thinly over the top. The two are not competing options. How far to push general ability, and which sites to copy once that ability runs out, are separate lines in the budget.

Editor's Note

The report went up on arXiv only recently, it has not been peer reviewed, and no other team has yet reproduced the experiments. No evidence that code or weights have been released turned up. There are 13 authors, listed under AgiBot X-Lab together with several universities. For the robot's body specifications the manufacturer's own published material was consulted, since the report omits them; those sources disagree with each other, and only the height appears here. Site adaptation was evaluated in one building, on one floor, along one route, and stairs, lifts and doors that have to be opened were outside the scope from the start. Three real-robot walks appear in all, each of them a one-off, and none carries statistics over repeated attempts. Change the site and the numbers will change with it.
