Executive Summary

For the first time, OpenAI has made public the pretraining loss of thirteen models since GPT-3.5. It came in a post that chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published on the company blog on November 22. Two days later the evaluation organization METR took the same data, refit it segment by segment, and wrote in its analysis that the speed at which loss is falling went from 0.022 a year to 0.156 a year starting in February 2026, a factor of 7.1.

The question is the exam paper those values were measured on. The main held-out set OpenAI has used since GPT-3.5 is drawn from the company's internal codebase, as Pachocki wrote himself. Researchers on the outside cannot see that code, so they can neither reproduce the value nor measure another company's model against the same yardstick. METR writes that if part of the evaluation set found its way into the training data, or if the models overfit the internal distribution, real progress may be overstated, and adds that no outside data exists yet to tell those possibilities apart.

Sections 1 through 4 set out what is written in the OpenAI post, the METR analysis, and Epoch AI's public material. Section 5, which moves to the question of who builds evaluation data and who verifies it, is this article's own reading.

1

OpenAI Puts Its Internal Loss Numbers Outside

Pachocki's post is titled "Perplexity and Intelligence." Its argument is that the capability of modern AI comes out of training a compressor and predictor on a very large corpus, and a proposal follows from that: make perplexity the shared metric the industry compares models with. Perplexity captures how little a model is surprised by the next token, and the smaller the number, the better the prediction. Pachocki calls the value perplexity, or cross-entropy loss, in the same breath. The numbers below are log-perplexity, the logarithm of that value, and what this article calls loss points at the same thing.

Four values appear in the post. GPT-3.5 sat at a log-perplexity of about 0.99 on the current main evaluation dataset and GPT-4 at about 0.91, losses later went below 0.8, and the company recently upgraded to a model at about 0.76. That last model is not named. The chart plots the trajectory of thirteen models since GPT-3.5, but the text carries no list of the intermediate values.

Ajeya Cotra, Jean-Stanislas Denain and Tom Cunningham of METR refit the base-model trajectory on that chart segment by segment two days later. From 2022 to early 2026, loss fell by 0.022 a year. Since February 2026 it has been falling by 0.156 a year. If that pace holds, the decline that took three years from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4.1 repeats in about 6.5 months.

METR called the picture "very striking" because it runs the wrong way. Under the standard scaling laws, loss falls more and more slowly as data and compute grow. Double the inputs and the improvement that comes back keeps shrinking. The published curve instead steepens from some point on.

The post carries one more thing besides the loss values, and that is a proposal. Pachocki writes that entering the log-perplexity window of roughly 0.68 to 0.7 should lead us to expect another transformative shift in capability, and adds that he does not know what those capabilities will be. He names two worries. One is that alignment auditing, the work of looking into what a model is actually pursuing, becomes harder than it is now. The other is that situational awareness grows, meaning the model notices whether it is being evaluated or trained. His point is to mark in advance the stretch where capability climbs while the instruments for looking into it go dull first. So he proposes common limits, grounded in perplexity and other measurements, that developers commit to not crossing.

Sam Altman wrote on X that the post was the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while. That was before METR's reanalysis.

2

OpenAI Measured on Code Only It Can Read

Pachocki states the origin of the evaluation set in the post itself. "The primary held-out datasets we've been using since GPT-3.5 are sourced from our internal codebase."

He is also the first to say that measuring general capability with code is unreliable. A footnote calls code prediction performance a poor proxy for general capability. The reason he uses this set anyway is that it is the number OpenAI watches most widely when it gauges progress internally. Nothing about the set was designed for comparison across labs. The composition of the data and the way it was collected are not disclosed. The post says only that a fixed snapshot is used.

What the metric cannot do is laid out in the same post, in four parts. Developers use different evaluation sets and train on different data mixtures, so values cannot be compared across companies. Perplexity does not correspond directly to the tasks we actually care about. Most notably, chain-of-thought reasoning does not lower a model's perplexity. And because nobody knows how much irreducible entropy sits in the data itself, there is no saying how far the remaining loss can fall.

A paradox sits between the proposal and the measurement. For developers to hold a common limit together, all of them have to be able to measure against the same yardstick. The value published here is measured only on one company's internal code, and that company's own post says values cannot be compared across companies.

3

METR Points to Three Places Where the Curve Could Move

METR does not say the acceleration is fake. Instead it names three confounders that could shift the number, and writes that none of them can be checked from outside.

3.1The evaluation set may have leaked into training

The evaluation set is drawn from OpenAI's internal codebase, and internal code also goes into the data the models train on. If the two overlap even slightly, the model is not predicting code it has never seen but recalling code it has. In that case what the curve draws is contamination rather than capability.

3.2Overfitting to the internal distribution

Even with no direct leak, if that evaluation set is the number watched most widely inside the company, the many choices made in training and tuning can tilt implicitly toward its distribution. This is the general problem that appears when the thing being measured holds the measuring instrument. METR puts the two together and writes that real progress may be overstated, and that more outside data would help tell the difference.

3.3The lag between the end of pretraining and release

The horizontal axis of the chart marks when a model reached the world, not when its pretraining finished. If OpenAI has lately been shipping finished models faster, the real acceleration is smaller than the chart shows. If the lag has grown instead, the real acceleration is larger. No outside information settles which.

So the proposal METR offers points at the data. What is needed in the long run is a held-out corpus curated by a third party, spanning code, scientific writing and prose written before 2022, before AI-written text entered the mix. What is missing right now is not a better model but a shared exam paper that anyone can recompute the same value on.

4

A Different Yardstick Does Not Show the Same Acceleration

A cross-check METR adds makes the matter more complicated. Over the same period, benchmark scores for OpenAI models show no acceleration of this size. The index the post names is Epoch AI's ECI, the Epoch Capabilities Index.

ECI synthesizes more than 50 benchmarks into a single scale and places more than 260 models on it. In a Bayesian analysis Epoch published on November 4, the reasoning frontier is rising at about 16.6 points a year, with a 90% interval of 14.6 to 18.7. It is a steady climb, and the discontinuous kink that shows up as 7.1 times on the loss side is not there.

ECI has recorded an acceleration of its own before. In a report this April, Epoch presented evidence that ECI began rising faster once reasoning models arrived. That was a separate event, at a different time and with a different cause attached to it. No report has yet shown ECI bending the same way over the stretch in question, the one since February 2026. The two indices are drawing different shapes for the same event.

That does not add up to a conclusion that the benchmarks are right and perplexity is wrong. In a footnote METR writes that composite capability indices including ECI are sensitive to statistical assumptions, or lean heavily on a handful of benchmarks that are already close to saturated. The absence of an acceleration is not fully reliable counter-evidence either. Two yardsticks are telling different stories, and there is no third, independent measurement to decide between them.

Hence the sentence in METR's post: "METR does not have a consensus view on this." It states that there is no agreement inside the organization and leaves all three readings open. The first is that contamination and overfitting have overstated progress. The second is that benchmarks are the better measure of capability and perplexity is a secondary input metric, so the outlook does not change much. The third is that perplexity is the more fundamental metric and that an acceleration in real capability follows the acceleration in pretraining, in which case a broad capability shift arrives within six months.

The three readings sit very far apart. One speaks of measurement error, another of an abrupt shift half a year from now. Separating them would take evaluation data open enough for the measurement to be run again elsewhere. Today there is none.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This

The shape of this episode is not peculiar to frontier models. There is one measuring instrument, and the thing being measured builds it and keeps it. Any organization that trains models on internal data and reports performance on an evaluation set it built itself stands in the same place. Does a quarterly rise in the internal score speak to the model's capability, or to how close the evaluation set and the training data have drifted? The question is whether anything in the organization can tell the two apart.

Three things are worth checking for anyone running an evaluation set of their own. They move the items METR says cannot be verified from outside into a form an organization can verify inside. Even for numbers no one else can reproduce, a record kept inside tells you where to look when the score moves.

  • What proves that the evaluation set and the training corpus do not overlap? Overlap has to be left behind as the result of a check, not as a declaration. Without a record of when a document-level duplicate check ran and what it caught, a rising score carries no grounds for ruling contamination out.
  • Are the people who build the evaluation set separate from the people who build the model? When the same team sets the questions and writes the answers, overfitting accumulates with nobody intending it. METR proposes a corpus curated by a third party for exactly this reason.
  • Is the composition and collection date of the fixed snapshot written down? Without knowing which document entered the evaluation set when and by what route, a change in the score cannot be attributed: the model may have changed, or the exam paper may have.

All three are questions to answer on the data side before a model is trained. Once training is over, there is no place left to create the record retroactively. That is why Pebblous puts provenance records and the independence of evaluation data first when it talks about AI-Ready Data. A claim that performance got faster is trustworthy only as far as the data that can verify it.

Editor's Note

The title, author and publication date of the OpenAI post, the log-perplexity values 0.99, 0.91, 0.8 and 0.76, the fact that the trajectory of thirteen models appears only in the chart, the English sentence placing the evaluation set in the internal codebase, the footnote calling code prediction a poor proxy for general capability, the passage naming perplexity and cross-entropy loss in the same breath, the 0.68 to 0.7 window and the proposal for common limits, the worry that alignment auditing gets harder and situational awareness grows in that window, and the four limits of the metric (no comparison across companies, no direct correspondence to tasks, chain-of-thought reasoning not lowering perplexity, and the unknown irreducible entropy) were all confirmed in the OpenAI blog post of November 22, 2026. The 0.022 and 0.156 a year, the factor of 7.1, the 6.5-month conversion, the three confounders of contamination, overfitting and release lag, the proposal for a third-party corpus, the sentence about having no consensus and the three readings, and the observation that benchmarks show no comparable acceleration along with the footnote on the limits of composite indices were confirmed in METR's analysis of November 24, 2026. The composition of ECI, the 16.6 points a year with a 90% interval of 14.6 to 18.7, and the April report finding that ECI rose faster after reasoning models arrived were confirmed in Epoch AI's public material. That April acceleration differs from this bend in the loss curve in both timing and cause, so the two events are written separately here rather than joined.

What this article chose not to write is worth recording too. The chart OpenAI published plots thirteen models, but the post carries no list of the intermediate values, so this article carries only the four values in the text and the two rates METR fit by segment. Neither side disclosed what actually went into the evaluation set, so contamination is written as a possibility alone, and Sam Altman's praise, posted as a short note on X, is left as a single line. The passage in section 5 about internal evaluation sets is this article's reading and appears in neither post.
