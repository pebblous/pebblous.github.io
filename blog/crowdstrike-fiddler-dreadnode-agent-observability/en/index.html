Executive Summary

CrowdStrike is buying two AI security startups at once. It announced both agreements, for Fiddler AI and for Dreadnode, at its Falcon Europe event in Barcelona on November 3. The company did not disclose the price, and the Wall Street Journal put the two deals together at about $495 million.

The place to look is where the money split. Dreadnode, which attacks models and agents before they ship in order to find the weak points, went for about $95 million. Fiddler AI, which writes down what an agent read and what it ran, went for about $400 million. The biggest check a security company wrote here bought a tool that keeps a record of behavior, not one that blocks an attack. George Kurtz, the chief executive, called Fiddler "the flight recorder for every agent" in the announcement.

Sections 1 through 3 follow what the announcement and the reporting say. Section 4, which moves the deal from a security question to a question about how records are designed, is this article's reading.

1

What Fiddler AI and Dreadnode Actually Do

Fiddler AI started in Palo Alto in 2018 and has raised about $99 million. Its work is to follow an agent's decision path and tool calls one step at a time, and to watch for behavior drifting outside the boundary originally set. CrowdStrike will fold the technology into its own platform under the name Falcon Observe and make it generally available in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2027. Krishna Gade, the co-founder, put the reason he started the company this way in the announcement. "We started Fiddler to help enterprises answer a fundamental question: can I trust what my AI agents are doing?"

Dreadnode was founded in 2023 and has raised $14 million. This side attacks models, applications and agents directly, before they go into service, to find where they break. The scale the announcement gives is more than 100 known AI attack strategies. Jailbreaks and prompt injection are in there, along with attacks that dig in over several turns and attacks built specifically for agents. The targets are not a single model either. The reasoning loops an agent carries its thinking through, the business applications it works in, and the MCP servers that hand it tools all get hit together.

What stands out more is how the findings get handed over. Dreadnode ranks them by severity, maps each one to four public frameworks including the OWASP Top 10 for LLMs and MITRE ATLAS, and turns them into reports a team can export and use to make a go or no-go call before deployment. Brad Palm, the chief executive, gave his reason for founding the company. "AI fails in ways traditional testing was never designed to catch, and the people best equipped to find those failures think like attackers. We founded Dreadnode to expose those weaknesses before AI reaches production."

That technology goes into Falcon Guardian, and the Dreadnode team joins CrowdStrike's Cyber Superintelligence Lab. The announcement splits the duties of the two products. Guardian protects agents from attack and abuse, and Observe confirms that an agent behaved as intended. One connection runs between them, in a single direction. Weaknesses found in pre-deployment testing feed Falcon Guardian as policy recommendations, so a flaw caught before shipping thickens the protection at runtime. Guardian also picked up an AI kill switch the same day, which shuts an agent down where it is running, cuts it off the network, and stops it from starting again when it strays from its defined behavior.

The terms are mostly cash with some stock subject to vesting. Both deals are expected to close in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2027, and because CrowdStrike has not published the amounts, the figures rest on the Wall Street Journal's account. SiliconANGLE put about four of every five dollars of the roughly $495 million total on the Fiddler AI side.

2

Saying the Model Did It Is Not an Answer

The sentence George Kurtz used to explain the deal points at accountability rather than security. "Every company is deploying agents and asking the same thing: what are agents actually doing? When a regulator or your board asks what an agent did, saying 'the model did it' is not an answer. Fiddler is the flight recorder for every agent." A flight recorder is not equipment that prevents an accident. It is equipment that tells you what happened after one.

CrowdStrike's announcement lays one more premise under that. AI agents do not have to be compromised to be dangerous, it says. An agent signs in to internal applications with legitimate credentials, uses approved tools, and pulls data it has permission to pull. Holding the permission and making the right call are two separate facts. Misreading an instruction, drifting away from the assigned job, carrying out an action nobody intended: all of it happens inside normal authority.

The question left after an incident, then, is not who got in but which step went off course. Traces of an intrusion answer the first one. Only the record answers the second. On the day a refund was approved in error, if nothing remains of which documents the agent read and which tools it called in what order, an investigation has nowhere to start.

Blocking and explaining are different things to design for. Blocking means deciding where to draw the boundary. Explaining means deciding what to keep and in what form. The second belongs less to the security team than to the people who handle data. That a security company put most of nearly half a billion dollars on the second is the thing to notice in this announcement.

3

The Fields a Single Line of the Record Has to Carry

One technical line in the announcement offers a clue. Fiddler AI instruments agents with OpenTelemetry and supports LangGraph, Amazon Bedrock, AWS Strands and Google ADK. OpenTelemetry is an open convention that fixes a common way of emitting logs and traces, and it defines a separate set of entries for generative AI. Whichever vendor a company picks, the fields it has to fill in to record one step of an agent are mostly written down in there.

Entry The question it answers
gen_ai.operation.name What was this step: a conversation, or a tool execution?
gen_ai.provider.name Which model provider was called?
gen_ai.agent.name Which agent did the work?
gen_ai.tool.name Which tool did it use?
gen_ai.tool.call.id Among several calls to the same tool, which call was this?
gen_ai.conversation.id Which conversation does this step belong to?
gen_ai.response.finish_reasons Why did it stop there?

▲ Key attributes of the OpenTelemetry semantic conventions for generative AI. Source: open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai

One caveat comes attached to that list. The entries above are still marked Development, so their names and their scope can change. Operational records for agents sit in territory where the specification has not settled, which makes handing a whole log schema to a vendor the same as handing a company's audit material to a specification that is still moving.

Set this beside the Dreadnode side from section 1 and the contrast gets sharp. Pre-deployment testing already has four public frameworks to hang a finding on, so whoever runs the test, there is a basis for passing results back and forth under the same names. The records an agent leaves while it is running have no such agreement yet. So the side you reach for after something goes wrong turns out to be the one whose names have settled less.

There are fields this convention does not fill in at all. The entries above explain the steps an agent took, while whose authority it ran them under, which repository and which version of a document it read, and which business record the result landed in are scattered across other systems. That gap is exactly where CrowdStrike says it will tie agent behavior to Falcon's identity, cloud and runtime data. Doing the same thing in-house takes agent logs, human account records and data access records joined on one identifier.

4

The Market Is Growing, but Record-Keeping Falls to Each Company

The reason this is hard to read as a one-off lies in a Gartner forecast from August. The market for products that help companies use AI safely grows 68.7% from $2.8 billion in 2026 to $4.8 billion in 2027, and reaches $7.7 billion in 2028. The market meant here is a different category from security products that use AI to detect threats. It counts separately the products that treat AI itself as what has to be managed.

In the same press release, Gartner's Shailendra Upadhyay set out what comes next. "As the market grows, we can expect increased consolidation and acquisitions, with larger cybersecurity companies buying these startups to broaden their offerings." These two deals landed ten weeks after that forecast. If watching agents is on its way to becoming one slot in an existing security platform instead of a product sold on its own, the choices open to a company narrow along with the platforms.

From here the article leaves the announcement behind. The fourth quarter of fiscal 2027, when Falcon Observe arrives, began back in November 2026, so the wait is not a long one. Even so, the day a product is installed and the day a record starts are not the same day. Internal agents keep running while a company decides to adopt it and wires up the instrumentation, and logs never written in that stretch do not get generated retroactively later. So what to check before a release schedule is the state of your own records. Three questions are enough.

  • Where do the tools an internal agent called and the documents it read end up? If they end up nowhere, at which step does the trail break?
  • Is there an identifier joining those records to human account records? Can the work an agent did be traced back to the person who asked for it?
  • How long are the records kept? Are they still there when a quarterly close or an outside audit comes asking?

Those three are easier to answer for whoever runs the data platform than for the security team. That is where the people sit who know which table fills up with what, and on what cycle. The gap a $400 million deal pointed at ends up in the same place.

Editor's Note

The first thing Pebblous looks at when diagnosing data quality is the route a value came in by. Data that keeps no trace of its source and its transformation steps is hard to call wrong and hard to call right. Agent behavior records are an asset of the same kind. They cannot be made after an incident, and only a company that fixed a schema and accumulated them beforehand can pull them out.
