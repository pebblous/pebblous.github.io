## LinkedIn (KO)

수노를 상대로 한 저작권 소송에서, 소장에 특정된 저작권 곡이 560개에서 6만여 개로 불어났습니다.

원고 측 전문가가 수노 측 로펌 사무실에 물리적으로 들어가, 학습 데이터 전체를 오디오 지문으로 대조한 결과입니다. 수노가 곡 단위 출처를 스스로 기록해 두지 않았기 때문에, 그 출처를 제3자가 법정 절차로 사후에 강제 복원해야 했습니다.

그사이 법원의 무게중심은 조용히 옮겨갔습니다. 2025년 Bartz v. Anthropic 이후 미국 법원은 "무엇으로 배웠는가"보다 "만들어낸 결과가 원작의 시장을 잠식하는가"를 공정이용 제4요소로 더 무겁게 보기 시작했습니다.

다만 분석용 학습과 경쟁 생성물을 가르는 이 선은, 아직 판사마다 강조점이 갈리는 확정되지 않은 법리입니다. 그럼에도 방향은 분명합니다. 저작물 하나하나의 출처가 소송 증거가 되는 순간, 데이터 계보는 윤리 구호가 아니라 소송과 정산이 딛고 서는 법적 인프라가 됩니다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

In the copyright case against Suno, the list of songs named in the complaint jumped from 560 to more than 60,000.

The increase came after the plaintiffs' expert physically entered Suno's law firm office and matched its entire training set with audio fingerprinting. It was a forensic reconstruction the court had to force, because Suno never logged where each song came from.

Meanwhile the legal center of gravity has quietly moved. Since Bartz v. Anthropic in 2025, U.S. courts weigh the fourth fair-use factor, whether the output erodes the original's market, more heavily than the question of what a model trained on.

That line between analytical training and competing generation is still drawn differently from judge to judge. The direction, though, is clear. The moment each song's origin becomes evidence, data lineage stops being an ethics slogan and becomes the legal infrastructure that litigation and settlement stand on.

## Facebook (EN)

Late in 2025, a few experts walk into a secure room at a law firm.

For two weeks their job is a single thing: to match, song by song, the music an AI had trained on against the originals.

That is how the count in the complaint grew from 560 songs to more than 60,000. But what stays with me isn't the number. It's that Suno itself didn't do this matching; a third party had to, through legal process, because the company had never written down where each song came from.

Meanwhile the question the courts are asking has been shifting.

From "what did it train on?" to "does what it made take the original's place?"

I've come to think of that scene as the after-the-fact recovery of provenance. Origins you don't record don't simply vanish. They come back later, in someone else's hands, at a far higher price. And this isn't only about music. In images, in news, the fights keep gathering around the same question: can you prove where it came from?

So for anyone working with data, it narrows to one question. Are we keeping the provenance of the songs, documents, and images we train on in a form we can later prove? That is why Pebblous has spent so long looking at the lineage of data. However the ruling lands, keeping a record of where things came from has no downside.
