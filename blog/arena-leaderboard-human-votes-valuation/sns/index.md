## LinkedIn (KO)

2억 달러를 받은 회사가 가진 자산은 모델도 칩도 아니었다. 사람들이 두 개의 답 가운데 나은 쪽을 고른 기록이다.

AI 모델 순위표를 운영하는 Arena가 10월 8일 시리즈 B로 그 돈을 받았다고 밝혔다. 라이트스피드 벤처파트너스와 코슬라 벤처스가 이끌었고, 이때 인정받은 기업가치가 31억 달러다. 올해 1월에는 17억 달러였다.

순위표 자체는 공짜다. 질문을 던지면 어느 모델인지 가린 채 답 두 개가 뜨고, 이용자가 마음에 드는 쪽을 누른다. 그렇게 쌓인 투표가 6,200만 표다. 돈은 반대쪽에서 들어온다. 그 기록으로 만든 평가 분석을 모델 개발사와 기업에 팔아, 1년치로 환산한 매출이 6월에 1억 달러를 넘었다.

이 값이 붙기 전에 먼저 일어난 일이 있다. 문제와 정답이 고정된 시험지가 믿음을 잃었다. 2025년 4월 메타는 라마 4의 실험용 버전으로 순위표 2위에 올랐지만, 며칠 뒤 공개판이 같은 순위표에 올라가자 32위가 됐다. 외워지는 문항과 달리 매번 새로 들어오는 사람의 비교 판단은 미리 맞출 수가 없다.

남는 물음은 둘이다. 그 표를 던진 사람들의 구성을 밝힌 자료는 공개된 범위에 없다. 그리고 순위를 매기는 쪽이 순위가 매겨지는 쪽에 분석을 판다. 같은 날 Arena는 모델의 안전성을 견주는 새 순위표도 함께 내놓았다. 규정 위반이 확인된 바는 없지만, 중립을 무엇으로 증명하느냐는 물음은 그대로 남는다.

사람이 붙인 라벨을 비용 항목으로만 세던 셈법이 여기서는 거꾸로 선다. 다만 값이 매겨진 것은 라벨의 양이 아니라, 누가 어떤 조건에서 판단했는지가 함께 남아 있는 기록이다. 페블러스가 이 소식을 투자 뉴스보다 데이터 쪽에서 먼저 읽는 이유가 여기에 있다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

The asset behind a $200 million round was neither a model nor a chip. It is the record of people choosing which of two answers they preferred.

Arena, which runs the AI model leaderboard, announced its Series B on 8 October. Lightspeed Venture Partners and Khosla Ventures led the round, which valued the company at $3.1 billion. In January it was worth $1.7 billion.

The leaderboard itself is free. You ask a question, two unlabelled answers come back, and you pick the better one. Sixty-two million of those picks have accumulated. The money arrives from the other side: Arena sells evaluation analytics built on that record to model labs and enterprises, and annualised revenue passed $100 million in June.

Something had to break first for those votes to be worth that. Benchmarks with fixed questions and fixed answers lost their credibility. In April 2025 Meta reached second place on the leaderboard with an experimental chat version of Llama 4; days later, when the released model was entered, it landed at 32nd. A fixed question can be studied for. A fresh human comparison cannot be.

Two questions survive the round. Nothing in the public record describes who casts the votes. And the party doing the ranking sells analytics to the parties being ranked. Arena published a new leaderboard scoring model safety on the same day. No rule has been shown to be broken, but the question of how neutrality gets demonstrated is still open.

Human labelling has long been booked as a cost to be minimised. Here the same item sits on the other side of the ledger. What carries the price, though, is not the volume of labels but a record that still says who judged what, and under which conditions. That is the angle we read this story from at Pebblous.

제가 오래 머문 자리는 투자 금액이 아니었습니다. 값이 매겨진 자산이 모델도 칩도 아니라 사람이 눌러 둔 기록이었다는 쪽입니다.

라벨은 오랫동안 비용이었습니다. 모델을 학습시키려면 어쩔 수 없이 치러야 하는 돈이었고, 줄일 수 있으면 줄이는 쪽이 잘하는 일이었습니다.

## Facebook (EN)

You type a question into a box and two answers come back. Neither one tells you which model wrote it.

You read both, pick the one you like better, and move on. It takes a few seconds.

Sixty-two million of those few seconds have now been priced. Arena, which runs the leaderboard they feed, raised $200 million last week at a valuation of $3.1 billion, close to double where it stood in January.

What kept me on the story was not the size of the round. It was that the asset being valued is neither a model nor a chip. It is a pile of buttons people pressed.

Labelling has been a cost line for a long time. Something you pay for because a model has to be trained, and something you look good for reducing.

This label behaves differently. It is not an answer key you build once and use for years. It holds its value only while someone keeps showing up to press the button today.

I have started calling this kind of data a flowing label. Which also means that when the flow stops, the value cools.

One thing stays with me. Everything disclosed is a total. Tens of millions of monthly visitors, more than 150 countries, 62 million votes. I could find nothing describing who casts them, where they live, or what work they do.

"Who pressed the buttons that made this ranking?"

This is close to why we ask teams at Pebblous to keep the conditions under which a number was produced, not only the number. An evaluation record that has lost the question of who judged it, and in what situation, has nothing to offer on the day its scores are doubted.

When you choose a model, how far do you trust an outside ranking, and where do you start measuring for yourself? I would like to know where other people draw that line.
