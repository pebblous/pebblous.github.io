비보다인 공동창업자 안드레이 게오르게스쿠가 지난 19일 TechCrunch에 한 말입니다. 필라델피아와 샌프란시스코에 실험실을 둔 이 회사는 마이크로플루이딕 칩 위에서 사람 조직 20종 이상을 기르고, HIVE라는 로봇 실험실이 그 조직에 직접 약을 넣고 결과를 읽습니다. 한 대가 한 번에 1만 개 조직을 시험하고 1~2주 안에 데이터를 낸다는 것이 회사 설명입니다.

그가 지목한 것은 모델의 크기가 아니라 데이터의 종류입니다. 지금의 세포 데이터는 전부 한 시점에 고정된 스냅숏이라, 모델은 세포 상태 A와 상태 B는 배워도 B가 A를 자극한 결과라는 것은 끝내 배우지 못한다는 진단입니다.

올해 6월 Nature Methods에 실린 연구는 2,220만 개 세포 코퍼스로 400개 모델을 사전학습한 뒤, 데이터를 계속 늘려도 성능이 곧 정체한다는 것을 보고했습니다. 논문이 확인한 것은 정체 현상까지입니다. 그 원인을 인과의 부재로 읽는 쪽은 창업자이지 저자들이 아닙니다.

검증되지 않은 대목도 분명합니다. 인체시험과 94% 일치했다는 간 조직 수치는 심사를 거치지 않은 회사 발표이고, 조기 접근권을 샀다는 대형 제약사 8곳은 이름이 공개되지 않았습니다.

페블러스가 데이터 품질 현장에서 반복해 만나는 장면도 이와 닮았습니다. 이미 쌓인 데이터를 아무리 잘 씻어도 처음부터 기록되지 않은 항목은 복원되지 않습니다.

남는 질문은 데이터가 얼마나 많은가가 아니라, 그 기록이 어떤 상태가 있었다는 기록인가 무엇을 바꿨더니 무엇이 달라졌다는 기록인가입니다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

A startup that grows human tissue in a robotic lab says the reason AI has not cured cancer is not the model. It is the kind of data the models were fed.

Vivodyne, spun out of the University of Pennsylvania and running labs in Philadelphia and San Francisco, builds more than 20 human tissue types on microfluidic chips and lets a robotic system called HIVE dose them and read the result. One unit tests 10,000 tissues at a time and returns data within one to two weeks, according to the company.

"Absent human testing, what are these models going to do?" co-founder Andrei Georgescu asked TechCrunch on August 19. "They're going to cure cancer in mice." His diagnosis is that today's cell data is all static snapshots, so a model learns that cell state A exists and that cell state B exists, but never that B is the effect of inflaming A.

A study in Nature Methods this June pretrained 400 models on a corpus of 22.2 million cells and found performance plateaued long before the corpus ran out. The paper stopped at documenting the plateau. Reading its cause as an absence of causality is the founder's move, not the authors'.

The unverified parts are easy to list. The 94% concordance with human trials in liver tissue is a company disclosure that has not been peer reviewed, and the eight large pharmaceutical companies said to be paying for early access are unnamed.

What we keep meeting in data quality work at Pebblous rhymes with this. However well you wash data that has already piled up, a field nobody recorded cannot be restored, and that choice gets made where the data is produced.

The question the company leaves behind is not how much data you hold. It is whether your records say that a state existed, or that changing one thing changed another.

## Facebook (EN)

"AI is going to cure cancer."

I started counting how many times I have heard that sentence and gave up.

On August 16, Dario Amodei put it a little differently. The line, he wrote, is now more cliché than inspiring, and most people think it is deceptive. That did not read like someone who had given up on optimism. It read like someone tired of the way optimism gets sold.

Three days later, a company attached something concrete to that fatigue.

"Absent human testing, what are these models going to do?"

Andrei Georgescu, co-founder of Vivodyne, asked and then answered himself. They are going to cure cancer in mice.

What he pointed at was not the model. It was the data. All of the cell data we have piled up, he says, is a photograph taken at one frozen moment. A model learns that this cell is in state A and that one is in state B. It never learns that B is what happened when something inflamed A.

A study in Nature Methods this June landed near the same spot. Four hundred models pretrained on 22.2 million cells, and performance stopped climbing well before the corpus was used up.

The paper did not name an absence of causality as the cause. The founder is the one who reads it that way. The temptation to weld the two together was strong, and leaving them apart felt more honest.

The company's answer was not a pipeline. It was a laboratory. More than 20 human tissue types grown on chips, with a robot dosing them and keeping before and after linked inside the same tissue.

Somewhere in the reading I started separating "a record of states" from "a record of interventions." Both are cell data. No amount of enlarging the first turns it into the second.

Plenty remains unconfirmed. The 94% concordance with human trials is a company disclosure that has not been peer reviewed, and the eight pharmaceutical companies said to have bought early access are unnamed.

What we run into in data quality work at Pebblous is not far from this. However carefully you wash data that has already accumulated, a field that was never written down does not come back. What gets kept is usually decided on the side that makes the data.

"Do our records say that some state existed, or that changing one thing changed another?"

Putting that question off and collecting more data is, for a while, quite comfortable.
