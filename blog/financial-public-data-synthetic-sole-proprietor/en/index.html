Executive Summary

The way the Korean government manufactures the sole proprietor records it publishes as open data is changing. From November 27 the Financial Services Commission adds six more financial public data APIs to the national public data portal, and three of them hold sole proprietor profile, financial statement and banking information that was invented rather than collected. Where the government used to delete names and trade names from the original ledger and send out what was left, it now sends out a ledger written from scratch that inherits only the statistical properties of the original.

The same kind of data from the same five institutions was opened once before, four years ago, by pseudonymization. The four sole proprietor APIs released then stay where they are and the three synthetic ones take their place alongside, bringing the field to seven APIs. The press release says the Financial Security Institute assessed whether the synthesis was adequate, but what it examined and against which standard does not appear in any public document. The Commission itself promised a year ago to put out a standard for using synthetic data by the first quarter of 2026, and this release does not point to that standard. In earlier research, credit scoring models trained on synthetic data scored lower than models trained on real records.

Sections 1 through 4 follow what the announcement and the published reporting state. What the missing review standard means, and the judgment in section 5, are this article's reading.

1

Three of the Six New APIs Carry Invented Numbers

The press release the Financial Services Commission issued on November 26 was titled "We are opening more AI-friendly financial public data." From Friday November 27, six APIs, six tables and sixty-four fields join the national public data portal. As of the end of October the financial public data holdings stood at 110 APIs and 349 tables, so this release takes the API count to 116.

The six APIs fall into four kinds. Korea Housing Finance Corporation contributes housing finance product data, which carries product names such as the Didimdol and Bogeumjari mortgage loans along with their sales periods, and securitization data, which carries securitization periods and asset transfer dates. The Commission itself opens financial regulation interpretation records as plain text, holding the question asked, the statutes involved, the gist of the answer and the reasoning behind it, a bundle offered explicitly for training language models. The remaining three are the sole proprietor synthetic data.

The three synthetic APIs differ in how many fields they carry. Profile data holds ten fields, among them the owner's gender and age band, the year of business registration and founding, the business region and industry, the headcount, and whether the business is suspended or closed. Financial statement data holds fourteen fields covering capital, revenue, operating profit, net profit, total liabilities and total assets. Banking data holds fourteen fields as well, covering average deposit and loan balances, guarantee balances, and whether the business carries working capital or facility loans. Added together that is thirty-eight, more than half of the sixty-four fields opening this time.

Five institutions supplied the data. Korea Credit Guarantee Fund, Korea Development Bank, Industrial Bank of Korea, Korea Housing Finance Corporation and Korea Inclusive Finance Agency each handed over the sole proprietor transaction records they hold. Instead of sending those records out as they are, the agencies ran them through a synthesis process, and the Commission's institution table names the Financial Security Institute as the body that assessed whether the result was adequate.

Usage figures came out alongside. As of the end of October the cumulative volume released stands at 4.42 billion records, API access applications at 93,088, roughly 2.7 times the 34,135 recorded at the end of last year, and data queries at 1.06 billion. The Commission said it will widen the opening again in 2027 in forms that are easy for AI to train on and use, with standardization and quality improvement carried out alongside.

The quietest passage in the whole announcement is the fact that the numbers in those three APIs are not the numbers of any real business. The revenue figure in a given row is not that shop's revenue, and no row pairs with a sole proprietor who exists. Yet the overall distribution and the relationships between fields are built to resemble the original, and that is the promise synthetic data makes. How well that promise was kept decides what this data is good for.

2

Four Years Ago They Masked the Rows, This Time They Rebuilt Them

The same kind of data from the same five institutions has been opened once already. In November 2022 the Financial Services Commission released four sole proprietor APIs as financial public data: profile, financial statement, banking and credit evaluation information. The method used then was plain pseudonymization. Fields that point straight at a person, such as the owner's name, the trade name and the business registration number, were dropped, and fields that can corner a person once you narrow them down, such as age, headcount or address, were blurred into bands before release.

This press release points at that method explicitly and says the new release moves beyond "plain pseudonymization." What changed is the direction of the process. Pseudonymization leaves an original row intact and erases or blurs part of it. The thread between a published row and a business that exists grows thin but never snaps. Synthesis does not send the original row out at all. It extracts the distributions and the relationships between fields from the original as a whole, then writes new rows that follow those properties. No published row has an original row behind it.

The two methods protect different things and give up different things. Pseudonymization preserves almost the entire shape of one original row, but match a few of its fields against another dataset and a path back to the person remains. Synthesis erases that path inside the process and in exchange guarantees nothing about the shape of any single original row. The diagram below draws out that fork.

A Korean government body building and publishing synthetic data is not itself a first. On May 30, 2024, the Personal Information Protection Commission published a reference model for generating synthetic data, releasing five sector sets across healthcare, public safety, retail and finance together with the generation process itself. The five were dental images, images of hard hat use, blood glucose measurements, telecom membership usage records, and corporate shareholder and representative information, and from June 3 companies and researchers could apply for them through the pseudonymized data support platform.

Where this release differs is in how you receive it. The reference model was a bundle handed over after an application and a check, while the sole proprietor synthetic data hangs permanently on the public data portal and can be pulled by anyone holding an API key. Synthetic data has taken a step toward a place where the side releasing it no longer watches who takes how much of it for what purpose. That is our reading, and the Commission has never explained the difference between the two routes.

2.1Only the Credit Evaluation API Stayed on the Pseudonymized Side

Three of the four APIs opened in 2022 gained a synthetic twin and one did not. No synthetic version was attached to the sole proprietor credit evaluation API. That leaves seven APIs in this field, four pseudonymized and three synthetic. The old method has not stepped back to make room for the new one; the two now sit next to each other on the portal.

Neither the announcement nor the follow-up coverage says why credit evaluation alone was left out. Credit grades and scores are values computed from the other fields, so inventing them while keeping the relationships between fields intact is presumably harder. That much is a guess, and no published document backs it. The Commission has given no reason to date.

What the Adequacy Review Looked At Is Not Public

The job assigned to the Financial Security Institute is written as a single line: "synthetic data adequacy assessment." It means a third party checked the output, which carries a different weight from an agency declaring its own work fine. What that one line checked, though, cannot be narrowed down from anything published.

The Financial Security Institute was designated a data specialized institution for the financial sector on August 6, 2020, together with Korea Credit Information Services. The amended Credit Information Act that took effect the day before introduced the concepts of pseudonymized and anonymized information, and handed designated institutions the work of combining data held by different organizations, pseudonymizing or anonymizing the result, and then assessing whether that processing was adequate. An adequacy review committee staffed with outside experts in law and technology examines whether the level of processing fits and whether any possibility of re-identification remains. By the end of 2024 the Institute had carried out 363 data combinations.

That review framework, however, is built on the premise that an original row is still there. The question "can anyone work out who this row is" only makes sense when the published row came from a person who exists. Synthetic data has no such correspondence, so the same question cannot simply be carried over. Assessments of synthetic data usually split along two axes. One is safety, also called privacy. It asks whether a published row can be traced back to someone who was in the original, or whether anyone can work out that a particular record was used in training. The other is utility. It asks whether the analysis you used to run on the original reaches the same conclusion when run on the synthetic version.

These two axes are not a distinction this article invented. The procedure the Personal Information Protection Commission followed in building its reference model is laid out that way. It ran in four stages, preparation, generation, utility and safety verification, and use. The utility side examined how closely the synthetic version resembled the original and whether it could reach the goal the original was meant to serve. The safety side examined the chance that an individual present in the original could be identified or inferred. The methods used included indistinguishability testing, which quantifies how well an AI can tell original from synthetic, and visual discrimination by domain experts, with regeneration and re-verification repeated until the target level was reached. The guide to generating and using synthetic data that followed in December 2024 inserted a review committee assessment as an extra step, making five in all, and stated that synthetic data released to the general public is verified with the weight placed on safety.

That guide, though, is a general one grounded in the Personal Information Protection Act. A standard for the financial sector had been announced separately. In a press release dated December 22, 2025, "Finance will lead the AI transformation", the Financial Services Commission wrote that it would set out concrete cautions and checklists for pseudonymization and anonymization in order to provide usage standards for unstructured data and synthetic data. It named a date as well: the standards would take effect during the first quarter of 2026 through amendments to the Credit Information Act enforcement decree and supervisory regulations and a revision of the financial sector guide to pseudonymization and anonymization. A footnote in the same document defined synthetic data as virtual data made by imitating the statistical properties of real data, pointing at the same spot as the phrase "maintains the statistical properties of the original" used in this month's release.

No public material settles whether those amendments actually arrived or whether this assessment followed that standard. This press release states neither the standard applied, nor which of the two axes the assessment examined, nor in what form the assessment result was recorded. A sentence the Financial Security Institute published in its 2025 data combination trends report sits in the same place. It said the Institute would support the safe use of data with high AI value, such as unstructured and synthetic data, which is written as future work rather than as a settled standard.

Sole proprietor data belongs to the harder end of the safety axis. A census-affiliated research team that built public-use synthetic microdata from the United States Annual Business Survey wrote that firm data is weaker in anonymity than individual data, because matching only the region and the industry already narrows the field to a particular establishment. The ten profile fields opening here include business region and industry, and a sole proprietor is a category in which the person and the business are one body. Safety verification is not finished until someone has also checked that the generator does not memorize and spit back the rare combinations in the original.

One thing forks here. Synthetic data that passes only the safety axis can earn the words "fit to release" but not the words "usable in place of the original." Conversely, a good score on the utility axis counts for nothing at release time if safety has not been confirmed. The receiving side has to know which guarantee the data carries before it can decide where to use it, and the sentence published today does not carry that distinction. That the distinction is needed is our judgment.

4

Will a Credit Model Trained on Synthetic Data Give the Same Answer?

The benefits the Financial Services Commission listed are specific. Credit scoring models built for small businesses and the self-employed, and tailored financial support policy. Both mean using the synthetic data as training material, which makes the question to ask equally clear. Does that model reach the same verdict as a model trained on the original?

The press release holds no figure that answers it. An expected benefit is an expectation rather than a measured result, and there is no mention of anything having been measured. Academia, however, has measured the same question already.

A paper Muñoz-Cancino and co-authors posted at the end of 2022, "Assessment of creditworthiness models privacy-preserving training with synthetic data", trained credit scoring models on synthetic data and measured how they performed on real data. The abstract reports a reduction of 3% of AUC and 6% of KS against models trained on real data. The authors read a loss of that size as small enough to keep credit risk research going while protecting borrowers' privacy.

Another result in the same paper sits closer to this release. The quality of the synthetic data got worse as the number of attributes grew. A reasonable reading is that more attributes means matching not only the distribution of each one but also the shape in which they tangle together. The financial statement and banking data opening here hold fourteen fields each, and values such as capital, revenue and total liabilities move in step with one another. The paper has never assessed this dataset; the link between the two is one we are drawing.

This series covered the same shape one article ago. In a study that re-measured the scaling laws of the tabular model TabPFN, the loss measured on synthetic data matched the prediction to within 0.001, while performance on real data came in 0.020 better than predicted. Measure inside the synthetic world and everything lines up; step one pace outside it and the numbers drift. Private research and public data release are standing in front of the same wall.

Getting over that wall takes a figure comparing synthetic against original on the same task, and this release leaves no place for such a figure to be produced. The original sits inside the five institutions, and whoever receives data through the portal receives only the synthetic version. The only parties able to train both models and compare them are the institutions holding the original, and no such comparison has been promised.

The method for comparing is already on the table. The census-affiliated team mentioned above built public-use synthetic microdata from the Annual Business Survey and the 2007 Survey of Business Owners, then, alongside quality metrics, re-ran one published journal analysis on the synthetic version. They checked whether the conclusion reached with the original could be reached with the synthetic data too. Done this way, a declaration that statistical properties were maintained turns into a checkable statement, because the analysis chosen as the benchmark and its result on the synthetic version are published together.

Applying the same thing here would not be hard. One of the five institutions picks an analysis it has already run on the original, reproduces it on the synthetic version, and publishes the two results side by side. A delinquency rate estimate for small businesses, a comparison of revenue distributions by industry, anything for which the original already has an answer. The suggestion is ours, not a plan the Commission has announced.

Why Pebblous Is Watching This

When the default on the releasing side moves from hiding to rebuilding, the seat responsible for quality moves with it. Pseudonymization is verified by looking at what was erased. Check the list of deleted fields and the width of the bands and you can gauge the risk that is left. Synthesis is verified by measuring what it resembles. There is no list of deletions, and resemblance cannot be confirmed by eye.

So synthetic data needs a report card attached. How far the distribution of each field overlaps the original, how much of the correlation between fields survived, and whether a model trained on the original and a model trained on the synthetic version hand the same business the same grade. These three are measurable values, and without measuring them the sentence about maintaining statistical properties stays a declaration with no way to check it.

None of this says the release is pointless. Opening self-employed data to research without sending out a single original row is something the method of four years ago could not do, and having a third party such as the Financial Security Institute inside the process is progress in itself. What is missing right now is writing down what that third party examined and by what measure, in a form the receiving side can read.

Anyone planning to build a small business credit scoring model on this data would do well to check three things first.

  • • Which axis was this data assessed on. Confirming there is no re-identification risk and confirming it can stand in for the original are different tests. The published sentence says only "adequacy" and does not separate the two
  • • Are the relationships between fields still alive. Measure the correlation of values that move together first, such as revenue against operating profit, or average loan balance against guarantee balance. In the earlier research the first place synthetic quality collapsed was the range with the most attributes
  • • Was the grading done entirely inside the synthetic world. Validate a synthetically trained model on synthetic data and the score comes out high. That number says how well the model memorized the rules the generator made, not how often it will be right about a real business

Editor's Note

We read seven articles covering this news and every one was a straight summary of the press release, with not a single piece carrying expert reaction or comparative analysis. Synthetic data entering public data release means the government's method of sending data out is itself changing, and the speed at which that change gets transcribed runs far ahead of the speed at which it gets verified. While digging through published material from the Financial Security Institute and the Financial Services Commission for a standard document behind the synthetic data adequacy assessment, we ran into a sentence the Commission wrote last December promising that standard by the first quarter of 2026. Whether the promised amendment arrived, and whether this assessment followed it, we could not confirm. The body that named a date a year ago and the body that opened this data are the same body, and the one sentence connecting them was never written down.
