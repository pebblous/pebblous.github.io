## LinkedIn (EN)

Two Chicago residents filed class actions on October 9 against Coco Robotics and Serve Robotics, the companies behind the delivery robots that run along the city's sidewalks.

The complaints describe the few seconds in which a customer walks up and opens the lid: a camera takes in the face and the hands, a LiDAR sensor measures their geometry in three dimensions, and what accumulates goes on to AI training. The sentence that sets these suits apart from the earlier face-data cases comes next. The robots, the plaintiffs say, also scan the faces, hands and bodies of people they merely pass on a Chicago sidewalk.

Illinois BIPA, the statute behind the claims, requires written notice and written consent before collection and sets damages of $1,000 or $5,000 per violation. Written consent works only where the other party can be named, and a person walking past has no such channel.

The two defenses differ in kind. Coco denies the facts, saying it has never used LiDAR in Chicago at all. Serve denies the purpose, saying the sensors exist to navigate and that its systems identify no one. The fight with Serve turns on whether a point cloud captured in order to drive counts as a biometric identifier, and a suit brought against X Corp under the same statute was dismissed in 2024.

In a robot team's inventory, LiDAR logs sit under sensor data. When the same files move into a training set, the use changes and the label stays. Part of what Pebblous does is trace a training set back to how its data was gathered.

## Facebook (EN)

A delivery robot gets out of your way.

Coming the other way on a sidewalk it slows down, and it tells a curb from a person from a utility pole as it picks a path. The navigation sensors on its lid are what do that work. On a Serve Robotics robot the cylinder up there holds a camera and a LiDAR together.

To avoid a person, it has to see the person. That sentence is the whole of these lawsuits.

On October 9 two Chicago residents filed class actions at the Cook County courthouse. One names Coco Robotics, the other Serve Robotics.

The scene both complaints describe is the few seconds in which a customer opens the lid and lifts out an order. A camera takes in the face, the head and the hands, and a LiDAR sensor measures their geometry in three dimensions.

Then the complaints add a sentence.

The robots also scan the faces, hands and bodies of other people they meet on Chicago sidewalks.

Not the person who placed the order. Not the person who came to the door. People who were walking down that street. Named in one line, that bottom row holds "the people off the list."

Illinois BIPA asks for written notice and written consent. You have to know who someone is before you can put a document in front of them, and only then does a place to refuse exist.

Behind a screen those conditions are usually in place. There is an account, there are terms. On the sidewalk they fall away one layer at a time, and the bottom layer has nothing left in it.

"What is a company supposed to ask of a person whose name it cannot know?"

Both defenses are still open. Coco says it has never used LiDAR in Chicago. Serve says its systems were not designed to create biometric profiles. No court has ruled.

Design intent and the nature of the output are different questions, though. Whether a point cloud taken right in front of a face lets someone recognize that face is answered by the data and not by a design document.
