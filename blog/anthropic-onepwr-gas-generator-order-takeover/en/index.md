# Did Anthropic Buy Electricity, or a Spot in the Turbine Line?

_The Financial Times reports that Anthropic paid about $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of Caterpillar generators that OnePWR had ordered two years earlier and could not finance_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> Anthropic has bought a fleet of heavy-duty gas generators outright for its data centers. The Financial Times reported it on November 8, and the deal itself was agreed in late September. The amount is about $3.4 billion and the capacity secured is 1.7 gigawatts. Anthropic did not order these generators, though. OnePWR Solutions, an equipment developer in San Antonio, Texas, had placed the order with Caterpillar some two years earlier and could not finance it, and Anthropic took that order over along with the engineering and construction capability needed to install it.

> At $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts, the price works out to $2,000 per kilowatt. A new gas turbine order in the United States waits an average of six years, according to Wood Mackenzie, and combined-cycle plants filed for 2030 and 2031 startup carry capital costs above $2,000 per kilowatt. The two numbers do not measure the same thing. One is the cost of building an entire plant; the other is what was attached to a generator order and the capability to install it. Even so, they show which band Anthropic's payment falls in. The power is expected to be usable within two years.

> Sections 1 through 4 follow the reported terms of the deal and the figures in public filings and industry counts. Section 5, which asks which ledger the money spent on data quality belongs in, is this article's own interpretation.

### Key Numbers

Of the four numbers below, the last two come from outside this deal. The global turbine backlog explains why the line for a new order grew so long, and the $895.4 billion is the size of the commitment book this $3.4 billion sits inside.

## What Anthropic Took Over

The deal itself is simple. Anthropic secured 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty gas generators built by Caterpillar for about $3.4 billion. The Financial Times reported it citing a person familiar with the matter, and TechCrunch and Tom's Hardware, among others, picked it up. Anthropic and OnePWR both declined to comment, and Reuters said it could not independently verify the report. Most of what follows rests on that reporting.

One record exists outside the reporting as well. Piper Sandler, the investment bank that advised OnePWR on the sale, lists a transaction on its own deal page dated September 26, 2026. The client is OnePWR Solutions, what was sold is a "power generation platform," the buyer is described only as "a frontier AI lab," and the value is $3.4 billion. The buyer's name is not there. The date, the amount and the seller all match the reporting, but the part that says the buyer is Anthropic still rests on the Financial Times alone.

What stands out is that the direction of the deal changed partway through. Anthropic was originally going to be a OnePWR customer. OnePWR Solutions, founded in 2025 in San Antonio, develops behind-the-meter power plants and microgrids. Instead of buying electricity, Anthropic took the equipment order OnePWR had placed with Caterpillar. The engineering and construction capability needed to erect those generators came with it, and OnePWR is helping with the installation work at Anthropic's data centers.

The company itself was not acquired. OnePWR remains in place and none of its staff moved to Anthropic. What changed hands was a production slot locked in roughly two years ago and the hands to turn that slot into working generation. Another of the terms is that the order predates the recent round of price increases.

> [!callout]
> The Financial Times called this the largest known single purchase of power generation equipment by an AI lab. Which data center the generators go to is not in the report. On the reported schedule, the power arrives in stages over the next two years, beginning shortly.

## From Electricity Customer to Equipment Owner

OnePWR's original plan shows how far this moved. In March 2026 Caterpillar, OnePWR and Vero3 announced a joint structure: natural gas generation bundled with carbon capture, battery storage and permanent underground storage of the captured carbon dioxide, supplied to data centers. The roles were split three ways. Caterpillar handled the generation equipment, the controls and the basic design of the capture plant. Vero3 stored the captured carbon dioxide and managed the related tax credits. OnePWR built, owned and operated the generation and sold power to customers under long-term contracts. A first project of 500 megawatts had been announced.

In that structure the AI company's place is the customer paying the electricity bill. Who owns and runs the generators is the supplier's problem, and the customer takes the contracted power. That was the place Anthropic was going to stand in. Then the financing stalled and the boundary between supplier and customer moved. Anthropic had the money to take hold of the equipment, and OnePWR had the spot inside the line. The March announcement did not name Anthropic, and there is still no confirmation that the generators now changing hands go to that 500-megawatt site.

Holding the power directly brings its own exposure. In the amended IPO filing of November 2, Anthropic wrote that certain of its planned data center facilities "rely on dedicated on-site natural gas-fired generation, which exposes us to fuel price volatility and supply disruptions that are outside of our control and for which we bear the cost on a pass-through basis." The filing adds that it carries those fuel costs without cost caps. A company that buys electricity watches only the bill. A company that holds the generators puts gas prices and the fuel supply chain on its own books.

## The Six-Year Line and the Price of Skipping It

The numbers explain why the wait for generation equipment grew so long. Wood Mackenzie counts the global turbine order backlog at about 110 gigawatts against annual manufacturing capacity of 60 to 70 gigawatts. Orders in the second quarter of 2026 alone reached 38 gigawatts, up 71% from a year earlier and a record. GE Vernova's contracted volume added 11 gigawatts in the third quarter, going from 116 to 122 gigawatts, and the company plans to lift output capacity from 20 gigawatts a year to 24 in 2028 and 30 in 2030. The volume already on the books has sold several years of production. That is where the Wood Mackenzie analyst's figure of six years for a new turbine in the United States comes from.

There is a counterview. Scott Strazik, GE Vernova's chief executive, says turbines are not the only bottleneck in data center construction. Building a single gas plant usually takes three years, and 80% of the company's backlog came from traditional customers rather than data centers. Delivery estimates differ by source as well. The Electric Power Research Institute puts large turbines at five years and small ones at 18 to 36 months. Six years is an average for the US market, not a number attached to every machine.

So the industry looks outside the line. Used turbines in good condition trade at $1.5 million to $1.7 million per megawatt, against $2 million to $2.5 million new. In one brokered sale two turbines totaling 29 megawatts went for $12.5 million, where the same capacity new would have cost about $35 million and a five-year wait, by the comparison the report of that sale drew. That sale prices out at $430,000 per megawatt, far below the quoted range, because the class of machine is different. The $1.5 million range is for the large turbines data centers want, while the two units on the brokerage market were 14.5 megawatts each. Converting retired jet engines is another route. Crusoe, an AI infrastructure company, ordered 13 units of 50 megawatts in April, 650 megawatts in all. Anthropic's path is neither of these. It is a third route: buying someone else's place inside the line outright.
