바다에 데이터센터를 숨겨 AI 규제를 비켜 가는 길은, 계산해 보면 쉬운 쪽이 아니라 더 어려운 쪽이었다. 9월 16일 arXiv에 올라온 논문이 H100 10만 장급 학습 한 번을 기준선으로 잡고 그 계산을 했다.

전력과 냉각은 벽이 아니었다. 해저 고압직류 케이블은 이미 훨씬 큰 부하를 나르고, 수심 11m에 잠겼던 마이크로소프트 나틱은 냉각에 들어가는 전력을 시설 전력의 3%까지 낮췄다. 육상 데이터센터는 같은 일에 전력의 4분의 1 이상을 써 왔다.

막은 것은 사람 손이다. 메타가 공개한 라마 3 학습 기록에는 54일 사이 예상 밖 중단이 419번 적혀 있다. 평균 세 시간에 한 번이고, 복구는 트랜시버를 뽑아 다시 꽂는 식의 손일이 흔했다. 봉인된 포드 안에서는 그 손이 닿지 않는다.

탐지 쪽 결론은 비대칭이다. 돌고 있는 포드는 열로도 소리로도 잘 잡히지 않는다. 열린 연안의 물살이면 표면 수온 이상이 바다의 자연 변동폭에 묻히고, 변압기가 내는 좁은 대역은 배와 해상 풍력발전기와 양수장도 함께 낸다. 정작 잘 보이는 것은 짓고 고치는 시기다. 압력용기 제작은 이름이 알려진 큰 조선소 몇 곳에 몰려 있고, 고압 전력 케이블을 깔 수 있는 배는 전 세계 20~30척에 그친다. 그 배들은 상업 선박이라 자기 위치를 송신한다.

그래서 저자들의 결론은 위험 경고가 아니다. 광산에 숨긴 지하 시설이나 평범한 산업 건물로 위장한 육상 시설이 하드웨어를 손보기 훨씬 쉬우니, 물속은 조약을 회피하기에 오히려 더 어려운 길이라는 것이다. 조약이 닿는 거리는 조문이 아니라 측정이 정한다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

A data center hidden on the seabed turns out to be the harder way to evade AI rules, not the easier one. A paper posted to arXiv on September 16 fixed a baseline and did the arithmetic: one training run equivalent to 100,000 H100s, carried out underwater.

Power and cooling are not the wall. Subsea high-voltage DC cables already move far larger loads, and Microsoft's Natick pod, sitting at a depth of 11 metres, held cooling to 3 percent of facility power. Land-based data centers have historically spent a quarter or more of their power on the same job.

Hands are the wall. Meta's published record for Llama 3 logs 419 unexpected interruptions across 54 days, roughly one every three hours, and recovery often meant reseating a transceiver by hand. Inside a sealed pod, nobody can reach it.

The detection findings run the same asymmetry. A pod that is already running is faint in heat and ambiguous in sound: open-coast currents bury the surface anomaly inside normal variability, and the transformer tone at twice mains frequency is shared by ships, offshore wind turbines and pumping stations. Construction and maintenance are a different matter. Pressure-vessel work sits with a short list of large, well-known yards, only 20 to 30 ships worldwide can lay high-voltage power cable, and those ships are commercial vessels that broadcast their own position.

So the authors do not end on a warning. A site buried in a mine, or a plant disguised as ordinary industry, is far easier to service, which makes the water the harder route for cheating on a treaty. A treaty reaches exactly as far as its measurements do.

## Facebook (EN)

"Once every three hours."

That is how often something broke unexpectedly while Meta trained Llama 3. The published log counts 419 interruptions across 54 days, and the repair was often as physical as pulling a transceiver out and pushing it back in.

Hold that interval up against a data center sealed inside a pressure vessel and lowered onto the seabed, and the phrase "hard to service" starts to mean something specific.

A paper posted to arXiv on September 16 worked through exactly that. Could you build frontier AI in the ocean to get out from under a treaty, and if you did, could anyone outside tell?

Power was not the obstacle. Microsoft's Natick pod, sitting 11 metres down, held cooling to 3 percent of facility power, so cooling was an advantage rather than a cost. The obstacle turned out to be a pair of human hands that cannot reach through steel.

The half I keep returning to is detection. A running pod is hard to hear, and not because it is quiet. The transformer hum sits at twice the mains frequency, the same narrow band as ships, offshore wind turbines and pumping stations. You can hear it and still have no idea whose it is. I have started calling that the attribution wall.

Data quality work stalls in the same place. Noticing that a value jumped is the quick part. Telling a sensor fault from a process change takes lineage, and without lineage the alerts pile up while nothing gets done.

"What share of the numbers you receive could you actually go back and measure again?"

One recommendation in the paper stayed with me. Write into the treaty that states accept each other's small hydrophones and autonomous vehicles in the waters where such a facility could sit. The point is not the sensors. The point is that refusing the clause becomes evidence in itself. A data contract can borrow the same design: put audit access and sample re-measurement into the terms up front, and the suppliers who cannot meet them filter themselves out.

Neither treaties nor data seem to run on promises. They run on what can be measured.
