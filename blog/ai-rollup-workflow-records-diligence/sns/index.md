## Facebook (EN)

At the bottom of a tax return there is a line for a person's name.

The accountant who writes it there carries the legal liability.

That signature line stayed in front of me while I read an announcement this week. An agent built by Thrive Holdings' accounting arm with OpenAI had processed more than 7,000 tax returns at 98% accuracy. In the same document, the company was explicit that professional sign-off is one thing AI does not replace.

Put the two sentences side by side and what remains is not a result but a question.

"Which returns are the other 2%? Who finds them, and when?"

Out of 7,000, that is at least 140. If nobody marks which 140, the pile in front of the person about to sign is still 7,000 returns. The labor of producing a return did not disappear. It moved to the labor of checking one that has been produced.

Writing this piece, I kept coming back to the phrase "work records." Buying an accounting firm brings more than a client roster and staff. It brings the returns, the workpapers, and the revision histories the firm left behind over years. None of that is listed as an asset in the purchase agreement. And yet it is the only place a ground truth exists for judging whether the agent got it right.

Those are the properties Pebblous means when it talks about AI-Ready Data. Which version was the final submission, where a person changed it, and why. A firm that kept those histories and a firm that did not are unlikely to fetch the same price, even with the same agent installed.

So what does a strategy that buys 70 companies actually buy. The companies, or what the companies wrote down.
