이유를 물을 권리가 없어서가 아니다. 한국 개인정보 보호법 37조의2는 2024년부터 완전히 자동화된 결정을 거부하고 그 이유를 설명받을 권리를 정했고, EU도 GDPR 22조와 AI Act 86조로 같은 보호를 세웠다.

문제는 이 권리들이 하나같이 '완전 자동화'라는 좁은 문턱 위에 서 있다는 점이다. 기업이 AI를 보조수단으로 두고 마지막에 사람이 한 번만 개입하면, 대부분의 보호는 그 순간 발동 요건에서 벗어난다. 개인정보보호위원회 스스로 사람이 개입하면 사실상 거부권 행사가 어렵다는 한계를 인정한 바 있다.

시간표까지 어긋났다. EU는 2026년 5월 채용·복지를 포함한 고위험 AI 의무의 적용을 2027년 12월로 16개월 미뤘다. 권리가 법에 적히는 것과 그 권리가 실제로 발동하는 것 사이에는 여전히 거리가 있다.

그 거리를 메우는 것은 결정의 근거를 나중에 다시 꺼내 볼 수 있게 하는 데이터 인프라다. 데이터가 충분히 좋았는가라는 공급자의 질문 다음에, 그 결정을 개인이 되물을 수 있는가라는 데이터 주체의 질문이 온다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Over the past two years, 351 job seekers were rejected by AI hiring exams at 17 public institutions across Korea. Most went on to their next application without ever learning why.

It is not that the right to ask is missing. Korea's Personal Information Protection Act, in force since 2024, grants the right to refuse a fully automated decision and to be told the reason. The EU built the same protection into GDPR Article 22 and AI Act Article 86.

Each of these rights, though, rests on a single word: solely. A right fires only when a decision was made entirely by machine. Let an employer keep AI as an aid and add one human signature at the end, and the protection quietly falls out of scope. Korea's own privacy regulator has admitted that once a person is involved, opting out becomes hard to exercise.

The timing makes it sharper. In May 2026 the EU pushed the high-risk obligations covering hiring and welfare back by 16 months, to December 2027. Writing a right into law and having it actually take effect remain two different things.

What closes that gap is the data infrastructure that lets a decision's basis be pulled back up later. After the supplier's question, was the data good enough, comes the subject's question: can the person question the decision it produced?

## Facebook (EN)

"I was rejected by the AI hiring exam."

Over the past two years, 351 people in Korea received a line like that. Most of them never found out why they got the score they did.

It is not that the right to ask is missing. That right is already written into law. Korea's privacy act has said, since 2024, that you may refuse a fully automated decision and be told the reason. The EU says much the same.

The trouble comes right after. Each of these rights stands on a single narrow doorway called "solely automated."

Let a company filter you out with AI and then add one human name at the very end, and the decision is no longer "solely automated." In that instant, the right to question it quietly slips out of reach.

If a single sentence, "a person looked at it once at the end," can dissolve your standing to ask why you were turned down, was that right ever really yours?

In Australia, an automated system charged welfare recipients with debts and asked them to prove, themselves, that they owed nothing. A court called it unlawful and the government paid back more than a billion dollars. In the Netherlands, and in Michigan, the failures shared one thing. The person on the receiving end had no way to trace the decision back to its reasons.

So lately I find that the question of what good data means has shifted by one step. From was the data accurate, to can the decision it produced be pulled back up and looked at again. That is close to why Pebblous keeps looking at how data's origin and path get recorded. The right to question a decision finds its answer, in the end, in the data that decision left behind.
