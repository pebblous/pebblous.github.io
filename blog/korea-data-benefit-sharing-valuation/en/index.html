Executive Summary

The idea of returning part of the money made from data to the people who supplied it has reached a government table. On November 26, 2026, Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission (PIPC) discussed a data benefit sharing proposal at the fifth plenary meeting of the 2026 Personal Data Future Forum. Choi Sun-mi, a principal researcher at the Electronics and Telecommunications Research Institute (ETRI), proposed a sharing model tied to Korea's MyData scheme and a pilot in fields that address social problems, such as elder care and emergency transport.

The participants' discussion spent longer on what has to come before the scheme than on the scheme itself. The press release recorded that members agreed on two prerequisites before benefit sharing can work in practice: a fair and transparent basis for calculating the value of data, and an incentive for companies to join voluntarily. Pricing comes before sharing, and on the pricing side a system already exists. Article 14 of the Framework Act on the Promotion of the Data Industry and Use of Data, known as the Data Industry Act, has carried a data valuation regime since April 2022. Article 20 of the same act set up a data quality certification regime alongside it, and three certification bodies were designated in July 2023.

Settled and unsettled are worth separating first. Data benefit sharing is not an act, not a decree, and not even a bill. The data valuation regime, by contrast, has designated bodies and recognized methods and is in its fourth year of operation. Sections 1 through 4 set out what the press release, the statutes and the day's reporting confirm. Sections 5 and 6, which work out what those methods demand of a data team, are this article's own reading.

1

What reached the forum is a proposal and two conditions

The 2026 Personal Data Future Forum, run by the PIPC, is a discussion body of some 40 experts drawn from academia, the legal profession, industry and civil society. The fifth plenary meeting on November 26 also brought in the commission's 2030 advisory group. Two items were on the agenda that day, and the second is the one taken up below.

  • Reforming the personal data regime for the age of AI. Presented by Choi Yoon-jung, director of the PIPC's Personal Data Protection Policy Division. Generative AI and AI agents are changing how personal data is processed, so the argument runs, and the current system built on formal prior consent needs to become a substantive, risk-proportionate one.
  • A data benefit sharing proposal. Presented by Choi Sun-mi, a principal researcher in ETRI's Technology Management Research Division. The presentation set out a sharing model tied to the MyData scheme and suggested piloting it first in fields close to social problems, such as elder care and emergency transport.

A footnote in the press release defines the scheme this way: a system that returns a set portion of the revenue or benefit generated by the use of personal data to the data subject, so as to raise public trust and participation. The party giving back is the company that used the data, and the party receiving is the individual who owns it. What comes back may be money or it may be a non-monetary benefit. That is as far as the published sentences go.

On the discussion that followed, the press release recorded two points of agreement. One is a fair and transparent basis for calculating the value of data; the other is an incentive structure that brings companies in voluntarily. Neither one is the substance of the scheme; each is a condition for the scheme to work. Deciding how much to return requires being able to put a figure on the data, and while no law compels anyone, companies need a reason to walk in on their own.

Those two conditions say where the weight of the discussion sat. The forum gathered around a question one step ahead of "let us share the proceeds," namely "we have to be able to price it before we can share it." Valuation is a precondition of benefit sharing before it is a subsidiary procedure within it.

Closing the meeting, Chairperson Song Kyoung-hee said that the forum had made it possible "to discuss broadly the tasks our society must think through together, from shifting to a preventive management framework for personal data, to redefining personal data for the age of AI, to the institutional reform we discussed today." The chairperson added that the themes covered so far would feed into future policy design. The phrase about feeding into policy design is the most accurate marker of the current stage.

2

No statute and no covered parties yet

The first thing to settle when reading regulatory news is whether it is an obligation. Data benefit sharing is not. There is no promulgated statute, no decree out for public comment, and no bill before the National Assembly. Nothing anywhere yet answers the question of who must return how much, starting when.

The publicly visible track so far has two stops. The first is a seminar on introducing data benefit sharing, held by the Ministry of the Interior and Safety on June 29, 2026 at the National Information Society Agency (NIA) in Seoul. The PIPC, the Financial Services Commission and the NIA took part. Professor Bang In-sik of the Seoul National University of Science and Technology presented on domestic and overseas trends in benefit sharing, and ETRI's Choi Sun-mi presented on data distribution and trading cases in fintech and related sectors, with policy recommendations. The second stop is the PIPC forum on November 26. Since the same presenter appeared at both, June and November read as one continuing line of discussion.

A remark at the June seminar by Hwang Gyu-chul, head of the AI Government Office at the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, records the stage most plainly.

Data benefit sharing is an idea that can improve the sustainability of the data ecosystem, but turning it into an actual institution requires careful design and sufficient social consensus.

The remark sets the word "idea" against the word "institution" inside a single sentence. The ministry said it would analyze the scheme's effectiveness on the basis of the seminar's recommendations and draw on it for data industry strategy and for the direction of legal reform. That is a review schedule, not a legislative one.

More than one ministry owns the file, which makes the timing harder to predict. The Ministry of the Interior and Safety is driving the design; the PIPC handles the personal data side; the Financial Services Commission covers the MyData and fintech link; the NIA provides working-level support. With the Ministry of Science and ICT, which runs the data valuation regime, four or five ministries are involved. For the proposal to become a bill, those ministries have to sign the same document.

The distance between the two regimes shows up the moment they sit on one page. The top row below carries four stops: an effective date, a guideline and two designation notices. The bottom row carries two, a seminar and a forum.

3

The Data Industry Act already asks about value and quality

The "basis for calculating the value of data" that the forum named as a condition does not have to be built from bare ground. The Framework Act on the Promotion of the Data Industry and Use of Data took effect on April 20, 2022, and Article 14 gives the Minister of Science and ICT the authority to designate data valuation bodies that appraise the economic value of data. Results come out as a monetary amount, a grade or a score.

3.1Three valuation methods

In July 2022 the Ministry of Science and ICT issued the Guideline on the Designation and Operation of Data Valuation Bodies, setting out the methods and the designation and operating procedures. Three methods are recognized, and they may be used singly or in combination.

  • Market approach. Estimates value from the prices at which comparable data has changed hands.
  • Income approach. Estimates the economic benefit the data will generate and discounts it to present value.
  • Cost approach. Takes as its basis what it would cost to build the data again.

3.2Four designated valuation bodies

Fourteen organizations applied to the call that closed in late October 2022, and on March 2, 2023 the Ministry of Science and ICT designated four as the first data valuation bodies: the Korea Technology Finance Corporation (KIBO), NICE D&B, the Korea Credit Guarantee Fund (KODIT) and the Korea Institute of Science and Technology Information (KISTI). The designation criteria call for a standing unit of at least ten people, including six specialists among them professional engineers, lawyers and staff with data experience, plus valuation models and facilities. The ministry said at the time that it would consider further designations in light of how the regime operated and what the market demanded; whether any further designation followed could not be confirmed.

The regime did not stop at a declaration; it carries a budget. According to press reports, the 2025 data valuation support program ran to 560 million won in total and covered half the appraisal cost for 70 companies, up to 15 million won each. Forty-five of those companies sought appraisals for guarantees and loans, and 25 for investment and trading. Documents that put a price on data are already being handed across financial counters.

3.3The same act also measures quality

Pricing is not the only thing the Data Industry Act set up. Article 20 of the same act covers data quality management and quality certification, and that side is running too. The structure is that the Minister of Science and ICT designates quality certification bodies, and those bodies take applications from companies, examine the quality of the data and certify it. An amendment to the enforcement decree that took effect on January 3, 2023 filled in the designation criteria and the certification procedure.

The decree already writes down what gets examined. Article 20-5 divides the object of certification into the content of the data, its structure and its management framework, and fixes the quality criteria for each as follows. The detailed criteria for each item are set by notice of the Minister of Science and ICT.

  • Content of the data. Completeness, validity, accuracy.
  • Structure of the data. Consistency.
  • Management framework. Usability, accessibility.

The bodies that will issue the certificates are settled as well. On April 20, 2023 the Ministry of Science and ICT issued the Guideline on the Designation and Operation of Data Quality Certification Bodies and opened a call, and by public notice on July 6 of the same year it designated three: CAS Co., Ltd., WiseStone Inc. and the Telecommunications Technology Association (TTA). In February 2025 the ministry and the Korea Data Agency published a data quality certification policy guide setting out the certification procedure and the review criteria by object of certification.

So the Data Industry Act holds two layers. Article 14 asks how much the data is worth; Article 20 asks whether it is any good. The infrastructure that benefit sharing would have to lean on is already laid on both sides, not only the pricing one. What does not exist is any provision making the two regimes presuppose each other: nothing says certification is a prerequisite for appraisal. For now the line between them belongs to practice rather than to law.

The two regimes do not measure the same thing, though. Data valuation measures the worth of a company's data asset as a whole, while benefit sharing has to ask what share of that worth belongs to one particular individual. A dataset built from the records of 100,000 people may carry a price of 10 billion won without it following that one person's share is 100,000 won, because one person's contribution is not the same as another's. The existing regimes can serve as infrastructure for benefit sharing without being the answer to it. That judgment is Pebblous's, not the forum's.

4

Paying individuals directly is rare abroad as well

The first presentation at the June seminar was reported as covering domestic and overseas trends, but the text of that presentation was not obtained. The two cases below were looked up independently, and nothing establishes that they match what was presented.

4.1The EU Data Act settles compensation between companies only

The EU Data Act, applicable since September 12, 2025, grants rights of access and portability over data generated by connected devices. It also has a compensation clause. Article 9 provides that compensation agreed between a data holder and a data recipient must be non-discriminatory and reasonable and may include a margin, while for small and medium-sized enterprises and non-profit research organizations it may not exceed the costs directly related to making the data available. Article 9(7) further requires the data holder to set out the basis for the compensation in enough detail for the recipient to verify it.

That compensation runs between companies, however. A user accessing data from their own device is in principle free of charge, and there is no clause directing payment to that user. What the EU designed is not a dividend to individuals but a structure that keeps data from being locked inside one company. It aims at a different place from the "return to the data subject" under discussion in Korea.

4.2California's data dividend stopped at a proposal

Precedents where paying individuals directly became an institution are hard to find. The most widely cited case, California's data dividend, was raised by Governor Gavin Newsom in a first State of the State address on February 12, 2019. The intent was that residents should share in the wealth created from their data, but the speech contained no design. Whether it would be a tax on companies or a rebate to individuals was undecided, and it never became law.

What that precedent offers Korea's discussion is that design, not direction, is the whole of it. California stopped less because many opposed giving something back than because nobody could write down how much would be returned on what basis. The forum members on November 26 putting a valuation basis first reads as the result of seeing the same wall. That connection is an inference, though, and no record confirming that the California case came up at the forum was found.

5

Each of the three methods asks for different records

What follows translates the demands of the regime into the work of a data team; it is not written in any press release or statute. The starting point is a simple question. If you were asked tomorrow to prove what your data is worth, what would you put forward? The answer depends on which valuation method is in play.

Using the market approach requires something to compare against. Harder than finding out what comparable data sold for is saying that your data is the same kind of thing. How many records, what period they cover, how often they are refreshed, how much is missing or duplicated: without a specification setting that out, no comparison holds. An organization whose data catalog records only a row count has to build that specification right before the appraisal.

The income approach goes further. Estimating the benefit the data will generate means first showing what it has generated so far, which in turn means knowing which models and which services used this data. In an organization where data lineage is broken, "this data lifted our revenue" stays a claim. The premises of the estimate hang on the same thing. Every formula for future benefit rests on an assumption that the data will remain usable, and what holds that assumption up is quality test results.

The cost approach looks simplest and is in practice the one most often left empty. Few organizations have written down, dataset by dataset, how much went into collection, how many people spent how many days on processing, and what review cost. Accounting costs are bundled by department or by project, and splitting them back out to a single dataset requires a work history to exist.

Overlay what the three methods demand and four items remain. Where did this data come from (source and legal basis for collection), what processing has it been through (processing history), is it fit to use (quality test results), and where has it been used (usage records)? A regulator's paperwork and a valuation body's paperwork may look like different documents, but the questions underneath overlap. And none of the four is the kind of thing that can be built in a month on the eve of an appraisal.

These four questions are not an arbitrary list. Laid over the six quality criteria in the enforcement decree seen in section 3.3, they line up almost exactly. Completeness, validity and accuracy are held up by quality test results; consistency can only be spoken to if a processing history exists. Usability and accessibility in the management framework show themselves in who actually used the data and for what. Only source and legal basis for collection sit outside the quality criteria, and that is a place the Personal Information Protection Act asks for separately. The items the law records as certification criteria and the items a valuation body would ask for on paper are scattered across different documents while pointing at the same thing.

If benefit sharing does become an institution, one more question attaches here: the basis for dividing an individual share rather than a total value. The MyData link raised at the forum may be a way around that question, because when individuals move their own data along a dedicated path, who supplied what remains on the record transmission by transmission. This is an inference about the design intent of the proposal, though, and the press release said nothing further about the content of the linked model.

The transmission path itself is already past the proposal stage. The Personal Information Protection Act amended in March 2023 introduced a right to request transmission of personal data, and after a transition period it took effect across all sectors on March 13, 2025. Notices covering transmission in the telecommunications and healthcare sectors took effect the same day. Both forms are there: a request to receive one's own data, and a request to have it sent to a third party. The plumbing for dividing a share is laid; what is empty is the part that decides what and how much flows back through it.

6

Why Pebblous is watching this

Putting a price on data is a place Pebblous has looked at for a long time. "Pricing Data" covered a patent for proving a dataset's contribution inside a virtual environment alongside Data Shapley and related contribution-measurement methods. The question then was who proves the value of data in an agent economy, and how; this proposal poses the same question as a matter of institutional design.

There is no need to overstate how close the two are. The problem contribution-measurement research addresses and the three methods a valuation body uses differ in precision and in purpose. The research side measures how much a single data point adds to model performance; the institutional side attaches an amount of money to one data asset. Still, the two clearly stand before the same wall. Dividing contribution requires a record of what went in and what came out.

That is why now, while the regime is unsettled, is a good time to take stock. Whether benefit sharing becomes law, and how many years out if it does, nobody knows. What can be checked today is whether your own data register answers the four questions in section 5. Pick one dataset and write down its source and legal basis for collection, its processing history, the date of the last quality test, and the list of models and services that used it. An organization that can fill all four will not be late when an appraisal arrives; where one is blank, that gap is not the kind that can be filled after the regime is settled. For outside confirmation of those four, the Article 20 quality certification is a counter that is already open.

Set this beside another document from the same regulator three days earlier and the contrast sharpens. The draft amendment to the Enforcement Decree of the Personal Information Protection Act, out for public comment on November 23, came with an effective date of March 9, 2027 and a list of obligations, a settled track covered in "Korea's PIPA AI Exception Lets You Train on Raw Data If You Can Prove It." The benefit sharing discussed three days later at the same commission is a proposal that names neither a date nor the parties it would cover. Reading the two documents with equal weight throws the schedule off. Where this meets data quality standards is collected in the ISO/IEC 5259 series, and the articles on the legal foundations of data sit in the Data Economy hub.
