# micro1 Wants to Buy Your Work Records. Do You Have the Right to Sell?

_micro1 is spending $1 billion over 12 months on operating manuals, internal documents and customer records. Confirming the right to sell is left to the seller._

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> This article reads the $1 billion enterprise data purchase that the AI data company micro1 announced on 9 October through a single question. Does the seller hold the right to sell? The target is not text lying around on the web but the record a company builds while it works. Work standards and in-house knowledge bases, internal documents, CRM records, project histories, quality checks, and the traces a team leaves of what it decided and why are all on the list.

> The window is the next 12 months, and Citi and Hercules Capital are funding it. The payments fall into three tiers. The phrase micro1 writes into its participation requirements, though, is "internal approval processes and permissions", and nothing in it asks a company to go and collect consent from the third parties tangled up in those records, customers among them. Establishing the right to sell stays on the selling side.

> Everything to this point is written in the press release, in micro1's own program page and in the reporting that worked through them. The diagram in Section 3, which lays three parties over a single row of CRM data, and the reading in Section 5 that takes the announcement as the point where a price was attached to a conversation about quality standards, are this article's own interpretation.

## micro1's $1 Billion Commitment on 9 October

micro1 builds and gathers the data used to teach AI models and agents. Until early 2025 it ran an AI recruiting service with about $7 million in annual revenue, then turned toward data, and it now gets named as a rival to Scale AI. On 9 October it announced a purchasing program called Company Data Partnerships, under which it will put $1 billion into buying or licensing enterprise operational data over the next 12 months. Citi and Hercules Capital are funding it.

The shopping list is specific. Standard operating procedures and internal knowledge bases, internal documents, CRM data, project histories, quality assurance processes, records of team decision-making, and the feedback people attach to what an AI produces. Finance and accounting, customer support, sales, legal and logistics are the kinds of work it gives as examples, all of them areas where the day-to-day practice is concrete. With the supply of web documents a model can still learn from running down, the new part of this announcement is that the suppliers being called on are ordinary working companies rather than companies that hold content, the way Reddit or Stack Overflow do.

The size of the sum shows up against the deals content holders have signed. Paying for training data is itself a pattern that has been running since 2024. Google's agreement with Reddit is reported at around $60 million a year, and a separate OpenAI agreement is said to be of a similar size. The $1 billion micro1 has put against 12 months would take more than ten deals of that size to reach. That is money you cannot spend by signing a few large agreements with content holders, which is why the supply has to come from many sources at once.

> [!callout]
> The sources are worth separating once here. What the press release itself states runs to the amount, the window, the de-identification, the funding and the name of the program. The price tiers, the eligibility requirements and the ownership sentence in Section 2 below come not from the press release but from the program page micro1 maintains, and secondary coverage carried them across. Reading this as a press release that announced a price list gets the facts wrong.

## What micro1 Buys, and at What Price

Payment splits into three bands according to the scale and the scarcity of the data. The table below sets out the bands micro1 published and the condition attached to each.

| Band | Amount | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic | $100K and above | Qualified enterprise data partnerships |
| Expanded | $500K and above | Large-scale datasets, or several teams taking part on an ongoing basis |
| Proprietary | $1M and above | Highly unique, proprietary operational data that is hard to find elsewhere |

Read on its own, the table looks like a guarantee of at least $100K. The illustrative figure on the payout estimator micro1 put up on the same page, though, runs from $50,284 to $92,776, short of the lowest band in the table. The company itself writes that the real amount moves with the sources of the data, its size, its quality, the access terms and the result of due diligence. The three bands are not what you will receive. They are the thresholds attached to a partnership that qualifies, and whether yours reaches that mark is settled once due diligence is over.

Nor is participation open to everyone. A company needs more than 30 employees and documentation with some structure to it, US companies come first and other Western markets after that, and the material has to be in English. Read from a Korean company's side, these requirements are somebody else's business for now, but the direction they point in is plain. Only a company whose everyday work survives as documents, and whose documents are organized well enough to be found, can get in line.

## Ownership and the Right to Sell Are Not the Same Thing

micro1 has an answer ready for the first worry that surfaces when someone is asked to hand data over: companies retain ownership of their underlying data, personally identifiable information is removed when relevant, certain datasets go through synthetic rewrites meant to loosen the direct ties to the original records, a representative sample can be reviewed before taking part, and on request or at the end of an engagement the data is deleted.

Pull a single word out of that explanation and the shape of the problem appears. Ownership settles whose file it is. A license right settles whether you can pass it to somebody else to use. The two do not sit on the same level. A box in your own warehouse is still not yours to sell if the goods inside belong to someone else.

One row of CRM data makes that difference sharp. In the row that records a single deal, at least three parties overlap.
