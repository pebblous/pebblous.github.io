Executive Summary

A dataset has arrived that promises to teach a model to find personal data while holding none of it. Nemotron PII Japan, which NVIDIA published in Tokyo, is 100,000 fake documents modelled on forty-three Japanese forms drawn from eight fields: healthcare and finance, then public administration, employment, education, law, telecom and IT, and e-commerce. The names, addresses and account numbers inside belong to no living person, and every one of those spots is marked with the kind of personal data it holds. It went out under CC BY 4.0, which permits commercial use.

Fine-tuning one open detection model on this data lifted its score on an external evaluation set from 13.41% to 73.80%, more than a fivefold rise. Yet the same dataset card writes a line right beside that score: good results on synthetic data do not guarantee complete detection, fairness, privacy protection, legal compliance or leak prevention on real data. That sentence, put there first by the people who built the thing, is the most important passage in this dataset.

Sections 1 through 4 follow what the dataset card and public reporting state. Section 5, which takes up the gap left by Japanese forms being published while Korean forms are not, is this article's own reading.

1

NVIDIA's Tokyo Release Is 100,000 Fake Documents

Nemotron PII Japan is a dataset NVIDIA published at its developer event in Tokyo. Nemotron Personas Finance Japan, a set of synthetic personas for Japanese finance, came out the same day. Both were presented as a way to help companies working in Japanese tune models on their own data.

The sources disagree from the publication date onward. NVIDIA's announcement gives November 9 as the release date, while the dataset card on Hugging Face is stamped November 10. The CASRAI article points out the one-day difference. In an article that works through numbers a company reported about itself, the fact that the very first checkable item, the date, differs between two sources is worth writing down.

Inside are 100,000 synthetic Japanese business documents, bundled into a single parquet file of 75.5MB. The eight fields are healthcare, finance, public administration, employment, education, law, telecom and IT, and e-commerce, and within them the set imitates forty-three forms actually in use. Fixed-field forms and free-form documents are mixed together. There are 56 types of personal data, and the places in the documents where one of those types is pointed at come to 810,703, an average of 8.11 per document.

The data splits into 95,000 documents for training and 5,000 for internal validation. There is no published fixed split, though. A researcher who wants to measure the score again under the same conditions cannot take the exact exam paper NVIDIA used. The licence is CC BY 4.0, so with attribution the data can be used commercially.

Building a model that detects personal data has always required documents that contain personal data, and that has been the standing contradiction of this field. Collecting the training data is itself an act of collecting personal data. Synthetic documents step around the whole thing. Not one living person's information goes in, so there is nothing to leak when the data is published, and because there is nothing to leak the licence can be opened. The CC BY 4.0 licence comes from there.

2

The Labels Are Not Added Afterwards

Personal-data detection sets are usually built by having people read existing documents line by line and mark them. It is expensive and slow, and the standard wobbles from one reader to the next. Above all, those documents hold real personal data, so the workers end up seeing it.

This dataset reverses the order. It starts by drawing a person's attributes from Nemotron-Personas-Japan, a bundle of synthetic personas built on official Japanese statistics. Values with a fixed format, such as residence registration numbers or account numbers, are filled in by rule-based generators, and proper nouns such as place names and institution names come from lists prepared in advance. Financial institution and corporate names are drawn with reference to real lists, but no real name is sent out into the document. Only then does a language model weave those values into natural Japanese sentences, and the woven result is checked again and brought back into format.

The process has a name. NVIDIA states on the card that it stamped this dataset out with NeMo Data Designer, its own synthetic-data generation tool. The personas used as input were not the published Nemotron-Personas-Japan dropped in as is, either. The work used a one-million-record Japanese persona asset assembled separately inside Data Designer on the basis of that data. Which also means that swapping in other forms and other personas to stamp out another country's paperwork with the same tool is not blocked by anything in the process.

The last step is the point. The labels are not attached by a person reading a finished document. A program takes the positions already fixed when the values went in and writes them down as they are. The side that built the document knows from the start which character to which character holds which kind of personal data. The annotation cost all but disappears, and so does the standard that used to wobble from worker to worker.

Of the four steps, the labels are fixed at the second, and at no point does a person step in.

2.1There Is an Original From a Year Earlier

The method is not new this time. NVIDIA published the region-neutral original, Nemotron-PII, on October 28, 2025. It was described as 100,000 records spanning more than 50 industries with more than 55 types of personal data, and the same release opened GLiNER-PII, an open-weight model trained on that data.

The scores reported then were 92% recall and 64% F1. Which evaluation set produced those numbers was not written down in the public documents. This Japanese edition names external evaluation sets, Sumi test and Sumi OOD. Stating which exam paper was used is a step forward over the year. That reading is ours, though, and NVIDIA has never said it intended any such thing.

3

What 13% to 74% Actually Measures

The score that went from 13% to 74% was measured on one open model, GLiNER multi-v2.1. It is the kind of model that picks the relevant part out of a document when you simply say in words what to look for, with no fixed list of categories set in advance, and it handles several languages at once. Pointed at Japanese paperwork as it comes, it scores low. It has never learned what Japanese forms look like, nor which types of personal data Japan uses.

The measurement conditions are on the card. The decision threshold was fixed at 0.5, and the metric is a micro F1 over eight common categories that counts a hit only when the character span and the label match exactly. A span off by a single character counts as wrong, so the marking is not generous.

Sumi test and Sumi OOD, the sets where the scores were measured, are not NVIDIA's own. They are two splits taken as they are from sumi-ja-pii-corpus, a third-party Japanese personal-data corpus published on Hugging Face. The first is 2,000 documents built from business-document templates such as emails and application forms. The second is 520 documents that plant personal data in sentences from Wikipedia, statutes and literary works to see how far detection carries beyond the templates. Not having built the evaluation set themselves adds a little weight to the scorecard.

The personal data planted in both splits is invented too. A model taught on synthetic documents was marked with documents whose personal data a generator wrote. Not one number on this scorecard was measured on documents that contain a living person's information. Every cell in the table below has to be read on that premise.

Sumi test (2,000 docs) 13.41% 73.80% 85.17% 65.10%
Sumi OOD (520 docs) 8.13% 65.30% 61.63% 69.43%

The rise alone is large. From 13.41% to 73.80% is more than 60 percentage points, and even on the out-of-distribution set the score went from 8.13% to 65.30%. A model that knew nothing of Japanese forms learned them, so the effect itself is plain.

There are three things these numbers do not say. The first is recall. After tuning, precision on Sumi test is 85.17% while recall is 65.10%. Eighty-five out of every hundred picks are right, but one in three pieces of personal data actually sitting in the document still gets missed. For work like masking, where a single leak is a problem, recall matters more than precision, and the side that went up is precision.

The second is the distance between inside and outside. The same model scored 98.08% on the 5,000 internal validation documents, more than 24 percentage points above the 73.80% on the external set. The internal validation documents came out of the same generator as the training data, so their forms and their phrasings are already known quantities. The card itself states that the documents resemble one another closely and that it used this score only to choose a checkpoint. It is the place where the warning, easy inside the synthetic distribution and hard outside it, shows up as a number.

That the two scores were not marked on the same ruler has to be written down alongside. The internal 98.08% was measured with the 56 labels left as they are, while the external 73.80% folds predictions made over 56 types into eight common categories. Those eight are person name, phone number, address, email address, date, URL, account number and credit card number. The harder marking is the one over 56 types, and the higher score came from there, so the 24-point gap may be a narrow reading of the difference but it is not an inflated one. Something else surfaces instead. Among those eight categories there is no device or document ID, and no organisational information such as a bank. As the next section shows, those are exactly the items the card picks out as ones where labelling wavers. The 73.80% is a score received with the shakiest items left out of the marking.

The third is who did the measuring. The CASRAI article tags these scores as company-reported results. They have not been through independent third-party verification or outside expert evaluation. And with no published fixed train/test split, anyone trying to measure again under the same conditions has to build a split of their own.

4

The Limits the Card Writes Down Itself

The part of this dataset most worth looking at is not the scorecard but the limitations listed beneath it. Notices like these usually read as boilerplate meant to deflect responsibility. The ones written here are specific, and they work against their author.

It states first that the labelling is not exhaustive. Values caught as slots during generation get labels, but when the same kind of information flows into a sentence outside those slots, it may carry no mark at all. It also states that even within the annotated scope, labels may be missing, character spans may be drawn wrong, or types may be classified wrong.

Three items are singled out. Locations of institutions and facilities, IDs of devices and documents, and organisational information such as banks may or may not receive a label, depending on the annotation policy and the context. The original reads as follows.

機関・施設の所在地、機器・文書のID、銀行などの組織情報には、注釈方針や出現箇所の文脈に応じて、ラベルが付く場合と付かない場合があります。

Whether a label is attached to the location of an institution or facility, the ID of a device or document, or organisational information such as a bank depends on the annotation policy and on the context where it appears.

As it happens, all three are items that matter in practice. A hospital address reveals the fact of treatment, a document number becomes the key to identifying a person once matched against other records, and the bank someone deals with is the starting point of a financial profile. A model taught on this data should not be expected to find those three consistently.

Missing labels affect the marking as well, and the CASRAI article raised that point. When personal data sits in a document but carries no label, a model that picks it out correctly is marked as a false alarm, because the answer key does not have it. Reported precision may therefore be lower than the truth. In the other direction, where a label span was drawn wrong, a wrong detection may be counted as right. How far it tilts either way cannot be known from the published material.

The evaluation design has a hole too. All 100,000 documents carry personal-data labels. There is no control group for measuring whether a model passes a document holding no personal data cleanly, or mistakes ordinary characters for personal data. Most documents handled in real work are the kind that hold no personal data, and a model that over-masks them is less useful. This dataset on its own cannot measure that property.

The control group was not far away, though. Inside the very sumi-ja-pii-corpus where the scores were measured sits a separate split named negatives, 2,000 documents without a single character of personal data. With zero answer spans, anything the model picks out there is caught as a false positive, which is exactly what the split was made for. The published scorecard carries only the 2,000 test documents and the 520 OOD ones. Two splits were taken from the same corpus and the third was not. Nowhere on the card is a negative control or a false positive mentioned, so whether the result was measured and left out or never measured at all cannot be known from the published material.

And there is one sentence that covers every limitation.

合成データで良好な結果を得ても、実データ上の完全な検出、公平性、プライバシー保護、法令遵守、漏えい防止を保証するものではありません。

Obtaining good results on synthetic data does not guarantee complete detection, fairness, privacy protection, legal compliance or leak prevention on real data.

The card goes on to state that reviewing a sample does not estimate the overall error rate, and that separate verification against the laws, internal rules and risk tolerance of the target field is needed before anything goes into production. There are also warnings that the generated documents must not be used as real evidence such as identity proof, contracts, medical records, remittance instructions or employment records, and that they could be abused for phishing, impersonation or forged documents.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This

The worth of synthetic data does not come from volume. It comes from how precisely the aim was taken. Stamping out another 100,000 documents is easy, and confirming how closely those 100,000 resemble the distribution of real working documents is hard. Whether the aim was true cannot be measured with synthetic data. In the end it takes real data to find out.

This release showed that distance as a number. Inside the synthetic distribution the score is 98.08%, and one step outside it becomes 73.80%. On the evaluation set further from the distribution it falls to 65.30%. That scores drop as you move away from the training data is true of every model, but with synthetic data this distance means how far the distribution we aimed at sat from reality. That the card carried those numbers side by side, hiding none of them, deserves credit.

The 73.80% and 65.30% called outside also came from documents with invented personal data planted in them. The forms changed and the sentences changed, but the personal data itself is still what a generator made, and how a living person's information is shaped and where it scatters never entered any of those three numbers. Measuring whether synthetic data took aim correctly needs one's own real documents in the end, and those documents sit with the side using the dataset, not the side publishing it.

Turn to Korean and the shape of the gap is sharp. NVIDIA published Nemotron-Personas-Korea, a set of Korean synthetic personas, in Seoul in April 2026. Fictional Korean people exist, in other words. But there is still no training set that uses those personas to invent documents on Korean forms and attach personal-data labels. The material is laid out and nothing has been made from it.

The Korean gap is being filled by the private sector. KDPII, a Korean conversation-based personal-data de-identification dataset, was published in IEEE Access in 2024 under CC BY 4.0, and newmean-ai, which builds multilingual personal-data masking models that run in the browser, has posted a report on GitHub comparing models on that dataset. In that report the Korean-only KoELECTRA (14 million parameters) recorded an 8.8% miss rate on personal-data characters in KDPII, missing less than the four multilingual student models of the same release (9.5 to 11.5%). A multilingual model one release earlier did better at 7.6%, though, and the report explains that the newer release gave up Korean miss rate while fixing a tendency to over-mask ordinary sentences in short texts. Locale-specific assets do change the result, but what is gained and what is given up differs from one evaluation set to the next.

The question this case leaves is not when a Korean edition will arrive. Something else has to be answered first. What did the model that finds personal data in our own company's documents learn from, and what was its score measured on? Anyone who cannot tell whether a vendor's scorecard holds a number from inside synthetic data or from real documents will decide on the 98% rather than the 74% that Nemotron PII Japan placed side by side.

There are three things to check when bringing in a personal-data detection model trained on synthetic data.

  • • Which distribution produced the score. A validation score from a set that came out of the same generator as the training data may have nothing to do with real performance. For Nemotron PII Japan the difference was 24 percentage points
  • • Whether it was measured on documents without personal data too. Measure only on documents that all hold personal data and there is no way to gauge false alarms. Most working documents are the kind without personal data, and a model that over-masks them is hard to use. In this case the evaluation corpus already in use carried 2,000 documents with no personal data, and the scorecard still had no result from them
  • • Whether recall was reported separately. A single F1 number hides it. In this case precision was 85% while recall was 65%. For work where not one item may slip through, the number to look at is the second one

Editor's Note

What stayed with me while writing this was not the scorecard but the limitations listed beneath it. Which items have wavering labels, what could not be measured: the people who built the thing wrote it down first. Releases that lead with the score and say nothing about the limits until someone asks are common, and this document runs the other way. Writing the limits down and having the receiving side read them are two different matters, though. CC BY 4.0 means there is no procedure at all for downloading it, and the file opens just the same whether or not the card was read to the end. Honesty on the publishing side does not stand in for verification on the using side. The sibling dataset, Nemotron Personas Finance Japan, appears to show a different record count in public reporting than on its dataset card, so this article does not state its size.
