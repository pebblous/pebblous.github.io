Executive Summary

Where pseudonymized or anonymized data will not carry an AI project, a path opens to train on personal data in raw form. The document that spells out the terms of entry is now public. On November 23, 2026 Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission opened public comment on a draft amendment to the Enforcement Decree of the Personal Information Protection Act. These are the detailed standards for the AI exception that was promulgated on September 8, 2026 and takes effect on March 9, 2027.

The draft asks for four things. Appoint an officer for the personal data used under the exception and put an internal management plan into operation; block any use beyond the scope of the Commission's resolution; limit and control access rights; delete the data once the need to use it ends. On top of that, four situations — among them any use of sensitive or unique identifying information — now call for a risk factor assessment before the review itself. The question has moved from whether personal data may train AI to what a company must prove before it does.

The statute is settled; the decree is still a draft. Comments are open until January 4, 2027, and review by the Ministry of Government Legislation follows. Sections 1 through 5 set out the promulgated Act, the draft as pre-announced, and the facts carried in same-day reporting. Section 6, which asks where these record-keeping duties overlap with data quality governance, is this article's own reading.

1

The Act is settled, the details are still a draft

The parent statute behind this notice has already cleared the legislature. The amended Personal Information Protection Act passed the National Assembly plenary on August 20, 2026, went to the government on August 28, and was promulgated on September 8 as Act No. 21910. It takes effect six months later, on March 9, 2027. The amendment inserts a new Section 5 into Chapter 3 for "special provisions on the processing of personal information for the development of artificial intelligence technology," running from Article 28-12 to Article 28-15.

Here is the path the Act opens. Personal data that was lawfully collected may be used for AI development beyond the purpose for which it was gathered. Improving the performance of a model already built counts as development. What a controller cannot do is make that call alone. The Commission has to review and resolve the request, and every requirement written into the Act has to be met.

Two of those requirements were left blank. The Act did not say which safeguards a controller must have in place, nor which cases call for a risk factor assessment first; it handed both to the Presidential Decree. The draft pre-announced on November 23 is the document filling in those blanks. Comments are due by January 4, 2027, through three channels — the government's public legislation portal, the Commission's email address, and ordinary post. The Personal Information Protection Policy Division is handling it.

One distinction is worth making here. The statute is final; the decree is a draft. A legislative pre-announcement is a comment period, and review by the Ministry of Government Legislation and approval by the Cabinet still lie ahead. The Commission says it will take in comments, clear those steps, and finish the amendment in time for the Act's effective date. So the list of duties below is what the published draft contains, and the fine print may read differently when the final version is promulgated.

2

Three requirements and a review come before the exception

The Act sets three requirements, and all three have to hold. Leave one open and the request does not reach review at all.

  • The nature of the data. Given the character of the information being processed — video, audio, images, codes, text — and how it relates to the technology being built, development has to be impracticable on anonymized or pseudonymized data. The requirement is that the other routes are closed; wanting to use raw data does not establish it.
  • Safeguards. Processing has to take place in an environment with the technical, managerial and physical measures the Presidential Decree prescribes. Cloud environments carry additional measures. This is precisely the blank the current notice fills.
  • Purpose and risk. The development purpose has to include public benefit, the interests of data subjects or third parties, or social benefit, and the risk of unfairly infringing the interests of data subjects or third parties has to be markedly low.

Of the three, the first is where a data team's hands go. Saying that pseudonymization makes development impracticable is not enough on its own. The claim has to arrive with what was tried and where it broke. You have to be able to show which de-identification method was applied with which settings, how far performance fell when a model was trained on the result, and whether that drop is steep enough to abandon the work. This passage in a filing is hard to write from memory once development is over. It is the kind of sentence that needs the record of a comparison run behind it. The draft does not prescribe a format for it, and reading the requirement as a data task is this article's interpretation.

Meet the requirements and you request a review from the Commission, which may attach conditions to its resolution where they are needed to protect data subjects' rights and keep the processing safe. After a resolution, the purpose and the type of use go into the privacy policy, and the Commission publishes the applicant, the main contents of the request, and a summary of the risk factor assessment each time it resolves. Even past the review, what a company said it would do stays visible from outside.

What changes after a successful review is written into the Act as well. Within the scope of the resolution, ten articles cease to apply: the limit on use and provision beyond the original purpose (Article 18) and the limit on use by a recipient (Article 19), notification of the collection source (Article 20) and of use and provision records (Article 20-2), the restriction on processing sensitive information (Article 23) and unique identifying information (Article 24), the restriction on resident registration numbers (Article 24-2), the provisions on fixed and mobile visual data devices (Articles 25 and 25-2), and the cross-border transfer provision (Article 28-8). The last one applies only where processing is outsourced abroad.

What the carve-out attaches to is the resolution, not the company. A controller that cleared the review still faces the original provisions on any processing that steps outside the purpose, data types and methods written into that resolution. A single organization ends up holding both states at once — data covered by the exception and data processed outside it — which turns drawing the boundary into a record-keeping task rather than an engineering one.

One open question remains. Law firms publishing commentary point out that the body of the exception speaks only of use beyond the original purpose, while the carve-out list includes Articles 18 and 19, which restrict provision to others. Whether the exception extends to third-party provision is unsettled on the text alone. This draft does not answer it.

3

Four cases add an assessment ahead of the review

Not every request travels the same road. The Act directs that in cases prescribed by Presidential Decree — judged by whether sensitive or unique identifying information is processed, what effect the work has on data subjects' rights and interests, and how large the risk is — a risk factor assessment comes before the review. The draft names four.

  • Use of sensitive information or unique identifying information.
  • Work that reflects the characteristics of data subjects needing special protection, such as children.
  • Development of AI that continuously observes or tracks data subjects and evaluates or predicts their characteristics or behavior.
  • Use that is expected to feed judgments or decisions with a material effect on data subjects' rights and interests.

The way to read this list is not to memorize the clauses but to put them to your own project. Does the training data carry values about health, biometrics or beliefs? Are minors among the users, and do their characteristics reach the model? Do behavioral logs stitched together across time for the same person go into training? Will the model's output feed decisions that change people's lives — hiring, lending, insurance? One "yes" among the four, and another gate stands in front of the review.

The third item is especially broad. A recommendation model or an anomaly detector has no intent to identify anyone, yet stitching one person's behavior together across time can still reach "continuous observation and tracking." The fourth is much the same. The test is where the model gets used rather than who builds it, so if the intended use is not written down during development, there is nothing to judge it against later.

Nor does the result stay inside the organization. As noted above, when the Commission resolves it publishes the applicant and the main contents of the request along with a summary of the risk factor assessment. How far a company looked into which risks goes out in summary form. The assessment is a document written to clear a review, and also a document written for publication.

4

The four safeguards turn into records on the data side

Put the "enhanced safeguards" of the draft into a data practitioner's language and most of it is documents and logs. The press release lists four items, and each one calls for a different record.

4.1An officer and an internal management plan

Personal data covered by the exception needs its own designated officer and an internal management plan that is established and carried out. Adding a box to the org chart does not finish the job. The scope of that responsibility only becomes definite once there is a list of which datasets fall under the exception, and the plan needs evidence that it actually ran, not just that it was written. After approval, the Commission supervises implementation on a regular basis and may require the submission of materials within the necessary scope.

4.2Blocking use beyond the scope of the review

This asks a controller to prevent any use that exceeds the scope written into the resolution. Prevention needs the boundary written down first. Which columns of which tables are bound to which resolution, and which training job that data entered, have to be checkable against each other before a one-line duty becomes an operable rule. An organization that keeps no data lineage has no way to prove it stayed inside the scope.

4.3Limiting and controlling access rights

This asks a controller to narrow who can reach the data and to keep a record of who did. Permission lists and access logs are old controls in privacy work, but what differs here is the unit they attach to: not "all personal data the company holds" but "the dataset used under this resolution." Unless the permission scheme is broken down per dataset, controlling only the data under the exception is hard to do.

4.4Deletion once the need ends

This provision requires deletion at the point where the need to use the data ends. Harder than deletion itself is fixing that point. Retention runs differently depending on whether it is the day training finished, the day the model shipped, or the day retraining stopped being a possibility. The press release puts no number on the deadline, and this article did not get to the text of the draft. Whichever way the deadline lands, though, a company that has not recorded the date, the targets and the method of deletion cannot show it complied.

On top of all this, the Commission may impose further safeguards as conditions according to the risk level of the case. Clearing the whole list may not be the end of it. The Commission can ask for more on a given request.

Narrow these four items to a single dataset and you lose the scope. The Act requires processing in an environment with safeguards in place, and adds further measures for cloud environments. Training on raw personal data rarely ends on one server in the building. The originals sitting in object storage, the intermediate artifacts preprocessing produced, the caches and snapshots scattered across nodes are all copies of the same data. Narrowing access rights and counting what has to be deleted both start from knowing how far the copies spread. What the additional cloud measures say is a matter for the text of the draft; reading it as a problem of counting copies is this article's own.

Provisions governing life after approval came in as well. The Commission supervises implementation of the resolution on a regular basis and may require materials, and where a resolution was obtained by false or improper means, where the requirements or conditions go unmet, where processing has not begun within six months of the resolution without good cause, or where a material change in circumstances makes the purpose plainly unattainable, it restricts processing in full after a further review and resolution. Legal commentary notes that the provision reads "shall restrict," leaving the Commission little discretion.

The six-month clause turns into a scheduling problem. Secure the review early and hang the development plan behind it, and the resolution can lapse before processing ever starts. The filing date should be counted backwards from the day the data will actually be touched, not from the day the paperwork happens to be ready.

5

The expert committee and the fast track move the calendar

Who looks at a request is also settled in the draft. To bring expertise in AI and privacy into the process, the Commission establishes a new expert committee that reviews matters in advance of the plenary session. How many members it has, what qualifies them and how they are appointed do not appear in the press release. What this article can confirm stops at "a pre-review body exists."

The arrival of a pre-review body adds a slot to the calendar. A review happens before a matter reaches the plenary, and any request for supplementation at that stage adds a round trip. If the six months in the previous section is a clock running after the resolution, this one runs before it. How long it takes will only be visible once the process is operating, but a schedule that treats filing through resolution as a single step is worth counting again.

The standard for a simplified procedure is written down too. Where the material facts — the AI development purpose, the type and scope of personal data, the processing method and environment — differ in no significant way from a previous resolution, and the earlier criteria and conditions can be applied as they stand, the procedure is shortened. Where a new infringement risk has appeared or an existing risk has grown markedly, the simplification does not apply.

The signal this sends to practitioners is clear enough: the axes of comparison are already fixed. Purpose, the type and scope of personal data, the processing method and environment. Getting a second request through quickly means the first one's account of those three axes is still on hand, and that the difference between the two can be explained. For a company building similar models repeatedly, the first filing becomes the template in practice.

ZDNet Korea reported that the draft also provides a basis for delegating intake of review requests, support for the expert committee's examination, and technical support for supervision to a specialized institution. No other outlet confirmed it, so this line carries its source.

6

Why Pebblous is watching this

Little in this draft is technically new. Access control, deletion, internal management plans — privacy work has used all of them for years. What changed is the unit they bundle into. An officer, a plan, permissions and deletion are now required as one set for "the dataset used under this resolution" rather than for the company as a whole. The question is whether the life of a single dataset can be shown in documents from beginning to end.

That question points at the same place data quality standards have long pointed. The ISO/IEC 5259 series covers the life cycle of AI training data and asks organizations to record where data came from, what processing it went through, who is accountable for it, and how its quality was measured. Lay that over the decree's four bundles and provenance and lineage meet "blocking use beyond scope," roles and responsibilities meet "designating an officer," and retention and disposal policy meets "deletion."

Calling the two the same thing would overstate it. The records the decree wants are confined to the specific dataset going before a review, while the records a quality standard wants face an organization's whole data management system. The purposes differ as well. One set of records exists to prove to a regulator that the risk of infringement is low; the other exists for a team to satisfy itself that a model was trained properly. What overlaps is not the conclusion but the question. Where did this data come from, who is accountable for it, how far did we agree to use it, and when do we erase it?

Which is why now, before the decree is settled, is a good time to take stock. It is early to design a compliance program, but a draft is enough to find out whether your data inventory can answer those four questions. Pick one dataset that might go under the exception and write four lines about it. What is the source of this data and the basis on which it was collected? Can you separate, today, the columns that fall inside the exception from the ones that do not? Is there a log of who reached this data last quarter? Do you know what would be deleted, and where the copies live? Blanks in those four lines are not the kind of gap that gets filled in March 2027. Access logs and data lineage are not built retroactively.

The January 4, 2027 comment deadline is worth noting alongside that. If a condition looks unworkable in practice — the trigger for the deletion clock, say, or the unit that access control attaches to — this is the stretch in which that can be said.
