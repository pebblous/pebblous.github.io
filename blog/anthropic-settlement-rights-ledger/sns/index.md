## LinkedIn (KO)

앤트로픽 합의금 15억 달러를 나누는 실무가 시작됐다. 그러자 최소 17년 전에 저자에게 권리가 돌아간 책을 옛 출판사가 자기 것이라고 청구했다.

합의는 약 50만 종의 저작에 1건당 3,000달러를 매겼다. 절판되어 권리가 저자에게 돌아갔거나 자비출판한 책이면 저자가 전액을 받고, 출판 계약이 살아 있으면 저자와 출판사가 절반씩 나눈다.

갈림길은 합의서가 다운로드 날짜로 정한 2022년 8월 10일 하나다. 그날 이 책의 권리가 누구에게 있었는가.

그 답을 적어 둔 곳이 업계에 없었다. 하루 만에 권리 반환 도서에 청구가 붙은 출판사가 스물다섯 곳까지 늘었다. 한 저자는 이미 돌려받은 책 16권이 한꺼번에 청구됐다고 알려 왔다. 계약서에서 수취인 정보를 그대로 끌어온 탓에 권리자도 아닌 에이전시가 청구인 칸에 오른 사례도 있었다. 돈을 어디로 보내는지 적는 칸을 권리가 누구에게 있는지 묻는 질문에 갖다 댄 것이다.

업계를 감시해 온 라이터비웨어의 빅토리아 스트라우스도, 저자를 대변하는 작가조합의 메리 라젠버거도 이 일을 출판사의 잇속 챙기기로 보지 않는다. 둘 다 부실한 기록 관리를 원인으로 지목했다. 실제로 여러 출판사가 착오를 인정했고 합의 관리자가 비율을 수정하는 중이다.

음악 쪽은 같은 문제를 등록부로 먼저 풀었다. 2021년부터 기계적 라이선싱 위원회가 악곡 데이터베이스를 누구나 조회할 수 있게 열어 두었다. 권리자가 직접 자기 정보를 등록하고 관리하는 창구도 함께 운영한다. 출판 쪽에는 그런 것이 없다. ISBN은 판을 식별할 뿐, 그 판의 권리가 지금 누구에게 있는지는 말해 주지 않는다.

데이터에 값을 치르기로 결정하는 순간 시험대에 오르는 것은 모델이 아니다. 그 데이터의 출처와 권리와 이력을 적어 둔 장부다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Anthropic's $1.5 billion copyright settlement reached the stage of dividing the money, and authors opening their allocation notices found former publishers claiming books whose rights had returned to them years ago. One title had reverted at least 17 years earlier.

The arithmetic is not complicated. The settlement puts $3,000 on each of roughly 500,000 works. A book that was self-published, or whose rights reverted, pays the author in full; a book still under a live contract splits evenly with the publisher.

It all turns on one fact: who held the rights on August 10, 2022, the download date the settlement designates.

Nobody had written that fact down. Within a day, Victoria Strauss of Writer Beware had 25 publishers on a running list of claims against rights-reverted titles, and one author reported 16 reverted books claimed at once. Literary agencies surfaced in the rightsholder field as well, in at least one case because a publisher pulled the payee information straight from the agency clause in the contract. That clause records where the money goes, not who owns the work, and at the claim stage the two were read as the same thing.

Neither Strauss nor Mary Rasenberger, CEO of the Authors Guild, reads this as publishers grabbing at money. Both point to recordkeeping. Several publishers have acknowledged the error, and the administrator is revising those percentages.

Music worked the same problem out with a register. Since 2021 the Mechanical Licensing Collective has run a publicly searchable musical works database alongside a portal where rightsholders maintain their own data. Book publishing has no equivalent. An ISBN identifies an edition and says nothing about who holds the rights to that edition today.

The moment you decide to pay for data, the thing on trial is not the model. It is the ledger recording where that data came from, who holds the rights to it, and what has changed since.

## Facebook (EN)

Could you put your hands on a letter you received twenty years ago?

That is the task in front of a great many authors this month.

Three thousand dollars per book. If the rights came back to you, the whole amount is yours. If the contract is still alive, you split it with your publisher. The rules were settled long before the notices went out.

Only one thing was never settled: who held the rights to the book on August 10, 2022.

Authors opening those notices found names they did not expect. Former publishers were claiming titles that had reverted years earlier. One author counted sixteen of them.

I went in expecting a story about publishers reaching for money, and the people who have watched this longest say something else. Victoria Strauss, who has tracked the industry for years, wrote that she is "always reluctant to attribute to malice what can be plausibly explained by poor recordkeeping." The head of the Authors Guild said she does not see this as a grab by the publishers either.

So it is not a story about who behaved badly. It is a story about a line nobody wrote down.

What the Authors Guild recommends comes down to paper. A copy of the reversion letter. Royalty statements that quietly skip the book in question. A screenshot of who Amazon currently lists as the publisher. A notarized statement. None of it is a register. Each one reconstructs ownership out of circumstance.

Until now, who owned an out-of-print book was not a question with money attached. Nobody was paying for the answer, so nobody kept the record current. Then the answer became worth three thousand dollars, and half a million titles needed it looked up at once.

"When the day comes that you have to pay for the data you use, can you pull out of your own records who to pay and how much?"

The gap I run into most often in this work is history. The values and the formats are in good order, and nothing anywhere says where the data came from, what terms it arrived under, or when those terms changed.

Rights keep changing. There is rarely a field for the date they changed. Some records nobody misses until the moment they carry a price.
