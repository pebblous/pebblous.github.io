## LinkedIn (EN)

The same book split lawful use from infringement, depending on where it came from.

In Bartz v. Anthropic, a U.S. court held that training an AI on lawfully purchased books was fair use, while downloading and stockpiling pirated copies from shadow libraries was infringement. Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion over roughly seven million books it had stored without authorization, the largest copyright settlement on record.

What decided fair use was not how transformative the training was, but how the data had been acquired. The price of a single book swung between zero and about $3,000 depending on its acquisition path. It is the first time data provenance was written into a judgment as an actual dollar figure.

Because Anthropic settled rather than appealed, the fair-use finding remains the judgment of a single district court, not a precedent confirmed on appeal.

The question companies now put to an AI vendor is changing. Not whether the model trained on copyrighted data, but whether the vendor can prove how that data was obtained.

## Facebook (EN)

Picture the same book, twice.

One copy you paid for at a store. The other you pulled from a file someone had quietly posted somewhere online. To an AI, the sentences in the two copies are identical, down to the letter.

And yet last July, a U.S. court saw them as entirely different things. Training on the lawfully bought book was fair use. The pirated copies, downloaded and stockpiled, were infringement. Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion over the seven million books it had kept without permission. That figure was never the price of learning. It was the price of how the books had been obtained.

For a while we have been asking AI what it learned. This ruling quietly changes the question.

"Can you prove how you got hold of that data?"

The same text now stands in two very different places, depending on whether a record of its origin exists. The value of data comes from its lineage, and this time a court wrote that down as a number. This is the ground Pebblous has long been watching: not which data you used, but whether you can account for where and how you obtained it.

The books on the shelf look the same as yesterday. What changed is that no one can any longer avoid asking where they came from.
