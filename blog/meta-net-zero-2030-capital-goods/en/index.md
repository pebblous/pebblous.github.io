# Meta Rethinks Its Net-Zero Deadline, and Servers and Concrete Are Why

_Meta_

## Executive Summary

> [!callout]
> Meta is taking another look at the deadline on its 2030 net-zero promise. In the [2026 sustainability report it published on November 19](https://trellis.net/article/meta-2026-sustainability-report/), the company said net emissions rose 45 percent in a single year to 11.8 million tons of CO2e, and Rachel Peterson, the vice president who runs data centers, and Blair Swedeen, global head of net zero and sustainability, wrote in the foreword that Meta is "re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing and interim milestones — including the 2030 timeframe for our net-zero emissions goal."

> Where that increase came from is the heart of the report. Capital goods, meaning the servers, chips and network equipment Meta buys and the steel and concrete that go into its buildings, made up 73 percent of total emissions and grew 58 percent in a year. The emissions that shook the promise were released before a single server switched on, in the building and filling of the sites. Renewable energy purchases do not offset this share, so signing more power contracts will not bring it down.

> Sections 1 through 4 set out what the report and the coverage confirm. Section 5, which reads the episode as a question of what gets counted and how far, is this article's own interpretation.

## The Same Year Comes Out as Two Numbers

The report Meta released on November 19 covers its environmental performance for 2025. Net emissions came to 11.8 million tons of CO2e, 45 percent above the year before. The 2024 figure was 8.2 million tons, so 3.6 million tons were added in twelve months.

The same report carries another number. Counted on a location basis, 2025 emissions are 21.7 million tons. That is nearly double the net figure, yet both are 2025 emissions Meta disclosed and both are correct. The gap comes from how electricity is counted. The location-based number uses the average emissions intensity of the grids the data centers are plugged into, while the market-based number reflects the renewable energy certificates and power purchase agreements the company has bought. Meta has reported matching all of its electricity use with renewable purchases since 2020, and those contracts erase the power share from the market-based ledger.

The number Meta attached its timeline review to is the market-based 11.8 million tons. Even the smaller of the two was enough to break the promise.

> [!callout]
> The sentence in the foreword went out under two names. Rachel Peterson, who runs data centers, and Blair Swedeen, global head of net zero and sustainability, wrote: "When we set our net zero emissions goal, we anticipated steady growth, falling clean energy costs, and changes in the broader system that would enable progress at the pace we envisioned. The reality has been far more complex." They did not say when the review will finish.

## Where the Line It Promised Not to Cross Sat

What Meta committed to in 2020 was reaching net zero across its entire value chain by 2030. Two targets sit underneath it. One cuts direct emissions (Scope 1) and purchased electricity emissions (Scope 2) by 42 percent against 2021. The other holds Scope 3, which covers everything else, at or below its 2021 level. The deadline Meta itself records for both is the end of 2031.

The weight sits at the back. Scope 3 accounts for about 99 percent of Meta's emissions. The share from running facilities or burning electricity in offices is already small, and almost all of the total comes from the supply chain. There is a reason the front end is small. As Meta has reported each year, it cut Scope 1 and 2 emissions 94 percent against 2017 by 2020 and covered what remained with 100 percent renewable matching and carbon removal purchases, so operational emissions have been net zero since that year. That left one line, the pledge not to exceed the 2021 level, carrying the substance of the net-zero promise.

That baseline is 5,772,583 tons. Scope 3 emissions in 2025 came to 11,858,913 tons. That is 2.05 times the baseline, or in the report's own phrasing 105 percent above 2021. Six years remain before the deadline, and the line it promised not to cross is already behind it.
