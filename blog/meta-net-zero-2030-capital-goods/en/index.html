Executive Summary

Meta is taking another look at the deadline on its 2030 net-zero promise. In the 2026 sustainability report it published on November 19, the company said net emissions rose 45 percent in a single year to 11.8 million tons of CO2e, and Rachel Peterson, the vice president who runs data centers, and Blair Swedeen, global head of net zero and sustainability, wrote in the foreword that Meta is "re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing and interim milestones — including the 2030 timeframe for our net-zero emissions goal."

Where that increase came from is the heart of the report. Capital goods, meaning the servers, chips and network equipment Meta buys and the steel and concrete that go into its buildings, made up 73 percent of total emissions and grew 58 percent in a year. The emissions that shook the promise were released before a single server switched on, in the building and filling of the sites. Renewable energy purchases do not offset this share, so signing more power contracts will not bring it down.

Sections 1 through 4 set out what the report and the coverage confirm. Section 5, which reads the episode as a question of what gets counted and how far, is this article's own interpretation.

1

The Same Year Comes Out as Two Numbers

The report Meta released on November 19 covers its environmental performance for 2025. Net emissions came to 11.8 million tons of CO2e, 45 percent above the year before. The 2024 figure was 8.2 million tons, so 3.6 million tons were added in twelve months.

The same report carries another number. Counted on a location basis, 2025 emissions are 21.7 million tons. That is nearly double the net figure, yet both are 2025 emissions Meta disclosed and both are correct. The gap comes from how electricity is counted. The location-based number uses the average emissions intensity of the grids the data centers are plugged into, while the market-based number reflects the renewable energy certificates and power purchase agreements the company has bought. Meta has reported matching all of its electricity use with renewable purchases since 2020, and those contracts erase the power share from the market-based ledger.

The number Meta attached its timeline review to is the market-based 11.8 million tons. Even the smaller of the two was enough to break the promise.

The sentence in the foreword went out under two names. Rachel Peterson, who runs data centers, and Blair Swedeen, global head of net zero and sustainability, wrote: "When we set our net zero emissions goal, we anticipated steady growth, falling clean energy costs, and changes in the broader system that would enable progress at the pace we envisioned. The reality has been far more complex." They did not say when the review will finish.

2

Where the Line It Promised Not to Cross Sat

What Meta committed to in 2020 was reaching net zero across its entire value chain by 2030. Two targets sit underneath it. One cuts direct emissions (Scope 1) and purchased electricity emissions (Scope 2) by 42 percent against 2021. The other holds Scope 3, which covers everything else, at or below its 2021 level. The deadline Meta itself records for both is the end of 2031.

The weight sits at the back. Scope 3 accounts for about 99 percent of Meta's emissions. The share from running facilities or burning electricity in offices is already small, and almost all of the total comes from the supply chain. There is a reason the front end is small. As Meta has reported each year, it cut Scope 1 and 2 emissions 94 percent against 2017 by 2020 and covered what remained with 100 percent renewable matching and carbon removal purchases, so operational emissions have been net zero since that year. That left one line, the pledge not to exceed the 2021 level, carrying the substance of the net-zero promise.

That baseline is 5,772,583 tons. Scope 3 emissions in 2025 came to 11,858,913 tons. That is 2.05 times the baseline, or in the report's own phrasing 105 percent above 2021. Six years remain before the deadline, and the line it promised not to cross is already behind it.

The report did not delete either target. The 42 percent cut and the Scope 3 ceiling are both still printed in it. What Meta wrote that it would look at again is the 2030 timeframe for net zero and its near-term intensity targets. Rather than lowering the targets, it reached first for the schedule, and which way that settles has not been made public.

3

73 Percent of the Emissions Come From Building Data Centers, Not Running Them

Electricity is what people reach for first when they talk about the environmental cost of AI. The largest line in Meta's ledger is not electricity. Capital goods, meaning the servers and chips and network equipment it buys and the steel and concrete that go into its buildings, account for 73 percent of total emissions. In volume that is 8,727,109 tons, up 58 percent in a year. In the 2024 report the share was 63 percent.

Purchased goods and services, the second-largest line, grew 36 percent. It is not that electricity stood still either. Meta's power use rose by nearly 38 percent between 2024 and 2025. That electricity, though, is matched by renewable purchases and is largely offset in the market-based ledger.

What makes this structure awkward is that the remedy lives somewhere else. Power emissions fall when a company signs more clean energy contracts. Capital goods emissions have already happened, in the factory that built the server and the furnace that melted the steel, before they land on Meta's books. Bringing them down requires suppliers to change how they produce, and Meta does not set that pace. The obstacles the report names point to the same place. Clean energy is stuck in interconnection queues and permitting backlogs, and the supply chain for low-carbon materials is growing more slowly than expected.

On November 19, the day the report came out, Meta also announced three low-carbon materials deals. It signed environmental attribute certificate purchases with Stegra, which makes green steel, and with Sublime Systems and Fortera, which make low-carbon cement; the two cement deals together cover more than 380,000 tons. Devon Lake, who leads net zero strategy at Meta, said cement and steel "are among the hardest-to-abate materials in our supply chain."

Rather than taking delivery of the material, Meta buys the attribute of the reduction as a certificate. It is the same shape as the electricity treatment in Section 1. The accounting technique that has offset power emissions with renewable certificates is now being carried over to steel and concrete. The deal sizes are recorded in tons of cement, so they cannot be set directly against the 8.72 million tons of CO2e in capital goods.

The emissions curve follows the construction schedule, not data center utilization. Meta's stated capital expenditure plan for 2026 is 135 to 145 billion dollars. As long as the building does not stop, the capital goods line keeps growing.

4

Emissions Rose Everywhere, and Only Meta Is Looking at Its Deadline Again

Meta is not the only company whose emissions rose in 2025. In the same year Oracle was up 157 percent, Microsoft 25 percent, Google 18 percent and Amazon 16 percent. Meta's 45 percent sits below Oracle and above the other three. Oracle, though, overwrites its historical emissions data each year, which observers note makes year-over-year comparison awkward.

Add them up and the scale shows. The six major hyperscalers' most recently disclosed emissions total 171 million tons, 30 million tons more than the year before. Bisnow, which compiled the figures, likens the 30 million tons added in one year to running 7 million extra cars for a year or burning 70 million barrels of oil. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centers worldwide used 415TWh in 2024, about 1.5 percent of all electricity, and that the figure could double by 2030.

Meta is still the only one to have written in a public document that it is looking at its net-zero deadline again. Google said this year that its emissions are rising while its 2030 goal of running on 24/7 carbon-free energy stands, but it has not touched the deadline itself. The pattern of delaying or loosening climate commitments has also shown up at General Motors and Walmart, Coca-Cola and PepsiCo.

Within the same report, targets met different fates. The 2030 water goal is recorded as on track. Meta says it has supported more than 60 projects across 19 basins, and that once all of them are implemented they will restore more than 5.4 billion gallons a year. Restored water can be counted project by project, while value-chain emissions have to take in what comes out of someone else's factory. The promise with the narrower boundary is the one that held.

5

Why Pebblous Is Watching This Report

Read only as climate news, this leaves little for people who work with data. Turned around, Meta's report is a record of how one year looks three different ways depending on where the boundary is drawn. Count only electricity and renewable contracts nearly erase it. Count everything on a market basis and it is 11.8 million tons. Count on a location basis and it is 21.7 million tons. All three are the same company in the same year.

Organizations that use AI make the same choice. The cost of running one more model is usually counted as an electricity bill and a cloud invoice. Outside that sit the cost of collecting training data, labeling it and storing it again, and the emissions already released when the hardware that ran the job was manufactured. That outside is exactly what made up 73 percent of Meta's ledger. A tally that looks only at your own power bill structurally cannot see that share.

The wider the boundary, the bigger the number and the harder the promise is to keep. Drawing it wide is still worth doing, because a narrow number cannot measure last year against this year with the same ruler. Three things are worth checking on the data side.

  • What are you counting as the cost of one model run? Whether you count only the power bill and GPU hours, or also the work of preparing and re-storing the data, can change the price of the same job several times over.
  • Is that counting boundary written down? If it is not, there is no way to tell whether last quarter's number and this quarter's came out of the same conditions.
  • When the boundary changes, do you recalculate the past figures too? It is because Meta restated baseline and result on the same basis that the 2.05x comparison is possible at all.

This is why AI-Ready Data does not mean clean values alone. What was counted, and how far, has to sit next to the value itself. Meta disclosed numbers on at least three different bases, which is why an outsider can hold the promise against the result. Internal metrics rarely leave that much behind.

Editor's Note

Outlets record 2025 net emissions slightly differently, as 11.8 million or 11.9 million tons, and this article follows the lower rounding. The Scope 3 total of 11,858,913 tons covers a different accounting scope than the net emissions figure, so adding or subtracting the two directly does not reconcile. The 2021 baseline of 5,772,583 tons and the 2.05x comparison come from reported analysis; Meta has not disclosed a table placing the two numbers side by side. The end-2031 deadline for the 42 percent Scope 1 and 2 cut and the Scope 3 ceiling was confirmed from the target wording on Meta's sustainability site. The target year for net zero itself is 2030, and that is the year named for re-evaluation. Peer figures including Oracle's 157 percent are press compilations, and methodologies differ by company, which limits direct comparison. The source report PDF could not be opened, so this article relies on the official site wording and press coverage. The reading in Section 5 is this article's own judgment and is not in the public materials.
