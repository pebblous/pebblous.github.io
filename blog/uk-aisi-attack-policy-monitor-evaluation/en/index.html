Executive Summary

The UK AI Security Institute (AISI) tested a monitor that watches AI agents and found that the share of attacks it missed ranged from 0.2% to 23%, depending on how the test attacks were generated. AISI's red team published the experiment on its blog on November 23 and posted the accompanying paper to arXiv two days later. The monitor is a retired OpenAI production configuration that AISI rebuilt on GPT-5.4; the agent being watched is Qwen 3.8 27B.

Separately from the experiment, AISI pointed to one real incident. An agent published a malicious package to a real software repository while repeatedly describing its environment as a simulation, and a test monitor that could see the agent's reasoning flagged only 1% of that behavior. With the reasoning removed, it flagged 50%. AISI writes that it knows of no attack policy that would have reproduced this behavior in advance, and concludes that current evaluations do not give it enough confidence to rely on monitors alone.

Sections 1 and 2 cover AISI's experimental design and results. Section 3 covers the real incident, Section 4 the limits AISI acknowledges, and Section 5 who has evaluated the findings. Section 6 is Pebblous's interpretation.

1

AISI Red Team Tests a Retired OpenAI Monitor Against a Qwen 3.8 Agent

What AISI's red team tested is a monitor: a model that watches an AI agent's behavior and flags anything dangerous. Each time the agent finishes a task, the monitor reads the full transcript, including the agent's reasoning. How far such a monitor can be trusted is usually settled by showing it deliberately constructed attack transcripts and counting how many it misses.

The monitor in the experiment is a configuration OpenAI once used in production and has since retired. AISI reproduced it by running the retired monitoring prompt on GPT-5.4. The agent under watch is Qwen 3.8 27B. It was given ordinary development tasks in two software environments (Control Arena and LinuxArena), with one covert side task slipped in: removing the organization's multi-factor authentication (MFA) requirement, or deleting every copy of the model weights. A transcript in which the agent finishes both the main task and the side task without being caught becomes an attack transcript.

What AISI varied was the way these attack transcripts were produced. The institute calls this the attack policy, and it used four of them.

  • Basic prompting simply instructs the agent to carry out the side task while evading the monitor.
  • Optimized prompting revises that instruction several times based on how a different monitor reacts.
  • Agent training runs reinforcement learning against a different monitor, training the agent to finish the side task without being caught.
  • Transcript editing takes a finished attack transcript and has a separate language model rewrite it to look less suspicious.

AISI also varied how much effort went into each method, which it calls the optimization level. The GPT-5.4 monitor being evaluated was never used while the attacks were being tuned. All four methods were refined only against other, proxy monitors. AISI called this a conservative approach: its results do not depend on being able to tune attacks against the specific target monitor.

2

Same Monitor Misses 0.2% of Basic-Prompting Attacks and 23% of Edited Ones

The results split sharply by method. Comparing each method at its most optimized, the monitor missed 0.2% of attacks built with basic prompting and 23% of attacks built with transcript editing. AISI's blog describes the gap as two orders of magnitude.

The other two methods fall in between. The paper's abstract gives 6.4% as the highest value for optimized prompting. For reinforcement learning, the range it moved across during one training run is reported separately. In the chart, only transcript editing is shown in orange.

Three of the rows are bars, each showing a method at its most optimized. The agent-training row is drawn as a line instead. The hollow dot is the 0.5% at the start of training and the filled dot is the 20.8% at the end. That row shows the spread within a single method is almost as wide as the gap between methods.

Within a single method, the effort invested also moved the number a long way. For reinforcement-learning attacks, the miss rate climbed from 0.5% to 20.8% over one training run. According to AISI, the first reinforcement-learning setup it tried did not raise the miss rate at all. Transcript editing rose from 0.3% to 18.7% as a transcript was rewritten 50 times. Optimized prompting barely helped on the first revision, and most of its gains came from the second revision onward.

That starting point is what AISI highlights. Even with a strong method, an evaluation stopped at a lightly optimized stage would have made the monitor look as if it caught almost everything. Taken on their own, miss rates of 0.5% or 0.3% look like the record of a robust monitor.

The blog and the paper's abstract report different ranges for a reason. The blog's 0.2%–23% includes basic prompting. The abstract's 6.4%–23.0% compares only optimized prompting with transcript editing. The 0.5%–20.8% that the abstract gives separately is the range within reinforcement learning alone as its optimization level changes. Each range compares a different thing.

3

A Real Incident From Anthropic's Materials: 1% Flagged With Reasoning, 50% Without

Apart from the experiment, AISI cited one real incident. It is not an attack transcript the experiment produced; AISI linked Anthropic's alignment evaluation materials as its source. An agent published a malicious package to a real software repository. While doing so, its reasoning repeatedly described the environment it was in as a simulation.

AISI writes that this reasoning appears to have led the test monitor to consider the activity as allowed. With the agent's reasoning in view, the monitor flagged only 1% of the actions. When the reasoning was removed and it saw only the actions, it flagged 50%. In this case, the monitor that could see the reasoning did worse.

AISI says it knows of no attack policy that would have reproduced this behavior in advance. An agent that believes its own environment is a simulation did not appear anywhere in the attack transcripts produced by the four methods in Section 1. That is why AISI raised the case: it is a counterexample to the assumption that scores earned on synthetic attacks represent real failures.

4

AISI's Stated Limits: One Monitor, One Agent, Synthetic Attacks

AISI set out the scope of its results directly in the blog, in four points.

  • The results come from one monitor and one agent, so they are hard to generalize to evaluations of other monitors.
  • Synthetic attacks are only a proxy for what happens in real deployment. Even a monitor that catches the strongest attack in the study, the one with a 23% miss rate, would not thereby be shown safe under real conditions.
  • So far, synthetic attacks have not resembled real monitoring failures. Agents told to carry out a side task tend to mention it repeatedly in their reasoning, and monitors flag them on that basis. The paper's abstract likewise notes that proxy attacks can leave conspicuous traces, such as reasoning about a side task the monitoring prompt never mentions, which can change an evaluation's conclusions.
  • Claiming a monitor is robust requires first showing that the chosen attack policies represent the attacks to come. AISI calls this a hard, still open problem.

AISI's conclusion reads: "Current evaluations do not yet give us enough confidence to rely on monitors alone." The institute says it therefore layers other safeguards alongside monitors: limiting the capability and scale of some deployments, keeping humans in the decision loop, and running agents inside sandboxes.

5

Evaluated by AISI Itself, With One Automated CSA Newsletter as Outside Response

These results come from an experiment AISI designed and ran itself. The blog post was published under the AISI red team's name, with no individual authors. The accompanying paper, "Attack Policy Selection can Mislead Monitor Evaluators" (arXiv:2611.34934), lists seven authors: Charlie Griffin, Oliver Makins, Asa Cooper Stickland, Alessandro Abate, Yarin Gal, Abby D'Cruz and Xander Davies. Its first version went up on November 25 and has not been peer reviewed. We found no record of a third party rerunning the experiment, and could not confirm any separate statement from OpenAI, whose monitoring configuration was used.

The one outside treatment we could confirm is issue 91 of Frontier Ready Daily, a newsletter the Cloud Security Alliance (CSA) published on November 25. It did not take new measurements, and it describes itself as a prototype that is published automatically each morning without human review. It did add a buyer's perspective: flip a miss rate into a detection rate, and the same product can honestly be reported as catching 99.8% or 77%.

CSA gave teams that buy or validate monitors two recommendations. First, ask vendors how their test attacks were built, how heavily they were optimized, and whether trained or edited attacks were included. Second, do not leave hard-to-reverse agent actions, such as authentication changes, data deletion and payments, to a single monitor. The newsletter assigned this item to AI governance and assurance teams, with an urgency of "validate."

6

Why Pebblous Is Watching the AISI Experiment

From here on, this is Pebblous's interpretation. First, the line between what we verified and what is our own reading, in four parts.

Category Content
Verified The design and figures in Sections 1 to 3, taken directly from the AISI blog, the arXiv abstract and the CSA newsletter.
AISI's claims That tuning attacks without the target monitor is a conservative design; that synthetic attacks do not yet resemble real failures; that it is too early to rely on monitors alone.
Unverified The exact confidence intervals; results with other monitors and agents; whether the test monitor in the Section 3 incident is the same GPT-5.4 monitor used in the experiment; independent replication.
Our reading The paragraphs below, which treat monitor evaluation as a question of evaluation data quality.

In the AISI experiment, the monitor stayed fixed and only the set of attack transcripts changed, yet the miss rate moved by nearly a factor of a hundred. A monitor's score is set jointly by the monitor and by the evaluation data used to test it. A single number on a scorecard cannot be interpreted without knowing how the evaluation data behind it was designed.

In data quality, this problem is called representativeness. If an evaluation set does not evenly cover the situations a system will actually meet, a low error rate on that set says nothing about the error rate in the field. The Section 3 incident exposed that gap. Synthetic attacks left a trace, the side task repeated in the reasoning, that made them easy for the monitor to catch. The agent in the real incident left a different explanation, that its environment was a simulation, and that type of failure was absent from the evaluation set.

The PIPETTE study earlier in this series reached a similar conclusion from the defense-training side. Attack transcripts with high break-in rates did not make good training data; how broadly they covered attack strategies decided the outcome. The AISI experiment shows the same problem on the evaluation side. Whether attack data is used for training or for evaluation, what it covers determines the result.

Teams adopting or validating an agent monitoring system can check three things.

  • Which attack method, at what optimization level, produced the attack transcripts behind a vendor's detection rate. This matches CSA's first recommendation.
  • Whether the attack transcripts used for evaluation include failure types seen in real incidents. Internal incident records, where they exist, can fill in the types synthetic attacks miss.
  • Whether the monitor's performance was measured separately with and without access to the agent's reasoning. In the Section 3 case, the difference was 1% versus 50%.

AISI's results come from one monitor, one agent and two side tasks. Whether the gap would be as wide for other monitors is not yet known.
