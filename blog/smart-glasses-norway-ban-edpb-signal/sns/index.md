## LinkedIn (EN)

Norway has named the places where smart glasses would be banned, down to parks, beaches, museums, playgrounds, schools, and sports facilities with changing rooms. What it has not named is the device.

The temporary ban was announced on October 5 by Torgeir Micaelsen, Norway's Minister of Digitalisation and Public Governance. The reason he gave was not what the technology can do but that people are recorded without knowing it. The device definition was left open across three options: glasses with a camera and sound recording, glasses with a camera and AI, or any body-worn technology carrying at least one of the three. The third option would catch a camera worn around the neck. The first would let a product with AI and no recording through.

Eight days later the European Data Protection Board adopted a statement at its Brussels plenary and picked a different point. The device should make capture obvious through an indicator light or a sound, and the company that makes the glasses, together with the provider of any linked service, is a controller in its own right and cannot pass that duty to whoever is wearing them.

Reading the two as places versus indicator lights misses where they overlap. The Board's own list of recommendations includes geofencing, so that capture is blocked in sensitive places. It is the same boundary. Norway asks a person to hold it and the Board asks the device.

Neither is finished. The Norwegian bill has not been introduced and has to pass parliament. The statement runs eight pages and binds no one. Meta widened the safeguards on its second-generation Ray-Ban glasses in July 2026, so that the camera stops when the capture light is covered or broken; that light is white, and a footnote in the Board's statement notes that a white light can be mistaken for a reflection or for sunlight.

At Pebblous, DataClinic is where the quality of AI training data gets put into numbers. Conditions not recorded at the point of collection are not recovered later. An indicator is a signal to the person being filmed and a list of places is a signal to the device, and both are attempts to write the terms down at the moment the data is made.

For a law to name the places and a device to recognize them, that list would have to be published in a form a machine can read. Neither document has gone that far.

You are in a changing room at a pool, and the person beside you is wearing glasses.

Can you tell, standing there, whether they are ordinary glasses or glasses with a camera?

That changing room is on the list of places the Norwegian government named on October 5.

Glasses with a camera and sound recording, glasses with a camera and AI, or any body-worn technology carrying at least one of those. The government set out the three options and wrote that it would weigh them further.

Eight days later the European Data Protection Board took hold of the same problem from the other end. Never mind the place: let the device announce that it is capturing. That duty sits with the company that makes the glasses, the Board wrote, and cannot be handed to the person wearing them.

The two answers look far apart, and then they touch.

Nothing in the Norwegian announcement asks the glasses to know where they are and switch themselves off. Which leaves taking the glasses off at the park gate, and spotting whoever did not, to whatever eyes happen to be there.
