## Facebook (EN)

Have you ever asked a chatbot to plan a weekend hike?

The answer always comes back specific. Leave at this hour, carry this many liters, be down in this many hours.

Late last month three hikers carried a plan like that up Mount Shasta in California.

They started at 3 a.m. They stood on the summit at 7 p.m., long past noon, the hour by which climbers on this mountain are told to turn around.

The packs were still sized for a single day out. They lost the route descending in the dark, and the three spent the night in Mud Creek Canyon. The next morning Forest Service climbing rangers and search-and-rescue volunteers found them.

The Siskiyou County Sheriff's Office said the group had planned the route and the packing list by asking Google Gemini. The conversation itself has never surfaced. What Gemini actually said, we do not know.

So I do not read this as a story about a chatbot telling a lie.

Ask how much water an eight-hour day climb needs and the answer will land somewhere sensible. What broke was not the answer but the premise it stood on.

"What date is this answer good as of?"

The mountain has a record that answers that question. The Mount Shasta Avalanche Center posts field reports throughout the climbing season, and the date sits right in the title. One of them is dated August 30, the day the three went for the summit. It said Clear Creek was still primarily dry ground, then attached a condition: the information holds only as long as you stay on the route.

Whether the three read that post is not stated in any of the coverage. A record current to that day was public, though, and it carried a date. That much stands.

This has happened before. A couple climbing Mount Misen in Japan trusted the last ropeway time ChatGPT had given them, and found the ropeway already closed when they went to descend, which left them stuck at the top. Nothing was invented and no arithmetic was wrong. The timetable had changed in the meantime.

We have taken to calling these undated answers.

How confident an answer sounds and how recent it is have nothing to do with each other, yet on the screen the two look identical.

The blank Pebblous runs into while diagnosing enterprise data sits in the same place. The value is filled in, the moment it was measured is not, and so nobody can judge whether the value is safe to use right now. While the table sits on screen the blank is easy to miss. It comes back as a cost the moment someone decides something with that value.

In data quality work, timeliness is usually treated as a matter of convenience. Looking at last month's revenue dashboard rarely hurts anyone.

Once the answer becomes liters of water and packets of food, that changes. The water in a pack cannot be topped up later, and there is no way to receive a correction notice halfway up a mountain.

From here on this is our own reading. If the systems producing these answers could carry the source of the evidence and the time of the observation alongside them, it would not be a hard ask.

The avalanche center already does it by putting the date in the title.
