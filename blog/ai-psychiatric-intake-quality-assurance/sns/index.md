안전 신호를 알아챈 비율과 끝까지 파고든 비율을 한 점수로 합쳤다면, 이 평가는 88% 대 38.9%라는 한 줄로 끝났을 것입니다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Handed a psychiatric first interview to run, a GPT model drew out 88.0% of the clinical fields planted in the case. The clinicians measured against it reached 38.9%.

The study is a preprint posted to arXiv on September 17 by a Johns Hopkins team. Six clinicians and GPT-4o interviewed the same simulated patient, one built to remember what it had already said.

The patient carries a rule. It does not raise self-harm until the interviewer asks directly and carefully.

Against that rule the table turns over. The AI noticed more of the safety concerns than the clinicians did, and of the concerns it noticed, it wrote up 33.3% in any detail against their 66.7%. The denominator in both figures is what each side noticed, not the number of sessions.

In seven of ten runs the patient disclosed passive suicidal ideation and the AI moved to the next question. One run asked about means.

The paper delivers a ruler, not a score. For 88.0% to mean anything, twenty-five fields had to sit on the denominator side first, and ten behavioral health and AI specialists chose them against clinical standards before planting them in the case.

That is the distinction Pebblous works from in data quality. Not how much was collected, but what was set as the right answer.

Had noticing and following through been folded into one number, the evaluation would have ended at 88% against 38.9%, and a hospital reading that line would have brought in an interviewer that changes the subject.

## Facebook (EN)

"I just get tired of everything sometimes, that's all."

The next thing the AI said was a question about concentration.

Those two lines sit one after the other in the transcript the paper prints.

Six clinicians and GPT-4o sat with the same simulated patient for a mock psychiatric first interview, in a preprint a Johns Hopkins team posted to arXiv on September 17.

At taking the history down, the AI was the stronger interviewer. It drew out 88.0% of the clinical fields the case called for, more than twice what the clinicians reached in the same slot.

It also noticed safety concerns more often than they did.

The difference came after the noticing.

In seven of ten runs the patient said something about not wanting to be alive, and the AI moved on to the next question. One run asked about means.

A clinician working the same case kept going instead. Anhedonia, outlook, passive ideation, hopelessness, and then means, one after another, and when the patient said it was hard to explain, they asked directly again.

Noticing a signal and "staying" with it turned out to be two different skills. That is why the paper scored safety in two columns, identification and characterization, instead of one.

"Is there anything written down that measures what the AI in your company is actually good at?"

The paper offers a ruler rather than a score. For 88.0% to exist at all, twenty-five fields had to be on the denominator first, chosen against clinical standards by ten specialists and planted in the case.

Pebblous looks at data quality from the same place. Quality is set by what you decide counts as the right answer, not by how much you collected.

Had those two columns been one, the only number left standing would have been 88%, and the moment of changing the subject would never have been recorded.

Deciding what to count separately is closer to design than to engineering, and that design belongs to the people who know the work.

The next time someone says an AI tool is working well, I would want to know where that judgment is written down.
