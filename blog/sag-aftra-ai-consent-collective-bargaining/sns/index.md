할리우드 배우 노조가 AI 학습 데이터 동의를 소송이 아니라 단체교섭으로 받아 냈다.

SAG-AFTRA의 2026년 새 TV·영화 계약은 91.42% 찬성으로 비준됐는데, 핵심은 임금이 아니라 AI 조항이다. 제작사가 인간이 맡을 배역에 합성 배우를 쓰려면 먼저 노조에 통지하고 교섭 테이블에 앉아, 그 합성 배우가 '상당한 추가 가치'를 준다는 것을 입증해야 한다. 합의가 깨지면 노조는 중재로 손해배상을 청구한다.

학습 데이터를 둘러싼 동의가 법원 판결이나 약관 클릭이 아니라 협상 절차의 산출물이 된 첫 사례다.

그림자도 분명하다. '상당한 추가 가치'의 정의는 계약서에 비어 있어 판정이 사실상 스튜디오 몫으로 넘어가고, 미국 노동자의 약 88%는 애초에 그런 협상 테이블 밖에 있다.

동의를 받았는가만큼 중요한 질문은, 동의를 거부할 힘이 있는가다.

## LinkedIn (EN)

Hollywood's actors won AI training-data consent at the bargaining table, not in court.

SAG-AFTRA members ratified their new 2026 film and TV contract by 91.42%, and its center of gravity is not pay but AI. Before a studio can put a synthetic performer in a role a human would play, it must notify the union, sit down to bargain, and prove the synthetic actor brings 'significant additional value.' If talks collapse, the union can seek damages in arbitration.

It is the first case where consent over training data was produced by a negotiation, rather than settled by a lawsuit or buried in terms of service.

The gaps are just as clear. The contract never defines 'significant additional value,' so the call effectively falls to studio lawyers, and roughly 88% of U.S. workers sit outside any such bargaining table to begin with.

The question is no longer only whether consent was obtained, but whether there is power to refuse it.

## Facebook (EN)

There was a moment last year when an actor who does not exist introduced herself, like any newcomer, on social media.

Her name was Tilly Norwood, an AI-generated performer. What unsettled people was not the finished face but what went into making it: the work of countless professional actors, learned without permission.

SAG-AFTRA did not take this to court. It brought it to the bargaining table instead.

The new contract, ratified this year, requires a studio to notify and bargain with the union before putting a synthetic performer in a role a human would play. Consent over training data, in other words, became something produced through negotiation rather than handed down in a ruling or buried in terms of service.

And yet I found it hard to read this as a clean victory. The phrase the whole exception rests on, 'significant additional value,' is never defined, and nine in ten American workers have no such table to sit at in the first place.

So a question lingers: as much as 'was consent obtained?', shouldn't we be asking 'is there any power to refuse?'

That is close to why Pebblous has spent so long looking at where training data comes from and how it was consented to. The same data carries a different weight depending on whether the people who hold it have any ground to bargain from.
